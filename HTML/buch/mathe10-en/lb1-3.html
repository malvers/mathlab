Areas of triangles and polygons

A plot of land with slanting borders, a triangular flower bed, a pavilion with six corners: to find their area you do not need to measure a height. Two sides and the angle between them are enough. You learn the area formula with the sine and split polygons into triangles.

UNIT 12 LESSONS

1The area with the sine

In the folding triangle the height onto the 30 cm ruler is exactly $20 \cdot \sin \gamma$: the 20 cm ruler is the hypotenuse of a right-angled part triangle. So the area is $\tfrac12 \cdot 30 \cdot 20 \cdot \sin \gamma$. It is largest at $\gamma = 90^\circ$; then $\sin \gamma = 1$ and the area is $300\,\text{cm}^2$.

KEY POINT · AREA WITH THE SINE

Triangle: $A = \dfrac12 \cdot a \cdot b \cdot \sin \gamma$ – two sides and the angle between them.

Parallelogram with the sides $a$, $b$ and the angle $\alpha$: $A = a \cdot b \cdot \sin \alpha$.

If only the three sides are known: first find an angle with the cosine rule, then use the formula.

Obtuse angles: the sine is also positive between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$. The formula holds unchanged, $\sin 120^\circ = \sin 60^\circ$.

INTERACTIVE · AREA OF A TRIANGLE

Change the two sides and the angle between them. Compare the area with $\tfrac12 \cdot g \cdot h$.

EXAMPLE 1 · THE FLOWER BED

A triangular flower bed has sides of 4 m and 6 m that enclose an angle of $120^\circ$. How large is it?

$A = \tfrac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot \sin 120^\circ = 12 \cdot 0.866 \approx$ 10.4 m²

EXAMPLE 2 · ONLY THREE SIDES

Calculate the area of the triangle with the sides 5 cm, 6 cm and 7 cm.

Angle between 5 and 6: $\cos \gamma = 0.2$, $\gamma \approx 78.46^\circ$. $A = \tfrac12 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 \cdot \sin \gamma \approx$ 14.7 cm².

A parallelogram has the sides 10 cm and 6 cm and an angle of $50^\circ$. What is its area?

$60 \cdot \sin 50^\circ \approx 46.0\,\text{cm}^2$

A triangle has the area $24\,\text{cm}^2$ and two sides of 8 cm and 12 cm. How large is the angle between them?

$24 = 48 \cdot \sin \gamma$, $\sin \gamma = 0.5$: $\gamma = 30^\circ$ or $150^\circ$.

A triangular meadow has sides of 80 m and 60 m with $65^\circ$ between them. You need 25 g of seed per m². How much seed is needed?

$A = 2400 \cdot \sin 65^\circ \approx 2175\,\text{m}^2$, seed $\approx 54.4$ kg.

Explain why, of all triangles with two sides of 20 cm and 30 cm, the right-angled one has the largest area.

$A = 300 \cdot \sin \gamma$ and $\sin \gamma \le 1$, with equality exactly at $\gamma = 90^\circ$.

2Splitting polygons

From its centre the floor of the pavilion falls into six triangles. Their angle at the centre is $360^\circ : 6 = 60^\circ$, and their sides to the centre are equally long. So they are equilateral with side 2 m: $6 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot \sin 60^\circ \approx 10.4\,\text{m}^2$.

KEY POINT · POLYGONS

Regular $n$-gon with circumradius $r$: $n$ identical triangles with the central angle $\dfrac{360^\circ}{n}$, so $A = n \cdot \dfrac12 \cdot r^2 \cdot \sin \dfrac{360^\circ}{n}$.

Any polygon: split it into triangles with diagonals, calculate each part area and add them.

Check: a regular polygon always has a little less area than its circumcircle. The more corners, the closer it gets.

INTERACTIVE · REGULAR POLYGONS

Increase the number of corners and watch how the polygon gets closer to the circle.

EXAMPLE 3 · THE OCTAGON

A regular octagon has the circumradius 5 cm. Calculate its area.

$A = 8 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 25 \cdot \sin 45^\circ = 100 \cdot 0.707 \approx$ 70.7 cm². The circle would have $78.5\,\text{cm}^2$.

EXAMPLE 4 · A PLOT OF LAND

A quadrilateral plot of land is divided by the 50 m long diagonal $AC$. In one part $AB = 30$ m with $40^\circ$ at $A$, in the other $AD = 40$ m with $35^\circ$ at $A$.

$\tfrac12 \cdot 30 \cdot 50 \cdot \sin 40^\circ \approx 482.1$ and $\tfrac12 \cdot 40 \cdot 50 \cdot \sin 35^\circ \approx 573.6$: together 1055.7 m².

EXERCISES

A quadrilateral has the diagonal 10 m. The two other corners are 3 m and 5 m away from it. How large is it?

$\tfrac12 \cdot 10 \cdot 3 + \tfrac12 \cdot 10 \cdot 5 = 40\,\text{m}^2$

Calculate the area of a regular hexagon with the side 4 cm.

Six equilateral triangles: $6 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 16 \cdot \sin 60^\circ \approx 41.6\,\text{cm}^2$.

A regular pentagon has the circumradius 10 cm. Calculate its area and compare it with the circle.

$5 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 100 \cdot \sin 72^\circ \approx 237.8\,\text{cm}^2$, the circle has $314.2\,\text{cm}^2$.

A triangular plot of land has sides of 40 m and 55 m with $68^\circ$ between them. One square metre costs €120. What does the plot cost?

$A = 1100 \cdot \sin 68^\circ \approx 1019.9\,\text{m}^2$, price $\approx 122{,}400$ €.

How many triangles do you get if you split an $n$-gon from one corner? How many from the centre? Why do you prefer the centre for regular polygons?

From one corner: $n - 2$, from the centre: $n$. From the centre all the triangles are identical, so you only calculate one.

Calculate the area of the triangle with $a = 7$ cm, $b = 9$ cm and $\gamma = 100^\circ$.

$31.5 \cdot \sin 100^\circ \approx 31.0\,\text{cm}^2$

A triangle has $c = 10$ cm, $\alpha = 40^\circ$, $\beta = 60^\circ$. Calculate $b$ with the sine rule and then the area.

$\gamma = 80^\circ$, $b \approx 8.79$ cm, $A = \tfrac12 \cdot 10 \cdot 8.79 \cdot \sin 40^\circ \approx 28.3\,\text{cm}^2$.

A kite has sides of 30 cm and 50 cm. At each of the two side corners the angle between them is $110^\circ$. What is its area?

The axis of symmetry divides the kite into two identical triangles with the sides 30 cm and 50 cm.

$2 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 30 \cdot 50 \cdot \sin 110^\circ \approx 1409.5\,\text{cm}^2$

Connecting with learning area 4: a pavilion floor (regular hexagon, side 2 m) is covered with floorboards. One square metre costs €45, plus 10 % for offcuts. What does the wood cost?

$A \approx 10.39\,\text{m}^2$, with offcuts $11.43\,\text{m}^2$, cost $\approx 514$ €.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Heron’s formula

For triangles of which you only know the three sides there is an astonishing shortcut. With half the perimeter $s = \tfrac{a + b + c}{2}$, $A = \sqrt{s\,(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}$. Without any angle at all.

It has come down to us in the “Metrica” of the engineer Heron of Alexandria from the 1st century. For the triangle with 5, 6 and 7 cm, $s = 9$ and $A = \sqrt{9 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{216} \approx 14.7\,\text{cm}^2$ – exactly as in example 2.

Source: Heron of Alexandria: Metrica, Book I, section 8; after T. L. Heath: A History of Greek Mathematics, Vol. 2, Oxford 1921.
