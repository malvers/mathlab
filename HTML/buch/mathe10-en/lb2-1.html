Power functions

If you double the edge of a cube, its volume becomes eight times as large. If you drive twice as fast, the braking distance becomes four times as long. Behind such relationships are power functions $y = a \cdot x^n$. You get to know their graphs, the parabolas and hyperbolas, and their properties: symmetry, monotonicity and asymptotes.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Power functions with a positive exponent

When building the cubes, the number of small cubes grows with $x^3$: 8, 27, 64. The surface grows with $6x^2$: 24, 54, 96. Both relationships are power functions, one with exponent 3, one with exponent 2.

KEY POINT · POWER FUNCTIONS $y = a \cdot x^n$

$n$ even (e.g. $x^2$): graph symmetric about the y-axis, for $a > 0$ only values $y \geq 0$, decreasing for $x < 0$, increasing for $x > 0$.

$n$ odd (e.g. $x^3$): graph point-symmetric about the origin, for $a > 0$ increasing everywhere, all values occur.

All graphs $y = x^n$ pass through $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$. The factor $a$ stretches ($|a| > 1$), compresses ($|a| < 1$) or reflects in the x-axis ($a < 0$).

Order of operations: $2x^2$ means $2 \cdot (x^2)$, not $(2x)^2$. Powers first, then multiply.

Braking distance: rule of thumb $s = \left(\tfrac{v}{10}\right)^2$ in metres, $v$ in km/h.

INTERACTIVE · POWER FUNCTIONS

Choose the exponent and change the factor. Look at the symmetry, the common points and the behaviour for large $x$.

EXAMPLE 1 · FINDING THE FACTOR

The graph of $y = a \cdot x^2$ passes through $(3, 18)$. Find $a$ and calculate $y$ for $x = -2$.

$18 = 9a$, so $a = 2$. $y(-2) = 2 \cdot 4 =$ 8.

EXAMPLE 2 · COMMON POINTS

Where do the graphs of $y = x^2$ and $y = x^3$ intersect?

$x^2 = x^3$, so $x^2 (x - 1) = 0$: $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$. At $x = -1$, $x^2 = 1$ but $x^3 = -1$.

The graph of $y = a \cdot x^3$ passes through $(2, -4)$. Find $a$. Does $(-1, 0.5)$ also lie on it?

$-4 = 8a$, $a = -0.5$. $y(-1) = -0.5 \cdot (-1) = 0.5$: yes.

By the rule of thumb $s = \left(\tfrac{v}{10}\right)^2$: how long is the braking distance at 50, 80 and 100 km/h? What happens at twice the speed?

25 m, 64 m, 100 m. Twice the speed, four times the braking distance.

A cube is to have eight times the volume of one with a 5 cm edge. How long will its edge be, and how many times as large its surface?

$k^3 = 8$, $k = 2$: edge 10 cm. Surface $k^2 = 4$ times as large ($600$ instead of $150\,\text{cm}^2$).

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Power functions with a negative exponent

For the rectangles with $24\,\text{cm}^2$, $b = \dfrac{24}{a} = 24 \cdot a^{-1}$. Half the length, twice the width: length and width are inversely proportional. The points lie on a branch of a hyperbola that gets closer and closer to the axes but never reaches them.

KEY POINT · HYPERBOLAS

$x^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{x^n}$. The graphs of $y = \dfrac{1}{x}$ and $y = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$ are called hyperbolas. They are not defined for $x = 0$.

$y = \dfrac1x$: point-symmetric about the origin, branches in the 1st and 3rd quadrants. $\;y = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$: symmetric about the y-axis, only positive values.

Both get as close as you like to the coordinate axes: the axes are asymptotes.

Inversely proportional: the product stays the same. Time times speed is the distance, length times width the area.

Decreasing with the square: illuminance and sound intensity decrease with $\tfrac{1}{d^2}$.

PICTURE · TWO HYPERBOLAS

$y = \tfrac1x$ (yellow) changes its sign at $x = 0$, $y = \tfrac{1}{x^2}$ (blue) stays positive on both sides.

EXAMPLE 3 · JOURNEY TIME

For 120 km at the speed $v$ you need the time $t = \dfrac{120}{v}$. Calculate $t$ for 40, 60 and 80 km/h.

3 h, 2 h, 1.5 h. Twice the speed, half the time.

EXAMPLE 4 · THE LAMP

At a distance of 2 m a lamp gives an illuminance of 900 lux. The illuminance is $E = \dfrac{k}{d^2}$. How bright is it at a distance of 6 m?

Three times the distance, $\tfrac19$ of the brightness: 100 lux. Or $k = 900 \cdot 4 = 3600$, $E = \tfrac{3600}{36}$.

For $f(x) = \dfrac1x$ and $g(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$ calculate the values at $-2$, $0.5$ and $4$.

The graph of $y = \dfrac{a}{x}$ passes through $(2, 3)$. Find $a$ and the y-value at $x = 6$.

Four people paint a fence in 6 days. How long do 3 people take, how long 8? Set up an equation $t = \tfrac{k}{n}$.

The sound intensity of a sound source decreases with the square of the distance. To what fraction does it fall at twice, three times and ten times the distance?

$\tfrac14$, $\tfrac19$, $\tfrac{1}{100}$

For which $x$ is $\dfrac{1}{x} > \dfrac{1}{x^2}$? Give reasons with the graphs and with a calculation.

Both graphs pass through $(1, 1)$. For negative $x$, $\tfrac1x$ is negative.

For $x > 1$. There $x^2 > x > 0$, so $\tfrac{1}{x^2} < \tfrac1x$. For $0 < x < 1$ it is the other way round, for $x < 0$, $\tfrac1x$ is negative.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

Match them up: point symmetry or symmetry about the y-axis? $y = x^2$, $y = x^3$, $y = \tfrac1x$, $y = \tfrac{1}{x^2}$.

Symmetric about the y-axis: $x^2$ and $\tfrac{1}{x^2}$. Point-symmetric: $x^3$ and $\tfrac1x$.

The volume of a sphere is $V = \tfrac43 \pi r^3$. Calculate it for $r = 3$ cm. How does it change if the radius is doubled?

$36\pi \approx 113.1\,\text{cm}^3$. Twice the radius: eight times the volume.

Which points lie on $y = x^3$, which on $y = \tfrac{1}{x^2}$? $(-3, -27)$, $(0.5, 4)$, $(2, 8)$, $(-1, 1)$

$x^3$: $(-3, -27)$, $(2, 8)$. $\;\tfrac{1}{x^2}$: $(0.5, 4)$, $(-1, 1)$.

Connecting: a cube has the volume $V = a^3$. Give the edge length as a function of the volume. How long is the edge for $1\,\text{l} = 1000\,\text{cm}^3$?

$a = \sqrt[3]{V}$. For $1000\,\text{cm}^3$: 10 cm.

EXCURSIONSCIENCE

Why there are no giants

If a person were ten times as large in every measurement, they would have $10^3 = 1000$ times as much mass. But their bones carry the load with their cross-section, and that only grows with $10^2 = 100$. Every square centimetre of bone would have to carry ten times as much – the giant would collapse.

Galileo Galilei wrote down this idea in 1638 in his “Discorsi”. It explains why large animals such as elephants have much thicker legs than small ones, and why ants can carry many times their own weight.

Source: G. Galilei: Discorsi e dimostrazioni matematiche intorno a due nuove scienze. Leiden 1638, Second Day.
