UNIT 15 LESSONS

1Tree diagrams and path rules

Lea spins a spinner with three equal sectors twice: two are red, one is blue. She wins if red comes up both times. Does she win in more than half of all games?

A random experiment that consists of several sub-experiments carried out one after the other is called a multi-stage random experiment. It is represented in a tree diagram: every stage is a branching, and on every branch stands the probability of that outcome.

Every outcome of the whole experiment corresponds to exactly one path from the start to an end. An event combines several outcomes, i.e. several paths.

For Lea, $P(\text{red}) = \tfrac23$ at every stage. In $\tfrac23$ of all games red comes first, and of these games red comes again in $\tfrac23$ of the cases. So $$P(\text{RR}) = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac49 \approx 0.44.$$ So Lea wins in less than half of the games.

KEY POINT · PATH RULES

At every branching the probabilities add up to $1$.

1st path rule (product rule): the probability of an outcome is the product of the probabilities along its path.

2nd path rule (sum rule): the probability of an event is the sum of the probabilities of all paths that belong to the event.

When drawing from an urn it matters whether the ball drawn is put back. With replacement the probabilities stay the same at every stage. Without replacement the contents of the urn change, and with them the probabilities of the next stage.

Notation: R stands for red, B for blue. RB means: first red, then blue.

Check: the probabilities of all the ends of the paths add up to $1$.

Lab: in the dice game you build the tree for two dice yourself, stage by stage.

INTERACTIVE · TREE DIAGRAM WORKSHOP

EXAMPLE 1 · AT LEAST ONCE

Lea spins her spinner twice again. How likely is red at least once?

“At least once red” consists of the three paths RR, RB and BR. It is quicker via the complementary event “never red”, which is only the path BB:

$$P(\text{BB}) = \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac13 = \tfrac19, \qquad P(\text{at least once red}) = 1 - \tfrac19 = \tfrac89 \approx 0.89.$$

EXAMPLE 2 · WITHOUT REPLACEMENT

Two balls are drawn without replacement from the bag with three red and two blue balls. Calculate the probability of a) two red balls, b) two different colours.

After a red ball there are still 2 red and 2 blue in the bag, after a blue one 3 red and 1 blue.

a) $P(\text{RR}) = \tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac24 = \tfrac{6}{20} = \tfrac{3}{10}$

b) $P(\text{RB}) + P(\text{BR}) = \tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac24 + \tfrac25 \cdot \tfrac34 = \tfrac{6}{20} + \tfrac{6}{20} = \tfrac35$

So two different colours are more likely ($\tfrac35$) than two of the same ($\tfrac25$). That is the answer to the exploration.

Tip: “at least once” is almost always calculated via the complementary event “never”: $P(E) = 1 - P(\overline{E})$.

2Two-way tables

At the vocational grammar school 200 students were asked: 80 do sport in a club, 70 cycle to school, 35 do both. Do club athletes cycle more often than the others?

If two characteristics with two values each are recorded for every person, the counts are arranged in a two-way table (2 × 2 contingency table). The four inner cells contain the combinations, the totals are in the margins. Here $S$ is “does club sport” and $F$ “cycles”:

$F$$\overline{F}$Total
$S$354580
$\overline{S}$3585120
Total70130200

If you divide all entries by 200, you get probabilities: a randomly chosen person does club sport and cycles with probability $\tfrac{35}{200} = 0.175$.

On the question: of the 80 athletes, 35 cycle, that is $\tfrac{35}{80} \approx 44\%$. Of the 120 others it is only $\tfrac{35}{120} \approx 29\%$. So club athletes cycle considerably more often.

KEY POINT · TWO-WAY TABLE
$B$$\overline{B}$Total
$A$$P(A \cap B)$$P(A \cap \overline{B})$$P(A)$
$\overline{A}$$P(\overline{A} \cap B)$$P(\overline{A} \cap \overline{B})$$P(\overline{A})$
Total$P(B)$$P(\overline{B})$$1$

Every row and every column adds up to its marginal value. The four inner cells add up to $1$ (with counts, to the total number).

$A \cap B$ means “$A$ and $B$”. $\overline{A}$ is the complementary event “not $A$”.

Every two-way table belongs to two tree diagrams, depending on which characteristic you look at first. The probabilities at the ends of the paths are exactly the four inner cells of the table.

Check: add the marginal values once along the rows and once along the columns. At the bottom right you must get the same both times.

How to say it: $A \cap B$ is read “$A$ intersection $B$” or simply “$A$ and $B$”.

Careful: $P(S \cap F) = 0.175$ is not the proportion of cyclists among the athletes. That is $\tfrac{35}{80}$.

INTERACTIVE · THE RAPID TEST

OUTLOOK · CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY

With the rapid test you do not ask for “ill and positive”, but for “ill, given positive”. You write $P_{T^+}(D)$ ($D$ for disease) and speak of a conditional probability. It can be read off the two-way table:

$$P_{T^+}(D) = \frac{P(D \cap T^+)}{P(T^+)}$$

If $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$, $A$ and $B$ are called stochastically independent, otherwise dependent. In the bike shop $0.3 \cdot 0.6 = 0.18 \neq 0.12$: buying an e-bike and paying by card are dependent. You will work with both ideas in more detail later.

3Simulating random experiments

At the school fête two dice are rolled. Beforehand you bet on a total score. Is it worth betting on 7 instead of 6 or 8? You have no time to roll 1,000 times.

If a random experiment is carried out $n$ times and an event $E$ occurs $k$ times, $$h_n(E) = \frac{k}{n}$$ is called the relative frequency of $E$.

With few trials $h_n$ varies a lot. The more trials you make, the less it varies: it settles down near the probability $P(E)$. This is called the empirical law of large numbers.

Instead of really rolling 1,000 times, you let the computer generate random numbers and replay the experiment. That is a simulation.

KEY POINT · SIMULATING IN THREE STEPS

1. Model: assign random numbers to the outcomes of the experiment, e.g. score ↔ random number from 1 to 6.

2. Carry out: repeat the experiment very often on the computer.

3. Evaluate: find the relative frequency. It is an estimate of $P(E)$. Every new simulation gives a slightly different estimate.

School fête: 6 of the 36 possibilities give 7, only 5 each give 6 and 8. Try it in the simulator under “Two dice”.

History: Antoine Gombaud, Chevalier de Méré, knew from experience that the bet on “at least one six in four rolls” wins in the long run. His questions about games of chance set off the correspondence between Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat in 1654. It is regarded as the beginning of probability theory.

INTERACTIVE · SIMULATOR

Choose a random experiment and let it run 1, 10, 100 or 1,000 times. The blue curve is the relative frequency, the yellow line the probability.

METHOD · SIMULATING WITH A SPREADSHEET AND GEOGEBRA

Spreadsheet (Excel, LibreOffice): two dice, total score 7, 1,000 trials.

A1: =RANDBETWEEN(1,6)+RANDBETWEEN(1,6) Drag the formula down to A1000. Count the hits and divide by the number of trials: B1: =COUNTIF(A1:A1000,7)/1000 F9 (Excel) rolls again.

GeoGebra: L = Sequence(RandomBetween(1, 6) + RandomBetween(1, 6), i, 1, 1000) CountIf(x == 7, L) / 1000

AWARENESS OF METHOD · CALCULATE OR SIMULATE?

Calculating with a tree or a table gives the exact value, but needs a manageable model.

Simulating also works for experiments that are too complicated to calculate, such as queues or the spread of a disease. But a simulation only gives estimates, and they only get more accurate slowly: for one more digit of accuracy you need about a hundred times as many trials.

EXCURSIONFOOD FOR THOUGHT

The Monty Hall problem

In a game show there are three doors. Behind one there is a car, behind each of the other two a goat. You choose a door. The host knows where the car is. He opens one of the other two doors, and always one with a goat. Then he asks: “Do you want to switch?”

In 1990 Marilyn vos Savant answered this question in her column “Ask Marilyn” in the US magazine Parade: switching doubles the chance of winning from $\tfrac13$ to $\tfrac23$. She then received more than 10,000 letters from readers. Most writers were sure she was wrong, many of them with doctorates.

Sources: Steve Selvin, letter in The American Statistician 29 (1975), p. 67. Marilyn vos Savant, “Ask Marilyn”, Parade, 9 September 1990.

INTERACTIVE · PLAY IT YOURSELF
WHY SWITCHING WINS

Explain with a two-stage tree: first stage your choice, second stage your strategy.

Your first choice hits the car with $\tfrac13$ and a goat with $\tfrac23$.

Staying only wins if the first choice was already right: $\tfrac13$.

Switching wins exactly when the first choice was a goat. The host has already shown the other goat, so the car must be behind the last door: $\tfrac23$.

What matters is that the host knows where the car is and never opens the car door.

LOOKING BACKTHE ESSENTIALS AT A GLANCE

MULTI-STAGE RANDOM EXPERIMENT

Several sub-experiments one after the other. In the tree diagram every stage is a branching, every outcome a path. At every branching the probabilities add up to $1$.

Two balls from without replacement: paths RR, RB, BR, BB.

1ST PATH RULE

Probability of an outcome = product along the path.

$P(\text{RB}) = \tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac24 = \tfrac{3}{10}$

2ND PATH RULE

Probability of an event = sum of the paths that belong to it.

$P(\text{different colours}) = \tfrac{3}{10} + \tfrac{3}{10} = \tfrac35$

COMPLEMENTARY EVENT

$P(E) = 1 - P(\overline{E})$. Especially useful for “at least once”.

Spinner twice, $P(\text{red}) = \tfrac23$: $P(\text{at least once red}) = 1 - \tfrac19 = \tfrac89$

WITH / WITHOUT REPLACEMENT

With replacement the probabilities stay the same, without replacement they change from stage to stage.

with: $\tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac35 = \tfrac{9}{25}$   without: $\tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac24 = \tfrac{3}{10}$

TWO-WAY TABLE

Two characteristics with two values each. Four inner cells, totals in the margins, bottom right $1$ or the total number. The inner cells are the ends of the paths of the matching tree.

$P(\overline{A} \cap \overline{B}) = 1 - P(A) - P(B) + P(A \cap B)$

RELATIVE FREQUENCY

$h_n(E) = \tfrac{k}{n}$. As $n$ grows it settles down near $P(E)$ (empirical law of large numbers).

600 rolls, 112 sixes: $h_{600} \approx 0.187$, close to $\tfrac16$.

SIMULATION

Model with random numbers, carry out very often, relative frequency as an estimate. Gives estimates, not exact values.

2,000 people are tested, 10% are ill. The test correctly identifies 90% of the ill and 80% of the healthy. How many people test positive altogether?

200 ill: 180 positive. 1,800 healthy: 20% false positive, i.e. 360. 540 altogether.

$P(B) = 0.45$ and $P(A \cap B) = 0.2$. Calculate $P(\overline{A} \cap B)$.

The column $B$ adds up to $P(B)$: $0.45 - 0.2 = 0.25$.
