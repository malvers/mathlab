Periodic processes: sine functions

The capsule on the big wheel, the length of the day over the year, ebb and flow, the current from the socket: everything repeats itself at fixed intervals. The function that describes such processes comes from the unit circle: the sine function.

UNIT 15 LESSONS

1The sine function on the unit circle

A point moves anticlockwise on the circle with radius 1. How high above the $x$-axis is it when it has turned through the angle $\alpha$? And how does this height depend on the angle?

In a right-angled triangle $\sin\alpha = \tfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$. On the unit circle the hypotenuse is 1, so $\sin\alpha$ is exactly the height of the point. This explanation works for every angle, also beyond 90° and for more than one turn.

For functions, angles are measured in radians: as the length of the arc on the unit circle. A whole turn is $2\pi$ long, so $360^\circ \mathrel{\hat{=}} 2\pi$.

KEY POINT · SINE FUNCTION

Radians: $x = \alpha \cdot \tfrac{\pi}{180^\circ}$, so $90^\circ = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$, $180^\circ = \pi$, $360^\circ = 2\pi$.

$f(x) = \sin x$: $D = \mathbb{R}$, $W = [-1, 1]$, period $2\pi$, zeros $x = k \cdot \pi$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$), maxima at $\tfrac{\pi}{2} + k \cdot 2\pi$, symmetric about the origin.

The cosine function is the sine function shifted $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ to the left: $\cos x = \sin\left(x + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$.

Calculator: watch the mode. DEG calculates in degrees, RAD in radians. In RAD mode $\sin 1$ is about $0.841$.

Memory aid: $1$ radian is about $57.3^\circ$.

EXAMPLE 2 · TWO SOLUTIONS

For which $x$ between $0$ and $2\pi$ is $\sin x = 0.5$?

The calculator gives $x_1 = \tfrac{\pi}{6}$ ($30^\circ$). Because the curve is symmetric about the line $x = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$ there is a second solution: $x_2 = \pi - \tfrac{\pi}{6} = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}$ ($150^\circ$). After that everything repeats every $2\pi$.

A periodic process from nature: the length of the day in Dresden varies roughly like a sine curve over the year, between about 8 hours in December and 16.5 hours in June.

UNIT 25 LESSONS

2Amplitude, period and shift

The capsule on the big wheel moves between 3 m and 53 m in height, mean height 28 m, one turn in 20 minutes. How do you have to change $\sin x$ so that the curve fits exactly?

KEY POINT · GENERAL SINE FUNCTION

$$f(x) = a \cdot \sin\big(b\,(x - c)\big) + d$$

$|a|$ = amplitude (largest deviation from the midline) · $p = \tfrac{2\pi}{b}$ = period · $c$ = shift to the right · $d$ = shift upwards, the midline is $y = d$.

Range: $d - |a| \leq f(x) \leq d + |a|$.

From max and min: $a = \tfrac{\text{max} - \text{min}}{2}$, $d = \tfrac{\text{max} + \text{min}}{2}$.

GeoGebra: create sliders for $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ and enter $f(x) = a \sin(b (x - c)) + d$.

INTERACTIVE · SINE WORKSHOP

Change the four parameters one at a time and watch what happens to the curve. Dashed: $\sin x$.

EXAMPLE 3 · THE BIG WHEEL

Set up a function for the height of the capsule (getting in at the bottom at $t = 0$, $t$ in min). For how long are you higher than 40 m?

Max 53, min 3: $a = 25$, $d = 28$. Period 20 min: $b = \tfrac{2\pi}{20} = \tfrac{\pi}{10}$. The sine is at the bottom at $-\tfrac{\pi}{2}$, a quarter period before it crosses the midline: $c = 5$.

$$h(t) = 25 \cdot \sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{10}(t - 5)\right) + 28 \qquad \text{Check: } h(0) = 25 \cdot \sin\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) + 28 = 3$$

$h(t) = 40$ gives $t \approx 6.6$ min and $t \approx 13.4$ min. So you are higher than 40 m for about 6.8 minutes per turn.

Give a sine function with amplitude 3, period $\pi$ and midline $y = 1$.

$b = \tfrac{2\pi}{\pi} = 2$: $f(x) = 3\sin(2x) + 1$

The mains voltage in Germany (and in the UK) is $U(t) = 325\,\text{V} \cdot \sin(2\pi \cdot 50 \cdot t)$ ($t$ in s). What are the amplitude and the period? How often per second does the voltage change sign?

Amplitude 325 V, period $\tfrac{1}{50}$ s $= 0.02$ s. Twice in every period, so 100 times per second. The 230 V is an average value: $325 : \sqrt2 \approx 230$.

In a harbour the water level varies between 1 m (low tide) and 4 m (high tide). From high tide to high tide takes about 12.4 h. At midnight it is high tide. Set up a function.

At midnight it is at the highest point. The sine reaches it a quarter period after crossing the midline, so $c = -\tfrac{12.4}{4} = -3.1$.

$W(t) = 1.5 \cdot \sin\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{12.4}(t + 3.1)\right) + 2.5$. Check: $W(0) = 1.5 \cdot \sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2.5 = 4$.

Solve the big-wheel exploration yourself: use a calculator to find the two times in the first turn at which the capsule is exactly 40 m high.

$\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{10}(t - 5)\right) = 0.48 \Rightarrow \tfrac{\pi}{10}(t - 5) \approx 0.5007$ or $\approx \pi - 0.5007$. So $t \approx 6.59$ min and $t \approx 13.41$ min.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

Without a calculator, sketch $f(x) = \sin x$ and $g(x) = 2\sin x$ for $0 \leq x \leq 2\pi$ in one coordinate system.

Both have the same zeros $0$, $\pi$, $2\pi$. $g$ goes up to $2$ and down to $-2$, $f$ only to $1$ and $-1$.

A pendulum swings to and fro every 2 s, the largest displacement is 15 cm. At $t = 0$ it is in the middle and moving to the right. Set up $s(t)$.

$s(t) = 15 \cdot \sin(\pi t)$ (in cm). Period $\tfrac{2\pi}{\pi} = 2$ s.

Connecting with chapter 3.1: what properties does $f(x) = \sin(2x)$ have? State the period, zeros and symmetry.

Period $\pi$, zeros $x = k \cdot \tfrac{\pi}{2}$, symmetric about the origin, because $\sin(-2x) = -\sin(2x)$.

EXCURSIONMATHEMATICS AND MUSIC

Sounds are sums of sine curves

A pure tone is a sine-shaped vibration of the air. The concert pitch A (A4) vibrates 440 times per second, so its period is $\tfrac{1}{440}$ s. Yet a violin, a trumpet and a voice play the same note quite differently. The reason: with every note, overtones vibrate along at twice, three times, four times the rate.

Joseph Fourier showed that practically every periodic vibration can be built from sine curves. MP3, JPEG, mobile communications and noise cancelling are all based on this idea today.

Source: Joseph Fourier, “Théorie analytique de la chaleur” (1822).
