Regression: functions from measurements

In the lab, in business, in sport: you measure values and look for the function that fits them best. This is called regression. The computer finds the curve in seconds. The real work stays with you: choosing the right model and checking critically what it says.

UNIT 1WITH DIGITAL TOOLS · 3 LESSONS

1The function that fits best

A candle is measured every hour: 20 · 18.4 · 16.9 · 15.2 · 13.8 · 12.1 · 10.6 cm. The points lie almost, but not exactly, on a straight line. Which line is the best?

The computer chooses the function for which the sum of the squared distances between the measured points and the curve is smallest (method of least squares). For the candle it gives $f(t) \approx -1.568\,t + 19.99$. It has burnt down after about $12.7$ hours.

KEY POINT · REGRESSION

A regression function is the function of a chosen type that fits the measurements best.

Linear $f(x) = mx + n$ · quadratic $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ · exponential $f(x) = a \cdot q^x$

The coefficient of determination $R^2$ lies between 0 and 1. The closer to 1, the better the function describes the measurements.

Procedure: enter the measurements · look at the scatter plot · choose the type of function with reasons · calculate the regression · interpret and check the result.

GeoGebra: points as a list L = {(0,20), (1,18.4), …}, then FitLine(L), FitPoly(L, 2) or FitExp(L).

Graphing calculator: enter the values in lists, choose LinReg, QuadReg or ExpReg in the statistics menu.

INTERACTIVE · REGRESSION LAB

Choose a series of measurements and a model. You can move the measured points, the curve is recalculated at once. The dashed line marks the place of the forecast.

EXAMPLE 1 · BRAKING DISTANCE

Measured braking distances: 30 km/h: 9.5 m · 50: 24 m · 70: 50 m · 90: 80 m · 110: 122 m · 130: 168 m. Which model fits? How long is the braking distance at 150 km/h?

Linear: $R^2 \approx 0.968$, but the points clearly lie on a curve. Quadratic: $f(v) \approx 0.00989\,v^2 + 0.013\,v - 0.27$ with $R^2 \approx 0.9997$. This also fits the physics: kinetic energy grows with the square of the speed.

Forecast: $f(150) \approx 224$ m. The German driving-school rule of thumb $\left(\tfrac{v}{10}\right)^2 = 225$ m gives almost the same.

In the lab, move one single point of the candle far upwards (a “measurement error”). How do the line and $R^2$ change?

The line tilts towards the outlier, $R^2$ drops clearly. Outliers have a big influence because the distances are squared. So always check for measurement errors.

2Checking models critically

For the cooling tea the quadratic and the exponential model fit equally well: both $R^2 \approx 0.9995$. After 45 minutes the exponential model predicts a difference of 6.8 °C from room temperature, the quadratic one 17.6 °C. Who is right?

The quadratic curve has a lowest point at about 36 minutes and rises again afterwards: the tea would get warmer by itself. Physically that is nonsense. So a high $R^2$ alone is not enough. A model must also fit the context.

KEY POINT · CRITICISING MODELS

Interpolation (a forecast within the measured values) is usually reliable. Extrapolation (far outside them) is uncertain.

Questions for every model: does the type of function fit the situation? What happens for very large or small $x$? Are there outliers? Was only a short period measured?

Outlook: there are other models, for example power functions, logistic growth with a saturation limit or sine regression for periodic data.

EXAMPLE 2 · WORLD POPULATION

The world population (in billions): 1950: 2.50 · 1960: 3.02 · 1970: 3.70 · 1980: 4.44 · 1990: 5.32 · 2000: 6.15 · 2010: 6.99 · 2020: 7.84. Compare the models in the regression lab (series “World population”). What do they say for 2050?

Exponential: $R^2 \approx 0.986$, forecast for 2050 about 13.7 billion · linear: $R^2 \approx 0.995$, about 10.1 billion · quadratic: $R^2 \approx 0.999$, about 11.0 billion.

Surprisingly, since about 1970 the population has been growing linearly rather than exponentially, the growth rate is falling. The United Nations expect about 9.7 billion for 2050. No simple model can foresee birth rates, education and prosperity.

EXERCISES

Explain the difference between interpolation and extrapolation using the candle as an example.

Length after 2.5 h (between the measurements): interpolation, reliable. Length after 20 h: extrapolation, the model gives a negative length, i.e. nonsense.

In the lab, test all three models for the tea. Which is best suited for a forecast after one hour? Give reasons.

Exponential: the temperature difference decreases more and more slowly and approaches 0. Linear would drop below 0, quadratic would rise again.

A company has increased its turnover by 30% in each of the last 4 years and extends the exponential model 20 years into the future. Evaluate.

$1.3^{20} \approx 190$: turnover would grow almost two hundredfold. Markets are limited, competitors appear. Four years of data do not support a forecast over 20 years.

EXCURSIONALWAYS LOOK FIRST

Four data sets, one line

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe presented four data sets that have almost the same regression line $y \approx 3 + 0.5x$ and the same $R^2 \approx 0.67$. When you look at them, they are completely different: once a cloud of points, once a curve, once a line with an outlier, once almost only a single $x$-value.

The lesson: numbers like $R^2$ never replace looking at the data.

Source: F. J. Anscombe, “Graphs in Statistical Analysis”, The American Statistician 27 (1973), pp. 17–21.
