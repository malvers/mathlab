Inverse functions and logarithms

Working out the area from the side length is easy. But the side length from the area? The capital from the time, but the time from the capital? For that you need the inverse. The inverse of squaring is the square root, the inverse of raising to an unknown power is the logarithm.

UNIT 15 LESSONS

1A function and its inverse

A square tile has the area $A = s^2$. At the DIY store you need it the other way round: an area of 900 cm² belongs to the side length $s = \sqrt{900} = 30$ cm.

The inverse function $f^{-1}$ undoes what $f$ does: if $f(x) = y$, then $f^{-1}(y) = x$. In the coordinate system $x$ and $y$ are swapped, so the graph is reflected in the line $y = x$.

Only a function in which every $y$-value occurs just once can be inverted. For $f(x) = x^2$, both $x = 3$ and $x = -3$ belong to $y = 9$. That is why the domain is restricted to $x \geq 0$. Then the inverse function is the square root function $f^{-1}(x) = \sqrt{x}$.

KEY POINT · INVERSE FUNCTION

Finding it: solve $y = f(x)$ for $x$, then swap $x$ and $y$.

Graph: reflection of the graph of $f$ in the line $y = x$. Domain and range swap roles.

Roots as powers: $\sqrt{x} = x^{\frac12}$, $\sqrt[n]{x} = x^{\frac1n}$, $\sqrt[n]{x^m} = x^{\frac{m}{n}}$ (for $x \geq 0$).

Notation: the $^{-1}$ in $f^{-1}$ is not a reciprocal. $f^{-1}(x)$ is not $\tfrac{1}{f(x)}$.

Irrational: $\sqrt2 \approx 1.41421356\ldots$ is not a fraction. Its decimal expansion never ends and never repeats.

INTERACTIVE · REFLECTING IN $y = x$

Choose a function and drag the yellow point along the graph. Its mirror image runs along the inverse function.

EXAMPLE 1 · FINDING THE INVERSE FUNCTION

Find the inverse function of $f(x) = 2x + 1$.

$y = 2x + 1 \Rightarrow x = \tfrac{y - 1}{2}$. Swap: $f^{-1}(x) = \tfrac{x - 1}{2}$. Check: $f(3) = 7$ and $f^{-1}(7) = 3$.

EXAMPLE 2 · POWER NOTATION

Calculate without a calculator: $8^{\frac23}$ and $27^{-\frac13}$.

$8^{\frac23} = \left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 4$ · $27^{-\frac13} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{27}} = \tfrac13$

The function $f(x) = (x - 1)^2$ with $x \geq 1$ is to be inverted. Find $f^{-1}$ and state the domain and range of both functions.

$y = (x - 1)^2 \Rightarrow x = 1 + \sqrt{y}$, so $f^{-1}(x) = 1 + \sqrt{x}$. $f$: $D$: $x \geq 1$, $W$: $y \geq 0$. $f^{-1}$: $D$: $x \geq 0$, $W$: $y \geq 1$.

Draw $f(x) = 2x + 1$ and $f^{-1}$ in one coordinate system. At which point do they intersect? Why does it lie on $y = x$?

$2x + 1 = \tfrac{x - 1}{2} \Rightarrow x = -1$: $S(-1, -1)$. A point that stays in place when reflected in $y = x$ must lie on that line.

Prove that $\sqrt2$ is not a fraction.

Assume $\sqrt2 = \tfrac{p}{q}$ as a fraction in its lowest terms. Square it and think about whether $p$ and $q$ are even.

From $2 = \tfrac{p^2}{q^2}$ it follows that $p^2 = 2q^2$. So $p^2$ is even, and therefore $p = 2k$ as well. Then $4k^2 = 2q^2$, so $q^2 = 2k^2$ and $q$ is even too. Then the fraction would not be in its lowest terms, a contradiction. So $\sqrt2$ is irrational.

2The logarithm

You solve $2^x = 32$ in your head: $x = 5$. But $2^x = 10$? The solution lies between 3 and 4. It has its own name: $x = \log_2 10$, “the logarithm of 10 to base 2”.

KEY POINT · LOGARITHM

$\log_a b$ is the number to which you have to raise $a$ to get $b$: $$\log_a b = x \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad a^x = b \qquad (a > 0,\ a \neq 1,\ b > 0)$$

Common logarithm: $\lg b = \log_{10} b$ (the log key on your calculator). Natural logarithm: $\ln b = \log_e b$ with Euler's number $e \approx 2.71828$.

Change of base for the calculator: $\log_a b = \dfrac{\lg b}{\lg a} = \dfrac{\ln b}{\ln a}$

Logarithmic function $f(x) = \log_a x$: the inverse of $a^x$. $D$: $x > 0$, $W = \mathbb{R}$, zero $x = 1$, vertical asymptote $x = 0$. Increasing for $a > 1$, decreasing for $0 < a < 1$.

Always true: $\log_a 1 = 0$, $\log_a a = 1$, $\log_a a^x = x$.

History: John Napier published the first tables of logarithms in 1614. They turned multiplications into additions and made the calculations of astronomy much easier.

EXAMPLE 4 · COUNTING DIGITS

How many digits does $2^{100}$ have?

A number $z$ with $10^{k} \leq z < 10^{k+1}$ has $k + 1$ digits, and $k$ is the whole-number part of $\lg z$. $\lg 2^{100} = 100 \cdot \lg 2 \approx 30.1$. So $2^{100}$ has 31 digits.

Compare $f(x) = \log_2 x$ and $g(x) = \log_{0.5} x$: what do they have in common, how do they differ? Use the graph above.

In common: $D$: $x > 0$, zero at 1, asymptote $x = 0$. $f$ increases, $g$ decreases. $g$ is the mirror image of $f$ in the $x$-axis: $g(x) = -f(x)$.

How many digits does $3^{50}$ have? How many does the record prime $2^{136{,}279{,}841} - 1$, discovered in 2024, have?

$50 \cdot \lg 3 \approx 23.86$: 24 digits. $136{,}279{,}841 \cdot \lg 2 \approx 41{,}024{,}319.6$: so about 41 million digits (the “$-1$” does not change the number of digits).

3Solving exponential equations

In chapter 2 a question was left open: when does €1,000 at 3% interest become €1,500? We are looking for $n$ in $1000 \cdot 1.03^n = 1500$. Now you can solve it exactly.

First get the power on its own: $1.03^n = 1.5$. Then take logarithms: $n = \log_{1.03} 1.5 = \dfrac{\lg 1.5}{\lg 1.03} \approx 13.72$. After 14 years the savings goal is reached.

KEY POINT · EXPONENTIAL EQUATION

For $a > 0$, $a \neq 1$, $b > 0$, $a^x = b$ has exactly one solution: $x = \log_a b = \dfrac{\lg b}{\lg a}$. For $b \leq 0$ there is no solution.

Procedure: get the power on its own on one side · take logarithms · check.

Half-life with factor $q$ per time step: $T_{1/2} = \dfrac{\lg 0.5}{\lg q}$ · doubling time: $T_2 = \dfrac{\lg 2}{\lg q}$

Without a calculator it works if both sides are powers of the same base: $4^x = 32 \Rightarrow 2^{2x} = 2^5 \Rightarrow x = 2.5$.

INTERACTIVE · SOLVING $a^x = b$

Enter the base and the right-hand side. Fractions and decimals work too.

EXAMPLE 6 · DOUBLING TIME

A population grows by 1.2% per year. After how many years has it doubled?

$1.012^n = 2 \Rightarrow n = \tfrac{\lg 2}{\lg 1.012} \approx 58.1$ years. Rule of thumb: $72 : 1.2 = 60$.

EXERCISES

a) $\approx 5.644$   b) $\approx 14.21$   c) $\approx 10.32$   d) $3^x = 27$, $x = 3$

The body breaks down a medicine at 15% per hour. After what time is less than 10% of the dose in the blood?

$0.85^t = 0.1 \Rightarrow t = \tfrac{\lg 0.1}{\lg 0.85} \approx 14.2$ h, so after just over 14 hours.

A substance decreases by 5% per year. Calculate the half-life.

$T_{1/2} = \tfrac{\lg 0.5}{\lg 0.95} \approx 13.5$ years

Radiocarbon dating: carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years. A bone contains only 30% of its original C-14. How old is it?

$0.5^{t/5730} = 0.3 \Rightarrow \tfrac{t}{5730} = \tfrac{\lg 0.3}{\lg 0.5} \approx 1.737 \Rightarrow t \approx 9{,}950$. The bone is about 10,000 years old.

State the inverse function of $f(x) = 10^x$ and sketch both graphs.

Connecting with chapter 3.2: iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. After how many days is less than 1% left?

$0.5^{t/8} = 0.01 \Rightarrow t = 8 \cdot \tfrac{\lg 0.01}{\lg 0.5} \approx 53.2$ days.

Show: $\lg(100 \cdot 1000) = \lg 100 + \lg 1000$. Formulate a general rule from this and explain why it holds.

$5 = 2 + 3$. In general $\log_a(u \cdot v) = \log_a u + \log_a v$, because when you multiply powers of the same base the exponents add: $a^r \cdot a^s = a^{r+s}$.

EXCURSIONLOGARITHMS UNCOVER FRAUD

Why so many numbers begin with 1

In 1881 the astronomer Simon Newcomb noticed that in tables of logarithms the first pages were much more worn than the last ones: numbers with a leading 1 were looked up more often. In 1938 the physicist Frank Benford examined more than 20,000 numbers from real life and found the same pattern.

According to Benford's law a number begins with the digit $d$ with probability $P(d) = \lg\left(1 + \tfrac1d\right)$.
