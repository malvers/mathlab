Systems of linear equations and matrices

How many tickets of each kind did the cinema sell? How much does a factory have to produce so that all raw materials are used up? Several unknowns, several conditions: a system of linear equations. With matrices you write it down compactly and solve it step by step with Gaussian elimination, for large systems with a CAS.

UNIT 15 LESSONS

1Matrices and the matrix form of a linear system

For the cinema evening, with $x$ standard, $y$ concession and $z$ children's tickets:

$$\begin{array}{rcrcrcl} x &+& y &+& z &=& 200 \\ 8x &+& 6y &+& 4z &=& 1280 \\ && y &-& 2z &=& 0 \end{array}$$

Really only the numbers matter. The letters always stand in the same places.

If you write only the numbers in a rectangular array, you get a matrix. The system then reads $A \cdot \vec{x} = \vec{b}$:

$$\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 8 & 6 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}}_{A} \cdot \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix}}_{\vec{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 200 \\ 1280 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}}_{\vec{b}} \qquad \text{in short:} \qquad \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 1 & 1 & 200 \\ 8 & 6 & 4 & 1280 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 0 \end{array}\right)$$

The form on the right is called the augmented matrix.

KEY POINT · MATRICES

An $m \times n$ matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns. The element in row $i$ and column $j$ is called $a_{ij}$.

Special matrices: square ($m = n$) · upper triangular matrix (only zeros below the diagonal) · diagonal matrix (non-zero only on the diagonal) · identity matrix $I$ (a diagonal of ones) · zero matrix (only zeros) · column matrix ($m \times 1$) · row matrix ($1 \times n$)

Examples: $I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ · upper triangular matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$

Computer science: a greyscale image is a matrix: every element is the brightness of a pixel.

UNIT 2WITHOUT A CALCULATOR · 5 LESSONS

2Gaussian elimination

An upper triangular matrix is easy: for $\;x + y + z = 6$, $\;y + z = 5$, $\;z = 3$ you read off $z = 3$, substitute into the second row ($y = 2$) and then into the first ($x = 1$). Gauss's idea: transform every system so that it has this echelon form.

KEY POINT · GAUSSIAN ELIMINATION

The solution set does not change if you

  • swap two rows,
  • multiply a row by a number other than zero,
  • add a multiple of one row to another.

Gauss: with these operations produce zeros below the diagonal, first in column 1, then in column 2 (echelon form), then substitute from the bottom upwards.

Gauss-Jordan: carry on until the identity matrix stands on the left. Then the solution stands directly on the right.

Notation: “$\text{II} - 8 \cdot \text{I}$” means: replace row II by row II minus 8 times row I.

Check: always substitute into all the original equations.

EXAMPLE 1 · THE CINEMA EVENING

Solve the cinema system by Gaussian elimination.

$$\left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 1 & 1 & 200 \\ 8 & 6 & 4 & 1280 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 0 \end{array}\right) \xrightarrow{\;\text{II} - 8 \cdot \text{I}\;} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 1 & 1 & 200 \\ 0 & -2 & -4 & -320 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 0 \end{array}\right) \xrightarrow{\;\text{II} : (-2)\;} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 1 & 1 & 200 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 160 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 0 \end{array}\right)$$

$$\xrightarrow{\;\text{III} - \text{II}\;} \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 1 & 1 & 200 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 160 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 & -160 \end{array}\right)$$

Backwards: $z = 40$, $y = 160 - 80 = 80$, $x = 200 - 80 - 40 = 80$. 80 standard, 80 concession, 40 children's tickets.

Check: $80 + 80 + 40 = 200$ · $640 + 480 + 160 = 1280$ · $80 = 2 \cdot 40$.

UNIT 35 LESSONS

3No, one or infinitely many solutions

Not every system works out. At the end of Gaussian elimination there may be a row with only zeros on the left. Then it depends on the right-hand side.

KEY POINT · SOLUTION SETS

Exactly one solution: the echelon form has a pivot (first non-zero element) in every row.

No solution: a row $(0 \;\; 0 \;\; 0 \mid c)$ with $c \neq 0$ appears, i.e. the contradiction $0 = c$.

Infinitely many solutions: a zero row $(0 \;\; 0 \;\; 0 \mid 0)$ appears and no contradiction. One variable stays free, you set it as a parameter $t$.

Geometrically (two unknowns): two lines meet in one point, are parallel or lie on top of each other.

Outlook: with three unknowns every equation describes a plane in space. Three planes can meet in a point, in a line or not at all. More on this in vector geometry.

INTERACTIVE · TWO LINES
EXAMPLE 2 · INFINITELY MANY

Solve $\;x + y + z = 3$, $\;x - y + z = 1$, $\;2x + 2z = 4$.

$\text{II} - \text{I}$: $-2y = -2$ · $\text{III} - 2\,\text{I}$: $-2y = -2$ · then $\text{III} - \text{II}$: $0 = 0$, a zero row.

$y = 1$, $z = t$ free, $x = 2 - t$: $L = \{(2 - t,\ 1,\ t) \mid t \in \mathbb{R}\}$

EXAMPLE 3 · NO SOLUTION

Solve $\;x + 2y = 3$, $\;2x + 4y = 5$.

$\text{II} - 2\,\text{I}$: $0 = -1$. A contradiction: $L = \{\,\}$. The two lines are parallel.

EXERCISES

How many solutions does the system with this echelon form have? a) $\left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 2 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 5 \end{array}\right)$   b) $\left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 2 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right)$   c) $\left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 2 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 2 \end{array}\right)$

a) none ($0 = 5$)   b) infinitely many   c) exactly one: $z = 1$, $y = -1$, $x = 5$

For which $a$ does $\;x + y = 1$, $\;2x + ay = 2$ have infinitely many solutions? What holds for all other $a$?

$\text{II} - 2\,\text{I}$: $(a - 2)y = 0$. For $a = 2$ this is $0 = 0$: infinitely many solutions. Otherwise $y = 0$, $x = 1$: exactly one solution.

For which $b$ does $\;x + 2y = 3$, $\;2x + 4y = b$ have no solution?

$\text{II} - 2\,\text{I}$: $0 = b - 6$. For $b \neq 6$ no solution, for $b = 6$ infinitely many.

A dealer mixes two kinds of coffee at €8/kg and €12/kg to make 10 kg of a blend for €100. At which total price would there be no sensible solution? Give reasons.

$x + y = 10$, $8x + 12y = P$ always has exactly one solution $y = \tfrac{P - 80}{4}$. But it only makes sense for $0 \leq y \leq 10$, i.e. $80 \leq P \leq 120$. For $P = 100$: 5 kg of each. For $P$ outside this range amounts would be negative. Mathematically solvable does not always mean sensible in context.

4Large systems with a CAS, calculating with matrices

In practice linear systems often have dozens or thousands of unknowns, for example in the structural analysis of a bridge or in image processing. For these you use digital tools. In class a CAS or GeoGebra is enough.

In GeoGebra:Solve({w+x+y+z=10, w-x+y-z=-2, 2w+x-y+z=5, w+2x+3y-z=10}, {w,x,y,z})

KEY POINT · MATRIX OPERATIONS

Addition and subtraction: only for matrices of the same size, element by element: $(A + B)_{ij} = a_{ij} + b_{ij}$.

Scalar multiplication: every element is multiplied by the number $r$: $(r \cdot A)_{ij} = r \cdot a_{ij}$.

Transposing: rows become columns: $(A^T)_{ij} = a_{ji}$. An $m \times n$ matrix becomes an $n \times m$ matrix.

Computer science: spreadsheets, databases and image formats store data as matrices. Rotating an image by 90° basically means transposing and reflecting.

Three friends buy drinks. Anna: 2 colas, 1 juice, 1 water for €8. Ben: 1 cola, 2 juices for €6.50. Cem: 1 juice, 3 waters for €5. What does each drink cost?

$2c + s + w = 8$, $c + 2s = 6.5$, $s + 3w = 5$ gives cola €2.50, juice €2, water €1.

Connecting with chapter 3.3: find the parabola through $(-1, 6)$, $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 3)$.

$a - b + c = 6$, $a + b + c = 2$, $4a + 2b + c = 3$. From I and II: $b = -2$, $a + c = 4$; from III: $4a + c = 7$, so $a = 1$, $c = 3$. $f(x) = x^2 - 2x + 3$

A system of 3 equations has the solutions $(1,\,2,\,3)$ and $(3,\,2,\,1)$. Explain why it then has infinitely many solutions.

If it had exactly one solution, there would be no second one. With linear equations, if there are two solutions, every point on the line through them is a solution too, for example $(2,\,2,\,2)$.

EXCURSIONCOMPUTER SCIENCE

How a search engine sorts pages

At the end of the 1990s Sergey Brin and Larry Page had an idea: a web page is important if important pages link to it. A “random surfer” clicks through the web: with probability 85% they follow a link on the current page, with 15% they jump to any page. How often they end up on a page in the long run is its PageRank.

Mathematically this is a matrix by which you multiply a vector again and again. After a few steps the vector hardly changes any more. In the example below A is almost level with C, although only a single page links to A: that one link comes from C, the most important page.

Source: S. Brin, L. Page, “The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine”, Computer Networks 30 (1998).
