Monotonicity, extrema, points of inflection

When is turnover highest? From when on does it grow more slowly? Where is a road steepest? The derivative reveals where a graph increases and decreases, where its maximum and minimum points are and where it changes from a left bend into a right bend. With $f'$ and $f''$ you read a graph without drawing it.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Monotonicity and the first derivative

$f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2$ has the derivative $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x = 3x(x - 2)$. For $x < 0$ and $x > 2$, $f'(x) > 0$, in between $f'(x) < 0$.

Where all tangents point upwards, the graph increases. So $f$ increases for $x \le 0$, decreases for $0 \le x \le 2$ and increases again for $x \ge 2$. The zeros of $f'$ are the boundaries of the intervals of monotonicity.

KEY POINT · MONOTONICITY THEOREM

If $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x$ in an interval, then $f$ is strictly increasing there.

If $f'(x) < 0$ for all $x$ in an interval, then $f$ is strictly decreasing there.

How to do it: find the zeros of $f'$, then test the sign of $f'$ at one point in each subinterval.

The converse does not quite hold: $f(x) = x^3$ is strictly increasing everywhere, although $f'(0) = 0$. Single zeros of $f'$ do not spoil it.

Sign table: it arranges the intervals clearly, as in the widget of the next unit.

EXAMPLE 1 · INTERVALS OF MONOTONICITY

Investigate $f(x) = x^2 - 6x + 5$ for monotonicity.

$f'(x) = 2x - 6$, zero $x = 3$. For $x < 3$: $f' < 0$, $f$ decreases. For $x > 3$: $f' > 0$, $f$ increases.

EXAMPLE 2 · ALWAYS INCREASING

Show that $f(x) = x^3 + x$ is strictly increasing everywhere.

$f'(x) = 3x^2 + 1 \ge 1 > 0$ for all $x$. Strictly increasing.

EXERCISES

Find the intervals of monotonicity: a) $f(x) = x^2 + 4x$   b) $f(x) = -x^2 + 2x + 3$

a) $f'(x) = 2x + 4$: decreases for $x \le -2$, increases for $x \ge -2$   b) $f'(x) = -2x + 2$: increases for $x \le 1$, decreases for $x \ge 1$

Find the intervals of monotonicity of $f(x) = x^3 - 12x$.

$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12 = 3(x - 2)(x + 2)$: increases for $x \le -2$, decreases for $-2 \le x \le 2$, increases for $x \ge 2$.

Show that $f(x) = \mathrm{e}^x - x$ increases for $x > 0$ and decreases for $x < 0$.

$f'(x) = \mathrm{e}^x - 1$. For $x > 0$, $\mathrm{e}^x > 1$, so $f' > 0$. For $x < 0$, $\mathrm{e}^x < 1$, so $f' < 0$.

For which values of $a$ is $f(x) = x^3 + ax$ strictly increasing everywhere?

$f'(x) = 3x^2 + a$. For $a \ge 0$, $f' \ge 0$ with at most one zero at 0: increasing. For $a < 0$, $f'(0) = a < 0$: not increasing everywhere. So $a \ge 0$.

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Calculating turning points

For $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2$, $f'$ changes from $+$ to $-$ at $x = 0$ and from $-$ to $+$ at $x = 2$. What does this mean for the graph?

At $x = 0$ the graph stops increasing and starts decreasing: a maximum point $(0, 0)$. At $x = 2$ it is the other way round: a minimum point $(2, -4)$. At both the tangent is horizontal. But not every horizontal tangent belongs to a turning point: for $x^3$ at 0 the graph increases before and after.

KEY POINT · TURNING POINTS

Necessary condition: if $f$ has a local extremum at $x_0$, then $f'(x_0) = 0$.

Sufficient condition: if $f'(x_0) = 0$ and $f''(x_0) < 0$, there is a maximum; if $f'(x_0) = 0$ and $f''(x_0) > 0$, a minimum. Equivalently: $f'$ changes sign at $x_0$ (from $+$ to $-$: maximum).

Local and global: a local extremum is the largest or smallest value in its neighbourhood. You find the largest or smallest value on the whole interval by comparing the local extrema with the values at the end points.

Second derivative: $f''$ is the derivative of $f'$. For $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2$: $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x$, $f''(x) = 6x - 6$.

If $f''(x_0) = 0$: then only the change of sign of $f'$ helps. Without a change: saddle point.

Change the coefficients. Red: maximum, green: minimum, white: point of inflection. Show $f'$ and $f''$ and compare their zeros with the marked points.

EXAMPLE 3 · MAXIMUM AND MINIMUM

Find the turning points of $f(x) = -x^3 + 3x + 2$.

$f'(x) = -3x^2 + 3 = 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm 1$. $f''(x) = -6x$.

$f''(1) = -6 < 0$: maximum $(1, 4)$. $f''(-1) = 6 > 0$: minimum $(-1, 0)$.

EXAMPLE 4 · GLOBAL, WITH END POINTS

Find the largest and the smallest value of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on the interval $[-2, 3]$.

Local: maximum $(-1, 2)$, minimum $(1, -2)$. End points: $f(-2) = -2$, $f(3) = 18$.

Global maximum 18 at $x = 3$ (at the end point), global minimum $-2$ at $x = 1$ and $x = -2$.

Show: $f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$ has two minimum points and one maximum point.

UNIT 34 LESSONS

3Curvature and points of inflection

For the bestseller $V(t) = -t^3 + 12t^2$, $V'(t) = -3t^2 + 24t$ is the number of new buyers per month. When is it largest?

$V'$ is largest where $V''(t) = -6t + 24 = 0$, that is at $t = 4$. There sales rise most strongly: $V'(4) = 48$, that is 4,800 copies per month. Up to $t = 4$ the rise gets steeper (left bend), after that flatter (right bend). The point $(4, 128)$ is a point of inflection.

KEY POINT · CURVATURE AND POINT OF INFLECTION

$f''(x) > 0$: the graph is convex (a left bend, the gradient increases). $f''(x) < 0$: concave (a right bend, the gradient decreases).

At a point of inflection the curvature changes. Condition: $f''(x_0) = 0$ and $f''$ changes sign there (or $f'''(x_0) \neq 0$).

A point of inflection is a point of extreme gradient: the instantaneous rate of change is largest or smallest there. A point of inflection with a horizontal tangent is called a saddle point.

Memory aid: if you ride a bike along the graph from left to right, in a left bend you steer to the left.

In the widget above the point of inflection of a cubic curve always lies exactly halfway between the maximum and the minimum.

EXAMPLE 5 · POINT OF INFLECTION

Find the turning points and points of inflection of $f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 1$.

$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 3(x - 1)(x - 3)$, $f''(x) = 6x - 12$.

Maximum $(1, 5)$, minimum $(3, 1)$. $f''(x) = 0$ at $x = 2$ with a change of sign: point of inflection $(2, 3)$, there the graph decreases most steeply, $f'(2) = -3$.

EXAMPLE 6 · SADDLE POINT

Investigate $f(x) = x^3$ at 0.

$f'(0) = 0$, $f''(x) = 6x$ changes sign at 0. Saddle point $(0, 0)$: a point of inflection with a horizontal tangent.

Where is $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 1$ convex, where concave?

$f''(x) = 6x - 6$: concave for $x < 1$, convex for $x > 1$.

Find the points of inflection of $f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$.

$f''(x) = 12x^2 - 4 = 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3} \approx \pm 0.577$, with a change of sign. $f\left(\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right) = \tfrac19 - \tfrac23 = -\tfrac59$. Points of inflection $\left(\pm 0.577, -0.556\right)$

Solve the exploration “The bestseller” completely: when is the total number largest, and how large is it? Why does the model end after that?

$V'(t) = 3t(8 - t) = 0$ at $t = 8$, $V''(8) = -24 < 0$: maximum $V(8) = 256$, that is 25,600 copies. After that $V$ would decrease, but games that have been sold do not disappear: the model only holds up to $t = 8$.

Show: every function $f(x) = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d$ with $a \neq 0$ has exactly one point of inflection, and it lies at $x = -\tfrac{b}{3a}$.

$f''(x) = 6ax + 2b = 0 \Rightarrow x = -\tfrac{b}{3a}$. $f''$ is a straight line with gradient $6a \neq 0$, so it changes sign there.

For $0 \le t \le 24$ the temperature during a day is described by $T(t) = -0.01t^3 + 0.3t^2 - 1.5t + 10$ (°C, $t$ in hours). When is it coldest, when warmest? When does the temperature rise fastest?

EXCURSIONEXTREME VALUES BEFORE THE DERIVATIVE

Fermat's method of maxima and minima

Some 50 years before Newton and Leibniz, the French lawyer and mathematician Pierre de Fermat was already looking for extreme values. His idea: close to a maximum the function value hardly changes if you shift $x$ by a tiny amount $e$. You set $f(x + e)$ and $f(x)$ “approximately equal”, divide by $e$ and then drop $e$.

His example: a line segment of length $a$ is to be split into two parts $x$ and $a - x$ so that their product is as large as possible. From $(x + e)(a - x - e) \approx x(a - x)$, after multiplying out and dividing by $e$: $a - 2x - e \approx 0$, with $e$ dropped $x = \tfrac{a}{2}$.

At its core, this is exactly our condition $f'(x) = 0$. Fermat described the method around 1636 in letters, it was printed only after his death.

Source: Pierre de Fermat: Methodus ad disquirendam maximam et minimam. In: Varia Opera Mathematica, Toulouse 1679.

Compare the local extrema with the values at the end points.

CURVATURE

$f'' > 0$: left bend (convex) · $f'' < 0$: right bend (concave).
