Vectors in space

A plane in the sky, a drone in the classroom, the corner of a roof truss: in space you need three coordinates. And for displacements, forces and velocities you need arrows with a direction and a length, vectors. In this chapter you turn a real three-dimensional coordinate system with the mouse and calculate with vectors as with numbers.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Points in space, position vectors and connecting vectors

The point $P(3, 2, 3)$ lies 3 units in the $x$-direction (forwards), 2 in the $y$-direction (to the right) and 3 in the $z$-direction (upwards). The dashed lines in the picture above show the way there.

A vector describes a displacement: by how much in the $x$-, $y$- and $z$-direction. You write it as a column. All arrows with the same length and the same direction represent the same vector, no matter where they start.

KEY POINT · VECTORS IN SPACE

A vector $\vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \\ a_3 \end{pmatrix}$ describes a displacement in space. Its arrows are of equal length, parallel and point in the same direction.

The position vector $\vec{OP}$ of a point $P(p_1, p_2, p_3)$ moves the origin $O$ to $P$: $\vec{OP} = \begin{pmatrix} p_1 \\ p_2 \\ p_3 \end{pmatrix}$.

The connecting vector from $A$ to $B$ is $$\vec{AB} = \vec{OB} - \vec{OA} = \begin{pmatrix} b_1 - a_1 \\ b_2 - a_2 \\ b_3 - a_3 \end{pmatrix}$$ (“tip minus tail”).

Physics (Year 11): forces, velocities and displacements are vectors: they have a magnitude and a direction. Mass or temperature do not.

Axes: here the $x$-axis points forwards to the left, the $y$-axis to the right, the $z$-axis upwards, as in most German school books.

INTERACTIVE · POINTS AND VECTORS IN SPACE

EXAMPLE 1 · CONNECTING VECTOR

Find $\vec{AB}$ for $A(2, -1, 1)$ and $B(-1, 3, 4)$.

$\vec{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 - 2 \\ 3 - (-1) \\ 4 - 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$. Check in the widget: these are the starting values.

EXAMPLE 2 · THE CUBOID

Bottom: $(0, 0, 0)$, $(4, 0, 0)$, $(4, 3, 0)$, $(0, 3, 0)$. Top: the same with $z = 2$. The space diagonal from the origin to the opposite corner is $\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

EXERCISES

Find $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{BA}$ for $A(1, 2, 3)$ and $B(4, 0, 5)$. What do you notice?

$\vec{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $\vec{BA} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ 2 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} = -\vec{AB}$: the same length, opposite direction.

The point $A(2, 1, 0)$ is moved by $\vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$. Where does it end up?

$A'(3, -2, 4)$

On which axis or in which plane do the points $P(0, 3, 0)$, $Q(2, 0, 5)$, $R(0, 0, -1)$, $S(1, 4, 0)$ lie?

$P$ on the $y$-axis, $Q$ in the $xz$-plane, $R$ on the $z$-axis, $S$ in the $xy$-plane.

$ABCD$ is a parallelogram with $A(1, 0, 2)$, $B(4, 1, 2)$, $C(5, 3, 4)$. Find $D$.

In a parallelogram $\vec{AD} = \vec{BC}$.

$\vec{BC} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$, so $D = A + \vec{BC} = (2, 2, 4)$.

2Adding, subtracting, multiplying by a number

The drone flies $\vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, then $\vec{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, then $\vec{c} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Altogether this is a single displacement by $\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

Two displacements one after the other give another displacement: the sum. You attach the second arrow to the tip of the first. The order does not matter, both ways form the sides of a parallelogram, the sum is its diagonal.

KEY POINT · CALCULATING WITH VECTORS

$\vec{a} + \vec{b} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 + b_1 \\ a_2 + b_2 \\ a_3 + b_3 \end{pmatrix}$   ·   $\vec{a} - \vec{b} = \vec{a} + (-\vec{b})$   ·   $r \cdot \vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} r a_1 \\ r a_2 \\ r a_3 \end{pmatrix}$

$r \cdot \vec{a}$ is $|r|$ times as long as $\vec{a}$, for $r < 0$ pointing in the opposite direction. Two vectors with $\vec{b} = r \cdot \vec{a}$ are called parallel (collinear).

A sum of multiples like $2\vec{a} - 3\vec{b}$ is called a linear combination.

Zero vector: $\vec{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ moves nothing. $\vec{a} + (-\vec{a}) = \vec{0}$.

Forces: if several forces act on one point, their vector sum acts, the resultant force.

EXAMPLE 4 · PARALLEL?

Are $\vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\vec{b} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ -4 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ parallel? And $\vec{c} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$?

$\vec{b} = -2\vec{a}$: parallel, opposite direction. For $\vec{c} = r\vec{a}$, $r$ would have to be $2$ (from the first row) and $-1$ (from the third): not parallel.

EXERCISES

Calculate with $\vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\vec{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$: a) $\vec{a} + \vec{b}$   b) $\vec{a} - \vec{b}$   c) $3\vec{a}$   d) $\vec{a} + 2\vec{b}$

a) $(3, 3, 1)$   b) $(1, -3, -3)$   c) $(6, 0, -3)$   d) $(4, 6, 3)$, each as a column vector.

Three forces (in N) act on a point: $\vec{F_1} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$, $\vec{F_2} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix}$, $\vec{F_3} = \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ -4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$. How large is the resultant force?

$\vec{F_1} + \vec{F_2} + \vec{F_3} = \vec{0}$: the forces cancel out, the point stays at rest.

For which $t$ are $\vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ t \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\vec{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$ parallel?

$\vec{a} = 2\vec{b}$ requires $t = 6$.

Write $\vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ as a linear combination $r\vec{a} + s\vec{b}$ with $\vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\vec{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

Third row: $r = 1$. First row: $1 + 2s = 5 \Rightarrow s = 2$. Check second row: $2 + 2 = 4$. ✓ $\vec{v} = \vec{a} + 2\vec{b}$

UNIT 34 LESSONS

3Magnitude, midpoint and centroid

How long is the space diagonal of the cuboid with the edges 4, 3, 2? The diagonal of the base is $\sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5$, above it stands the height 2.

Pythagoras twice: the space diagonal is $\sqrt{5^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{29} \approx 5.39$. This is how you calculate the length of any vector in space.

KEY POINT · MAGNITUDE, MIDPOINT, CENTROID

Magnitude (length): $|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}$. The distance between two points is $|\vec{AB}|$.

Midpoint of the line segment $AB$: $\vec{OM} = \tfrac12 \left(\vec{OA} + \vec{OB}\right)$, that is the mean of the coordinates.

Centroid of the triangle $ABC$: $\vec{OS} = \tfrac13 \left(\vec{OA} + \vec{OB} + \vec{OC}\right)$.

Unit vector: $\tfrac{1}{|\vec{a}|} \cdot \vec{a}$ has length 1 and the same direction as $\vec{a}$.

Centroid: it divides every median in the ratio 2 : 1. A cardboard triangle balances exactly on this point.

EXAMPLE 5 · LENGTH AND UNIT VECTOR

Find $|\vec{a}|$ and the unit vector of $\vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

$|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = 3$, unit vector $\tfrac13 \vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 2/3 \\ -1/3 \\ 2/3 \end{pmatrix}$

EXAMPLE 6 · CENTROID

Find the centroid of the triangle with $A(1, 0, 2)$, $B(3, 4, 0)$, $C(5, 2, 4)$.

$S\left(\tfrac{1 + 3 + 5}{3}, \tfrac{0 + 4 + 2}{3}, \tfrac{2 + 0 + 4}{3}\right) = $ $S(3, 2, 2)$

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

A cube with edge length 2 stands with one corner at the origin, the edges on the positive axes. Give all corners and calculate the length of the space diagonal.

All points with coordinates from $\{0, 2\}$, for example $(2, 0, 2)$. Space diagonal $\sqrt{4 + 4 + 4} = 2\sqrt3 \approx 3.46$.

A plane flies with $\vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 200 \\ 150 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ km/h (airspeed), the wind blows with $\vec{w} = \begin{pmatrix} -20 \\ 30 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ km/h. How fast is it over the ground?

$\vec{v} + \vec{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 180 \\ 180 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, magnitude $180\sqrt2 \approx 254.6$ km/h.

Connecting with physics: a load of 100 N hangs from two ropes. Rope 1 pulls with $\vec{F_1} = \begin{pmatrix} -30 \\ 0 \\ 50 \end{pmatrix}$ N. With what force does rope 2 pull if everything is at rest?

Weight $\vec{G} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ -100 \end{pmatrix}$. Equilibrium: $\vec{F_2} = -\vec{F_1} - \vec{G} = \begin{pmatrix} 30 \\ 0 \\ 50 \end{pmatrix}$ N, magnitude $\approx 58.3$ N.

Show with vectors: in every parallelogram the diagonals bisect each other.

With $\vec{OD} = \vec{OA} + \vec{OC} - \vec{OB}$ (parallelogram), the midpoint of $AC$ is $\tfrac12(\vec{OA} + \vec{OC})$ and that of $BD$ is $\tfrac12(\vec{OB} + \vec{OD}) = \tfrac12(\vec{OA} + \vec{OC})$. Both are the same point.

EXCURSIONUNRECOGNISED AND AHEAD OF HIS TIME

A grammar school teacher invents vector calculus

Hermann Grassmann taught mathematics at a grammar school in Stettin. In 1844 he published a book with the title “Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre” (the theory of linear extension). In it he calculated with directed line segments, added them, multiplied them by numbers, and that in any number of dimensions. At its core this is vector calculus as you learn it here.

Hardly anyone understood the book. It was hard to read and far ahead of its time. Grassmann never got a professorship in mathematics and turned to linguistics, where he became famous for his work on Sanskrit. Only decades later did mathematicians and physicists recognise what he had created.

Source: Hermann Graßmann: Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre, ein neuer Zweig der Mathematik. Leipzig 1844.
