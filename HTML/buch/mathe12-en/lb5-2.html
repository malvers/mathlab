Scalar product and angles

Are two edges of a roof truss perpendicular to each other? At what angle do two rods meet in space? Is this quadrilateral really a rectangle? A single tool answers all of this: the scalar product. It turns two vectors into a number, and this number reveals the angle. Even search engines use it to compare texts.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1The scalar product and orthogonal vectors

For $(3, 1)$ and $(-1, 3)$ you get $3 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 3 = 0$, and the arrows are perpendicular to each other. The same for $(2, 5)$ and $(5, -2)$. Coincidence?

No coincidence. Using Pythagoras' theorem you can show: two vectors are perpendicular to each other exactly when the sum of the products of their components is 0. This sum is called the scalar product, because its result is a number (a scalar), not a vector.

KEY POINT · SCALAR PRODUCT

$$\vec{a} \circ \vec{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3$$

We have $\vec{a} \circ \vec{a} = |\vec{a}|^2$ and, if $\varphi$ is the angle between the vectors, $\vec{a} \circ \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| \cdot |\vec{b}| \cdot \cos \varphi$.

Orthogonal: two vectors (not equal to $\vec{0}$) are perpendicular to each other exactly when $\vec{a} \circ \vec{b} = 0$. You write $\vec{a} \perp \vec{b}$.

Sign: $\vec{a} \circ \vec{b} > 0$ for an acute angle, $< 0$ for an obtuse angle.

Geometrically: $\vec{a} \circ \vec{b}$ is the length of $\vec{a}$ times the length of the shadow that $\vec{b}$ casts on $\vec{a}$ (dashed in the widget).

Physics: the work is $W = \vec{F} \circ \vec{s}$: force in the direction of the path times the path.

Check whether $\vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\vec{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ are perpendicular to each other.

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Calculating angles and investigating quadrilaterals

From $\vec{a} \circ \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| \cdot |\vec{b}| \cdot \cos \varphi$ you can work out $\cos \varphi$ if you know the vectors. For $(3, 0, 1)$ and $(1, 3, 2)$: $\cos \varphi = \tfrac{5}{\sqrt{10} \cdot \sqrt{14}} \approx 0.423$, so $\varphi \approx 65°$.

So with the scalar product you can calculate every angle in space, and with connecting vectors also the angles in triangles and quadrilaterals. Together with the rules from chapter 5.1 you can prove which special shape a quadrilateral has.

KEY POINT · ANGLES AND QUADRILATERALS

$$\cos \varphi = \frac{\vec{a} \circ \vec{b}}{|\vec{a}| \cdot |\vec{b}|}, \qquad 0° \le \varphi \le 180°$$

Parallelogram: $\vec{AB} = \vec{DC}$   ·   Rectangle: in addition $\vec{AB} \circ \vec{AD} = 0$

Rhombus: parallelogram with $|\vec{AB}| = |\vec{AD}|$ (then also $\vec{AC} \perp \vec{BD}$)   ·   Square: rectangle and rhombus at the same time

Angle in a triangle at $A$: both vectors must start at $A$: $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{AC}$, not $\vec{BA}$.

Calculator: $\varphi = \cos^{-1}(\ldots)$, in degrees (DEG).

EXAMPLE 3 · ANGLE IN A TRIANGLE

Find the angle at $A$ in the triangle $A(1, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 1, 0)$, $C(0, 0, 1)$.

$\vec{AB} = (-1, 1, 0)$, $\vec{AC} = (-1, 0, 1)$, $\cos \alpha = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt2} = \tfrac12$, $\alpha = 60°$. All sides are $\sqrt2$ long: an equilateral triangle.

EXAMPLE 4 · THE RECTANGLE FROM THE EXPLORATION

Check $A(2, -2, 0)$, $B(2, 2, 0)$, $C(-1, 2, 4)$, $D(-1, -2, 4)$.

$\vec{AB} = (0, 4, 0) = \vec{DC}$: parallelogram. $\vec{AD} = (-3, 0, 4)$, $\vec{AB} \circ \vec{AD} = 0$: rectangle. $|\vec{AB}| = 4 \neq 5 = |\vec{AD}|$: not a square.

At what angle is the space diagonal of a cube inclined to an edge? To the diagonal of the base?

Show that $A(2, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 3, 1)$, $C(-2, 0, 2)$, $D(0, -3, 1)$ form a rhombus, but not a square.

Calculate all three angles of the triangle $A(2, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 3, 0)$, $C(0, 0, 4)$ and check the angle sum.

At $A$: $\vec{AB} = (-2, 3, 0)$, $\vec{AC} = (-2, 0, 4)$, $\cos \alpha = \tfrac{4}{\sqrt{13} \cdot \sqrt{20}}$, $\alpha \approx 75.6°$. Likewise $\beta \approx 60.1°$, $\gamma \approx 44.3°$. Sum $180°$. ✓

Prove Thales' theorem with vectors: if $C$ lies on the circle with diameter $AB$ and centre $M$, then $\vec{CA} \perp \vec{CB}$.

Set $\vec{MA} = \vec{r}$, then $\vec{MB} = -\vec{r}$ and $\vec{MC} = \vec{c}$ with $|\vec{c}| = |\vec{r}|$.

$\vec{CA} = \vec{r} - \vec{c}$, $\vec{CB} = -\vec{r} - \vec{c}$, $\vec{CA} \circ \vec{CB} = -\vec{r} \circ \vec{r} + \vec{c} \circ \vec{c} = -|\vec{r}|^2 + |\vec{c}|^2 = 0$.

EXCURSIONTHE ANGLE BETWEEN TWO TEXTS

How search engines compare texts

How does a computer find out whether two texts are about the same topic? An idea from the 1970s: you count how often each word occurs and write the counts as a vector. With only three words (goal, ball, cake) a sports report becomes, say, $(3, 1, 0)$, a second one $(2, 0, 1)$, a recipe $(0, 0, 2)$.

Similar texts point in similar directions. The measure is the cosine of the angle: $\cos \varphi = \tfrac{\vec{a} \circ \vec{b}}{|\vec{a}| \cdot |\vec{b}|}$. The two sports reports come to about 0.85, sports report and recipe to 0: they are perpendicular to each other, they have nothing in common.

Real texts have tens of thousands of words, that is vectors with tens of thousands of components. The scalar product works there in just the same way. This “cosine similarity” is still found today in search engines, recommender systems and language models.

Source: G. Salton, A. Wong, C. S. Yang: A Vector Space Model for Automatic Indexing. Communications of the ACM 18 (11), 1975.
