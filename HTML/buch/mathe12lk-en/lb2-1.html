Limits and continuity

A hot coffee cools down, but never below room temperature. A curve hugs a straight line without ever reaching it. A function has a hole at one point, and yet you know exactly where it wants to go there. Limits describe what happens when you get closer and closer to something. They are the foundation of all of differential calculus.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Behaviour at infinity

For the coffee, $T(60) = 20 + 60 \cdot 0.9^{60} \approx 20.11$. The longer you wait, the smaller $0.9^t$ becomes, and $T(t)$ gets arbitrarily close to 20.

You write $\lim\limits_{t \to \infty} T(t) = 20$ (read: “the limit of $T(t)$ as $t$ tends to infinity is 20”). The line $y = 20$ is called a horizontal asymptote.

KEY POINT · LIMIT AT INFINITY

$\lim\limits_{x \to \infty} f(x) = g$ means: $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to $g$ provided $x$ is large enough. Then $y = g$ is a horizontal asymptote.

Polynomial functions of degree 1 or more behave for $x \to \pm\infty$ like their term with the highest power, they tend to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$.

Rational functions $\tfrac{Z(x)}{N(x)}$: degree of the numerator smaller than degree of the denominator → limit 0. Degrees equal → quotient of the coefficients of the highest power. Degree of the numerator larger → no horizontal asymptote.

Also: $\tfrac1x \to 0$, $\mathrm{e}^{-x} \to 0$ and $q^x \to 0$ for $0 < q < 1$, each for $x \to \infty$.

Trick: divide the numerator and the denominator by the highest power of the denominator. All terms like $\tfrac{1}{x}$ or $\tfrac{3}{x^2}$ vanish for $x \to \infty$.

Asymptote comes from Greek and means “not falling together”.

INTERACTIVE · STEP BY STEP TOWARDS THE LIMIT

Choose a function and the direction of approach. With every step $k$, $x$ goes ten times as far out or ten times as close to the point.

EXAMPLE 1 · EQUAL DEGREES

Find $\lim\limits_{x \to \infty} \dfrac{3x^2 + 1}{x^2 + 2}$.

Divide by $x^2$: $\dfrac{3 + \frac{1}{x^2}}{1 + \frac{2}{x^2}} \to \dfrac{3 + 0}{1 + 0} = 3$. Horizontal asymptote $y = 3$.

EXAMPLE 2 · POLYNOMIAL

How does $f(x) = -2x^3 + 5x^2 + 1$ behave for $x \to \infty$ and for $x \to -\infty$?

What matters is $-2x^3$: for $x \to \infty$, $f(x) \to -\infty$, for $x \to -\infty$, $f(x) \to +\infty$.

UNIT 23 LESSONS

2Limit at a point and continuity

$f(x) = \tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$ is not defined at $x = 1$. But the values $f(0.999) = 1.999$ and $f(1.001) = 2.001$ show: from both sides $f(x)$ approaches the number 2.

For $x \neq 1$, $f(x) = \tfrac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x - 1} = x + 1$. The graph is the line $y = x + 1$ with a hole at $(1, 2)$. It is different with $g(x) = \tfrac{1}{x - 1}$: here the values grow without bound as $x \to 1$, the line $x = 1$ is a vertical asymptote.

KEY POINT · LIMIT AT A POINT AND CONTINUITY

$\lim\limits_{x \to a} f(x) = g$ means: $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to $g$ if $x$ is close enough to $a$, and that from the left and from the right.

Pole: $|f(x)|$ grows without bound as $x \to a$; $x = a$ is a vertical asymptote, with or without a change of sign. Hole: $f(a)$ is not defined, but the limit exists.

$f$ is called continuous at the point $a$ if $f(a)$ is defined and $\lim\limits_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$. Intuitively: you can draw the graph there without lifting the pen. Polynomial functions, $\mathrm{e}^x$ and $\sin x$ are continuous everywhere.

From the left, from the right: if different values come out from the left and from the right, there is no limit at this point, for example with parcel postage.

In the widget above you find a pole ($x \to 1$ for the first function) and two holes.

FIGURE · POLES WITH AND WITHOUT A CHANGE OF SIGN

Gold: $f(x) = \tfrac{1}{x - 2}$ changes from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ at $x = 2$. Cyan: $g(x) = \tfrac{1}{(x - 2)^2}$ goes to $+\infty$ from both sides.

EXAMPLE 3 · HOLE OR POLE?

Investigate $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2}$ and $g(x) = \dfrac{x + 2}{x - 2}$ at the point $x = 2$.

$f(x) = x + 2$ for $x \neq 2$: hole, $\lim\limits_{x \to 2} f(x) = 4$.

For $g$ the numerator tends to 4, the denominator to 0: pole with a change of sign, vertical asymptote $x = 2$.

EXAMPLE 4 · PARCEL POSTAGE

Is the price function of the parcel service continuous at 2 kg?

From the left (just under 2 kg): €5. From the right (just over 2 kg): €7. The one-sided limits are different: no limit, so not continuous. The graph jumps.

UNIT 33 LESSONS

3Points of discontinuity and piecewise defined functions

A taxi costs $4 + 2.2x$ euros up to 5 km, after that $15 + 1.8(x - 5)$ euros. At $x = 5$ both terms give €15: the price does not jump, it only changes its gradient. The parcel postage, on the other hand, jumps from €5 to €7 at 2 kg. The taxi function is continuous, the postage function is not.

KEY POINT · KINDS OF DISCONTINUITY

$f$ is continuous at the point $x_0$ of its domain if the left-hand limit, the right-hand limit and the function value agree. Otherwise $x_0$ is a point of discontinuity:

  • Jump: the one-sided limits exist but are different.
  • Removable discontinuity: the limit exists, but $f(x_0)$ is a different value. If you change $f(x_0)$, $f$ becomes continuous.

At a gap or a pole $f$ is not defined. If $f$ has a limit at a gap, it can be extended continuously.

A piecewise defined function made of continuous pieces is continuous if and only if the pieces fit together at the joins.

Careful: $\tfrac1x$ is a continuous function, because it is continuous at every point of its domain. The point 0 does not belong to it at all.

Intermediate value theorem: if $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have different signs, then $f$ has a zero in between. Without continuity this does not hold, because a jump can lead across zero.

A kink is allowed: $|x|$ is continuous at 0. Whether you can also differentiate there is another question (chapter 2.2).

INTERACTIVE · MAKING IT CONTINUOUS PIECE BY PIECE

Change the parameter until the two pieces fit together. Open circles do not belong to the graph, the white dot is the function value.

EXAMPLE 5 · FINDING THE PARAMETER

For which $a$ is $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ for $x \leq 1$ and $f(x) = ax - 1$ for $x > 1$ continuous?

Left: $\lim\limits_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = f(1) = 2$. Right: $\lim\limits_{x \to 1^+} (ax - 1) = a - 1$.

Continuous if and only if $a - 1 = 2$: $a = 3$. At all other points $f$ is continuous anyway, because both pieces are polynomials.

EXAMPLE 6 · REMOVABLE OR NOT?

Investigate $g(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$ for $x \neq 1$ with $g(1) = 3$ at the point 1.

$\lim\limits_{x \to 1} g(x) = \lim\limits_{x \to 1} (x + 1) = 2 \neq 3 = g(1)$.

Removable discontinuity: with $g(1) = 2$, $g$ would be continuous.

$f(0) = -1 < 0 < 1 = f(1)$, and $f$ is continuous. $f(0.5) = -0.375$: zero in $[0.5, 1]$. $f(0.75) \approx 0.172$: zero in $[0.5, 0.75]$.

A taxi company charges a basic fee of €3 and €2.40 per km for the first 5 km, after that $a$ euros plus €1.60 for every further km. Set up the fare as a piecewise defined function. For which $a$ is it continuous? Why would a company not want a jump downwards?

$p(x) = 3 + 2.4x$ for $0 < x \le 5$ and $p(x) = a + 1.6(x - 5)$ for $x > 5$. At 5 km: $3 + 12 = 15$, continuous for $a = 15$.

With $a < 15$ a slightly longer ride would cost less than a ride of exactly 5 km; passengers would order a detour.

Is $f(x) = x \cdot \sin\tfrac1x$ for $x \neq 0$ and $f(0) = 0$ continuous at the point 0? Justify without a calculator.

How large can $\left|\sin\tfrac1x\right|$ be at most?

Yes. $|f(x)| = |x| \cdot \left|\sin\tfrac1x\right| \leq |x|$, so $f(x)$ lies between $-|x|$ and $|x|$, and both tend to 0. Hence $\lim\limits_{x \to 0} f(x) = 0 = f(0)$. The graph oscillates infinitely often, but is squeezed between the lines $y = x$ and $y = -x$.

The concentration of a drug in the blood is $c(t) = \dfrac{8t}{t^2 + 4}$ ($t$ in hours). Find $\lim\limits_{t \to \infty} c(t)$ and interpret it.

Connecting with Year 11: of 1,000 iodine-131 nuclei, $N(t) = 1000 \cdot 0.5^{t/8}$ are left after $t$ days. Find the limit for $t \to \infty$. When, by calculation, is less than one nucleus left?

Limit 0. $0.5^{t/8} < 0.001 \Rightarrow t > 8 \cdot \tfrac{\ln 0.001}{\ln 0.5} \approx 79.7$ days. For such small numbers, however, the model is only an average.

$f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + x}{x}$ is not defined at 0. How must $f(0)$ be defined so that $f$ is continuous everywhere?

$f(x) = x + 1$ for $x \neq 0$, so $f(0) := 1$. This is called a continuous extension.

EXCURSIONINFINITELY MANY STEPS, A FINITE GOAL

Achilles and the tortoise

In the 5th century BC the Greek philosopher Zeno of Elea claimed: swift Achilles can never catch up with a tortoise that has a head start. For when he arrives where it was, it is already a bit further on. When he arrives there, it is further on again, and so on infinitely often.

With limits the puzzle is solved. If Achilles is twice as fast and the tortoise has a head start of 1 metre, he runs one after the other $1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \ldots$ metres. These infinitely many sections have the limit 2: after 2 metres he has caught up with it.

So infinitely many steps can add up to a finite distance and a finite time. Exactly this idea is behind every limit.

Source: handed down by Aristotle, Physics, book VI, chapter 9 (239b).

LOOKING BACKTHE ESSENTIALS AT A GLANCE

AT INFINITY

$\lim\limits_{x \to \infty} f(x) = g$: horizontal asymptote $y = g$.

$\tfrac{3x^2 + 1}{x^2 + 2} \to 3$ · $20 + 60 \cdot 0.9^t \to 20$

RATIONAL FUNCTIONS

Degree of numerator < degree of denominator: 0 · equal: quotient of coefficients · larger: no horizontal asymptote.

$\tfrac{6x^2}{2x^2 + 5} \to 3$

AT A POINT

The same value from the left and from the right. Pole: vertical asymptote. Hole: the limit exists.

$\tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$: hole at 1, limit 2

CONTINUITY

$\lim\limits_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$: can be drawn without lifting the pen.

Parcel postage: jumps, not continuous

POINTS OF DISCONTINUITY

Jump: one-sided limits differ. Removable: limit $\neq f(x_0)$. Piecewise: make the pieces fit at the joins.

$x^2 + 1$ and $ax - 1$ at 1: continuous for $a = 3$
