The derivative

A speedometer does not show how fast you drove on average but how fast you are driving right now. How can you calculate the speed at a single instant from a distance that depends on time? The answer is one of the greatest ideas in mathematics: you let a secant turn into a tangent. Its gradient is called the derivative.

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Average rate of change: the secant

In the first 3 seconds the stone falls $s(3) = 45$ m, after 1 second it was $s(1) = 5$ m. How fast was it on average between $t = 1$ and $t = 3$?

On average it covered 40 m in 2 seconds: $\tfrac{45 - 5}{3 - 1} = 20$ m/s. Geometrically this is the gradient of the line through the points of the curve $(1, 5)$ and $(3, 45)$. Such a line through two points of a graph is called a secant.

KEY POINT · AVERAGE RATE OF CHANGE

The average rate of change of $f$ on the interval $[a, b]$ is the difference quotient $$\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.$$

It is the gradient of the secant through $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$. Its unit is “unit of $f$ per unit of $x$”, for example m/s or °C per hour.

Delta: you often write $\tfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$, with $\Delta$ (delta) for “difference”.

Year 11: for a straight line the rate of change is the same everywhere, it is its gradient $m$. For curves it depends on the interval.

EXAMPLE 1 · TEMPERATURE

At 8 a.m. it was 12 °C, at 2 p.m. 21 °C. Calculate the average rate of change.

$\tfrac{21 - 12}{14 - 8} = 1.5$ °C per hour. This number does not tell you whether it got warmer evenly.

EXAMPLE 2 · STANDARD PARABOLA

Calculate the average rate of change of $f(x) = x^2$ on $[1, 3]$ and on $[-1, 1]$.

$\tfrac{9 - 1}{2} = 4$ and $\tfrac{1 - 1}{2} = 0$. The second secant is horizontal, although the parabola is not constant there.

$\dfrac{b^2 - a^2}{b - a} = \dfrac{(b - a)(b + a)}{b - a} = a + b$

UNIT 23 LESSONS

2Instantaneous rate of change: from the secant to the tangent

How fast is the stone exactly after 2 seconds? On the interval $[2, 2 + h]$: $\dfrac{s(2 + h) - s(2)}{h} = \dfrac{5(4 + 4h + h^2) - 20}{h} = \dfrac{20h + 5h^2}{h} = 20 + 5h.$

The smaller $h$, the closer this is to 20. For $h \to 0$ you get the instantaneous speed 20 m/s. Geometrically, the second point of the secant moves closer and closer to the first one until the secant becomes the tangent.

KEY POINT · DERIVATIVE

If the limit $$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}$$ exists, $f$ is called differentiable at the point $x_0$. $f'(x_0)$ is called the derivative of $f$ at the point $x_0$ (differential quotient).

$f'(x_0)$ is the instantaneous rate of change and the gradient of the tangent to the graph at the point $(x_0, f(x_0))$.

How to say it: $f'(x_0)$ is read “f dash of x nought” (or “f prime of x nought”).

Tangent: it touches the graph at the point $P$ and has the same direction as the curve there. Further away it may well cut the graph again.

EXERCISES

Use the difference quotient to find $f'(1)$ and $f'(-2)$ for $f(x) = x^2$.

$\tfrac{(1 + h)^2 - 1}{h} = 2 + h \to 2$; $\tfrac{(-2 + h)^2 - 4}{h} = -4 + h \to -4$

Explain without calculating: for $f(x) = 3x + 1$, $f'(x_0) = 3$ at every point.

The graph is a straight line with gradient 3. Every secant, and so every tangent, is the line itself.

Find $f'(2)$ for $f(x) = x^3$.

$(2 + h)^3 = 8 + 12h + 6h^2 + h^3$

$\tfrac{12h + 6h^2 + h^3}{h} = 12 + 6h + h^2 \to 12$

$f(x) = |x|$. Calculate the difference quotient at 0 for $h = 0.1$ and $h = -0.1$. Is $f$ differentiable at 0?

$\tfrac{0.1}{0.1} = 1$ and $\tfrac{0.1}{-0.1} = -1$. From the right 1, from the left $-1$: no limit. The graph has a corner there, $f$ is not differentiable at 0.

Show in general: for $f(x) = x^2$, $f'(x) = 2x$ at every point $x$.

$\dfrac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \dfrac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h \to 2x$

UNIT 32 LESSONS

3The derivative function graphically

For $f(x) = x^2$, $f'(-2) = -4$, $f'(1) = 2$, $f'(3) = 6$. If you plot all these gradients of tangents against $x$, you get a straight line: $f'(x) = 2x$.

If you assign the derivative $f'(x)$ to every point $x$, you get a new function, the derivative function $f'$. You can often sketch its graph from the graph of $f$ alone, without any formula.

KEY POINT · SKETCHING THE DERIVATIVE

Where $f$ is increasing, $f'(x) > 0$, the graph of $f'$ lies above the $x$-axis. Where $f$ is decreasing, $f'(x) < 0$.

Where $f$ has a horizontal tangent (maximum or minimum), $f'$ has a zero.

Where $f$ rises or falls most steeply, $f'$ has a maximum or a minimum.

How to do it: first mark the points with a horizontal tangent, then the sign for each section, finally the steepest points.

4Linear approximation and differentiability

A square plot of land with a side length of 50 m is enlarged all round so that every side is 0.2 m longer. By how much does the area grow? Exactly, it is $50.2^2 - 50^2 = 20.04$ m². The derivative of $A(x) = x^2$ at the point 50 is $A'(50) = 100$, and $100 \cdot 0.2 = 20$ is almost exactly right. Near the point of contact the tangent replaces the curve.

KEY POINT · LINEAR APPROXIMATION

If $f$ is differentiable at the point $x_0$, then the tangent $t(x) = f(x_0) + f'(x_0) \cdot (x - x_0)$ is the best linear approximation of $f$ near $x_0$: $$f(x_0 + h) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0) \cdot h.$$

The error $f(x_0 + h) - t(x_0 + h)$ becomes small faster than $h$ itself for $h \to 0$: even the error divided by $h$ tends to 0.

Differentiable means continuous: if $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $f$ is also continuous there. The converse does not hold: $|x|$ is continuous at 0 but has a kink there.

Close up, every curve is straight: if you zoom in close enough on a differentiable curve, you only see its tangent. At a kink you see a corner even in the smallest section.

Application: engineers and economists often calculate with the tangent instead of the curve for small changes, for example for marginal revenue or the expansion of components.

INTERACTIVE · ZOOMED IN, EVERY CURVE IS STRAIGHT

Make the window smaller. Curve and tangent become indistinguishable, the error shrinks faster than $h$.

EXAMPLE 7 · A ROOT WITHOUT A CALCULATOR

Approximate $\sqrt{4.1}$ with the tangent to $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ at $x_0 = 4$. We have $f'(4) = \tfrac14$, as chapter 2.3 shows.

$\sqrt{4 + h} \approx 2 + \tfrac14 h$, with $h = 0.1$: $\sqrt{4.1} \approx 2.025$.

Exact value $2.024\,846$: error only about $0.000\,15$.

EXAMPLE 8 · JOINING SMOOTHLY

$f(x) = x^2$ for $x \le 1$ and $f(x) = ax + b$ for $x > 1$. Find $a$ and $b$ so that $f$ is differentiable at the point 1.

Differentiable requires continuous: $1^2 = a + b$. The gradients must agree: on the left it is $2 \cdot 1 = 2$, on the right $a$.

$a = 2$, $b = -1$. The line is exactly the tangent to the parabola at $(1, 1)$; the transition has no kink.

EXERCISES

Find the tangent to $f(x) = x^2$ at $x_0 = 3$ and use it to approximate $3.1^2$. How large is the error? Compare it with $h^2$.

$t(x) = 9 + 6(x - 3)$, $t(3.1) = 9.6$. Exactly: $9.61$. The error is $0.01 = 0.1^2 = h^2$.

A cube has an edge length of 10 cm, its volume is $V(a) = a^3$. We have $V'(10) = 300$. By about how much does the volume grow if the edge becomes 1 mm longer? What is the exact increase?

$300 \cdot 0.1 = 30$ cm³. Exactly: $10.1^3 - 1000 = 30.301$ cm³.

For $\mathrm{e}^x$ and $\sin x$ the gradient at 0 is 1 in each case. Approximate linearly and compare with the calculator: a) $\mathrm{e}^{0.1}$   b) $\sin 0.2$. Give the relative errors.

a) $1 + 0.1 = 1.1$ instead of $1.105\,17$, relative error about 0.47%   b) $0.2$ instead of $0.198\,67$, relative error about 0.67%

$f(x) = -x^2 + 4x$ for $x \le 1$ and $f(x) = a(x - 3)^2 + b$ for $x > 1$. Find $a$ and $b$ so that $f$ is differentiable at 1.

The gradient of $a(x - 3)^2$ at the point 1 is $2a \cdot (1 - 3) = -4a$, as for $x^2$, only shifted and stretched.

On the left at 1: $f(1) = 3$, gradient $-2 \cdot 1 + 4 = 2$. On the right: $-4a = 2$, so $a = -\tfrac12$. Continuous: $-\tfrac12 \cdot 4 + b = 3$, so $b = 5$. $f(x) = -\tfrac12 (x - 3)^2 + 5$ for $x > 1$: two parabolic arcs that merge without a kink.

Prove: if $f$ is differentiable at the point $x_0$, then $f$ is continuous there. Explain why the converse is false.

Write $f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0) = h \cdot \dfrac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}$ and let $h \to 0$.

The first factor tends to 0, the second to $f'(x_0)$. So $f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)$ tends to $0 \cdot f'(x_0) = 0$, that is $\lim\limits_{h \to 0} f(x_0 + h) = f(x_0)$: $f$ is continuous.

A counterexample to the converse: $|x|$ is continuous at 0, but the difference quotients have the limit $-1$ on the left and $1$ on the right.

EXCURSIONWHO INVENTED THE DERIVATIVE?

Newton versus Leibniz

Isaac Newton developed his “method of fluxions” from 1665 in Cambridge, but did not publish it for a long time. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz found the same ideas independently and published them in October 1684 in the Leipzig journal Acta Eruditorum under the title “Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis”.

What followed was one of the most bitter disputes in the history of science. In 1712 a report by the Royal Society declared Newton the inventor and accused Leibniz of copying. The report had essentially been written by Newton himself, at that time President of the Royal Society. Today both are regarded as independent discoverers.

It is mainly Leibniz's notation that has caught on, for example $\tfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}$ for the derivative. The dash in $f'(x)$ goes back to Joseph-Louis Lagrange.

Sources: G. W. Leibniz: Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis. Acta Eruditorum, October 1684. Royal Society: Commercium epistolicum, London 1712.
