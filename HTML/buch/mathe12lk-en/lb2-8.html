Optimisation problems

Now differential calculus becomes a tool. A sheet of card is to become a box with the largest possible volume, a can is to make do with as little tin as possible, a lifeguard is to reach a drowning swimmer as quickly as possible. You learn to find the best among many possibilities, not to forget the end points and to distinguish cases when the problem depends on a parameter.

UNIT 17 LESSONS

1Objective function, constraint, domain

From a sheet of card measuring 30 cm × 20 cm, squares with side $x$ are cut out at the corners. For which $x$ does the box hold the most?

The box is $30 - 2x$ long, $20 - 2x$ wide and $x$ high. Its volume is the objective function $V(x) = x(30 - 2x)(20 - 2x) = 4x^3 - 100x^2 + 600x$ with the domain $0 < x < 10$. $V'(x) = 12x^2 - 200x + 600 = 0$ gives $x \approx 3.92$ cm (the second solution 12.74 lies outside). The volume is then about 1,056 cm³.

KEY POINT · SOLVING OPTIMISATION PROBLEMS

1. Objective function: which quantity is to be maximal or minimal? Set up a formula for it, often with two variables.

2. Constraint: what is fixed (perimeter, volume, material)? Use it to replace one variable so that the objective function depends on one only.

3. Domain: fix it from the context.

4. Extremum: find it with $f' = 0$ and the sufficient condition. Compare the values at the end points.

5. Answer in context with a unit, and check the model critically.

Extremum at an end point: if the value with $f' = 0$ lies outside the domain, the optimum can be at an end point.

Without a derivative: if an objective function is too complicated, computers look for the optimum by clever trial and error, for example with particle swarms.

EXAMPLE 1 · RECTANGLE UNDER A PARABOLA

Under the parabola $f(x) = 9 - x^2$ there is a rectangle with one side on the $x$-axis and two corners on the parabola. When is its area largest?

Corners at $\pm u$: width $2u$, height $9 - u^2$. $A(u) = 2u(9 - u^2) = 18u - 2u^3$, $0 < u < 3$.

$A'(u) = 18 - 6u^2 = 0 \Rightarrow u = \sqrt3 \approx 1.73$, $A''(u) = -12u < 0$. $A_{\max} = 12\sqrt3 \approx 20.8$ square units.

EXAMPLE 2 · THE CAN

A can is to hold 1 litre. Which dimensions need the least tin?

Constraint $\pi r^2 h = 1000 \Rightarrow h = \tfrac{1000}{\pi r^2}$. Objective function $S(r) = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h = 2\pi r^2 + \tfrac{2000}{r}$.

$S'(r) = 4\pi r - \tfrac{2000}{r^2} = 0 \Rightarrow r^3 = \tfrac{500}{\pi}$, $r \approx 5.42$ cm, $h \approx 10.84$ cm. The most economical can is as tall as it is wide.

EXERCISES

Two numbers add up to 20. When is their product largest?

$P(x) = x(20 - x)$, $P'(x) = 20 - 2x = 0$: $x = 10$, both numbers 10, product 100.

With 60 m of fencing a rectangle is marked out against a wall (the wall as one side). Find the largest area.

$A(x) = x(60 - 2x)$, $A'(x) = 60 - 4x = 0$: $x = 15$ m, side along the wall 30 m, $A = 450$ m².

As in exercise 2, but the wall is only 20 m long. What changes?

Now $60 - 2x \le 20$, so $x \ge 20$. On $[20, 30)$, $A$ decreases, the optimum lies at the end point $x = 20$: $A = 20 \cdot 20 = 400$ m².

The profit of a business is $G(x) = -x^3 + 6x^2 + 15x - 20$ (in €1,000, $x$ in hundreds of units, $0 \le x \le 8$). At which quantity is it largest?

$G'(x) = -3x^2 + 12x + 15 = 0 \Rightarrow x^2 - 4x - 5 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 5$. $G''(5) = -18 < 0$. $G(5) = 80$, so €80,000 at 500 units. End points: $G(0) = -20$, $G(8) = -28$.

A window consists of a rectangle with a semicircle on top. The perimeter is 6 m. For which radius does it let through the most light?

Width $2r$, height of the rectangle $h$. Perimeter $2r + 2h + \pi r = 6$. Area $A = 2rh + \tfrac{\pi}{2} r^2$.

$h = 3 - r - \tfrac{\pi}{2} r$, $A(r) = 6r - 2r^2 - \tfrac{\pi}{2} r^2$, $A'(r) = 6 - (4 + \pi) r = 0$: $r = \tfrac{6}{4 + \pi} \approx 0.84$ m. Then $h \approx 0.84$ m too: the height of the rectangle equals the radius.

UNIT 25 LESSONS

2Distances, paths and parameters

The lifeguard’s time depends on the point $x$ at which she goes into the water. If she takes the straight route, she needs 21.2 s; if she runs until she is opposite the swimmer, 19.7 s. In between there is a best point. The objective function now contains roots, its derivative needs the chain rule from chapter 2.4, and the equation $T'(x) = 0$ is best solved with the CAS.

KEY POINT · SOLVING OPTIMISATION PROBLEMS
  1. Name the quantity that is to become extremal and write it down as a term, often with several variables at first.
  2. Use the constraint to replace all variables but one: this gives the objective function.
  3. Give the sensible domain.
  4. Find the local extrema with the derivative and compare them with the values at the ends: you are looking for the global extremum.
  5. Answer the question in context.
KEY POINT · A PARAMETER IN THE PROBLEM

If the problem depends on a quantity $m$, such as the length of a wall, the optimum can lie inside or at the end point, depending on $m$. Then you distinguish the cases and give the result for each case.

Trick: instead of a distance $d = \sqrt{\ldots}$ you minimise $d^2$. The square root is strictly increasing, so both have their minimum at the same point, but $d^2$ is easier to differentiate.

With the CAS: if the equation $Z'(x) = 0$ is hard, you solve it numerically or read off the minimum from the graph. Justifying that it is the global optimum is still your job.

EXAMPLE 3 · THE SHORTEST DISTANCE

Which point of the parabola $y = x^2$ is closest to the point $P(3, 0)$? How large is the distance?

A point on the parabola is $Q(x, x^2)$. Objective function: $d^2(x) = (x - 3)^2 + x^4$, $D = \mathbb{R}$.

$\left(d^2\right)'(x) = 2(x - 3) + 4x^3 = 0 \iff 2x^3 + x - 3 = 0$. Guess: $x = 1$. Polynomial division: $(x - 1)(2x^2 + 2x + 3)$, and the second factor has no zero.

$\left(d^2\right)'$ changes from $-$ to $+$ at 1: $Q(1, 1)$, distance $d = \sqrt{4 + 1} = \sqrt5 \approx 2.24$. For $x \to \pm\infty$, $d^2$ grows beyond all bounds, so the minimum is global.

EXAMPLE 4 · THE LIFEGUARD

She is standing at $A(0, 30)$, the swimmer is at $B(50, -20)$, and the water line is the $x$-axis. She runs at 6 m/s and swims at 2 m/s. Where should she go into the water?

Running distance $\sqrt{30^2 + x^2}$, swimming distance $\sqrt{20^2 + (50 - x)^2}$. Objective function $T(x) = \tfrac{\sqrt{900 + x^2}}{6} + \tfrac{\sqrt{400 + (50 - x)^2}}{2}$ on $D = [0, 50]$.

The CAS solves $T'(x) = 0$: $x \approx 44.3$, $T \approx 19.3$ s. Values at the ends: $T(0) \approx 31.9$ s, $T(50) \approx 19.7$ s.

She should go into the water about 44 m along the shore and saves almost 2 s compared with the straight route.

EXERCISES

A rectangular garden is to have an area of 400 m². For which dimensions do you need the least fence?

$xy = 400$, $U(x) = 2x + \tfrac{800}{x}$ for $x > 0$. $U'(x) = 2 - \tfrac{800}{x^2} = 0$ at $x = 20$: a square with sides of 20 m, 80 m of fence. For $x \to 0$ and $x \to \infty$, $U$ grows beyond all bounds, so the minimum is global.

A rectangle in the first quadrant has one corner at the origin and the opposite corner on the graph of $f(x) = \mathrm{e}^{-x}$. When is its area largest?

$A(u) = u\,\mathrm{e}^{-u}$ for $u > 0$. $A'(u) = (1 - u)\,\mathrm{e}^{-u} = 0$ at $u = 1$, change of sign from $+$ to $-$: $A = \tfrac1{\mathrm{e}} \approx 0.368$ (compare chapter 2.5, example 7).

With 60 m of fencing a rectangle is marked out against a wall of length $m$; the wall replaces one side. Find the largest area in terms of $m$. Compare with exercises 2 and 3.

Call the side along the wall $y$. Then $0 < y \le m$, and $2x + y = 60$.

$A(y) = \tfrac{y(60 - y)}{2}$, $A'(y) = 30 - y = 0$ at $y = 30$.

Case $m \ge 30$: $y = 30$, $x = 15$, $A = 450$ m². Case $m < 30$: $A$ increases on $(0, m]$, the maximum is at the end point $y = m$: $A = \tfrac{m(60 - m)}{2}$, so 400 m² for $m = 20$.

A cylinder is placed in a cone with radius 6 cm and height 12 cm so that the circle at its top touches the curved surface of the cone. How large can the cylinder be at most? What fraction of the cone is that?

Intercept theorem: $\tfrac{12 - h}{r} = \tfrac{12}{6}$, so $h = 12 - 2r$. $V(r) = \pi r^2 (12 - 2r)$ on $[0, 6]$. $V'(r) = \pi(24r - 6r^2) = 0$ at $r = 4$, $h = 4$: $V = 64\pi \approx 201$ cm³. At the ends $V = 0$. The cone holds $144\pi$ cm³, and the cylinder fills $\tfrac49$ of it.

A rectangle stands symmetrically on the $x$-axis under the graph of $f_a(x) = a - x^2$ ($a > 0$). Find the largest area in terms of $a$ and check your result with example 1.

$A(u) = 2u(a - u^2)$ on $(0, \sqrt{a})$, $A'(u) = 2a - 6u^2 = 0 \iff u = \sqrt{\tfrac{a}{3}}$. Largest area $A = 2\sqrt{\tfrac{a}{3}} \cdot \tfrac{2a}{3} = \tfrac{4a}{3}\sqrt{\tfrac{a}{3}}$.

For $a = 9$: $u = \sqrt3$, $A = 12\sqrt3 \approx 20.8$, as in example 1.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

Solve the exploration “The box” for an A4 sheet (29.7 cm × 21 cm). Which $x$ is optimal?

$V(x) = x(29.7 - 2x)(21 - 2x)$, $V'(x) = 12x^2 - 202.8x + 623.7 = 0$. Solution in $0 < x < 10.5$: $x \approx 4.04$ cm, $V \approx 1128$ cm³.

Connecting with chapter 2.5: explain why the box really has a maximum, without the second derivative.

At the end points $x = 0$ and $x = 10$, $V = 0$, in between $V > 0$. The only point inside with $V' = 0$ must be the maximum.

Criticising the model of the can: real tin cans are often taller than the calculation $h = 2r$ suggests. Give reasons why the model only partly matches reality.

For example: the lid and the base are thicker than the side or have a seam that costs material. Cans have to fit on shelves, in your hand and on machines. The price of the tin is only part of the costs. The label and stacking also play a role.

Connecting with physics: show in general for the lifeguard: from $T'(x) = 0$ it follows that $\tfrac{\sin\alpha}{v_1} = \tfrac{\sin\beta}{v_2}$, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the angles of the two paths to the normal to the water line.

With $A(0, a)$, $B(b, -c)$: $T(x) = \tfrac{\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}}{v_1} + \tfrac{\sqrt{c^2 + (b - x)^2}}{v_2}$. With the chain rule: $T'(x) = \tfrac{x}{v_1\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}} - \tfrac{b - x}{v_2\sqrt{c^2 + (b - x)^2}}$. The fractions $\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}}$ and $\tfrac{b - x}{\sqrt{c^2 + (b - x)^2}}$ are exactly $\sin\alpha$ and $\sin\beta$. This is the law of refraction of light.

A beam with a rectangular cross-section is cut from a tree trunk with a circular cross-section (diameter 40 cm). Its strength is proportional to $b \cdot h^2$. For which dimensions does it carry the most?

$b^2 + h^2 = 40^2$, so $T(b) = b(1600 - b^2)$ on $(0, 40)$. $T'(b) = 1600 - 3b^2 = 0$: $b = \tfrac{40}{\sqrt3} \approx 23.1$ cm, $h = 40\sqrt{\tfrac23} \approx 32.7$ cm. The ratio is $h : b = \sqrt2$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY AND PHYSICS

Light takes the fastest route

A stick in water looks bent, because light changes its direction at the surface of the water. Willebrord Snellius and René Descartes had found the law for this: the ratio of the sines of the two angles to the normal is fixed. But why the sines, of all things?

Pierre de Fermat answered this in 1662 in two papers that he enclosed with a letter to the physician Marin Cureau de la Chambre. His assumption: nature does not choose the shortest route, but the fastest one, and light is slower in water than in air. With his method for maxima and minima he worked through exactly the lifeguard’s problem and, to his own surprise, obtained the sine law of refraction (exercise 14).

The followers of Descartes held this against him, because Descartes had assumed that light was faster in water. Fermat was right: in 1850 Léon Foucault measured that light is slower in water.

Source: Pierre de Fermat: “Analysis ad refractiones” and “Synthesis ad refractiones”, enclosures to a letter to Marin Cureau de la Chambre, 1662.
