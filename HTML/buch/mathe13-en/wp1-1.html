Lines and planes

A laser beam, the flight path of a drone, a roof surface, a pane of glass: in space, lines and planes are described with vectors. A point to start from and one or two directions are enough. With the cross product you also find the vector that is perpendicular to a plane. Everything can be turned in real 3D.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Lines: parametric equation and point test

After $t$ seconds the drone is at the point $X$ with $\vec{OX} = (1, 2, 1) + t \cdot (1, -1, 2)$. For all real $t$ together, negative ones included, you get a line.

Every point of the line is reached from the point of support by adding a multiple of the direction vector. Each point belongs to exactly one value of the parameter $t$.

KEY POINT · LINE IN SPACE

Parametric equation: $g\colon \vec{x} = \vec{p} + t \cdot \vec{u}$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$, with position vector $\vec{p}$ and direction vector $\vec{u} \neq \vec{0}$.

Line through $A$ and $B$: $\vec{x} = \vec{OA} + t \cdot \vec{AB}$.

Point test: $Q$ lies on $g$ if $\vec{OQ} = \vec{p} + t \cdot \vec{u}$ for one $t$, that is all three rows give the same $t$.

Trace points are the points of intersection with the coordinate planes: for example $z = 0$ for the $xy$-plane.

Not unique: any point of the line can be the point of support, any multiple ($\neq 0$) of $\vec{u}$ the direction vector.

Line segment: if you restrict $t$ to $0 \le t \le 1$, you get the line segment from $A$ to $B$.

INTERACTIVE · LINE, POINT TEST, TRACE POINTS

Change the point of support $P$, the direction vector $\vec{u}$ and the test point $Q$. With $t$ the golden point $X$ moves along the line. Red: trace points.

EXAMPLE 1 · POINT TEST

Do $Q(3, 0, 5)$ and $R(4, 0, 7)$ lie on $g\colon \vec{x} = (1, 2, 1) + t \cdot (1, -1, 2)$?

$Q$: $1 + t = 3$, $2 - t = 0$, $1 + 2t = 5$, each time $t = 2$: $Q \in g$.

$R$: $1 + t = 4 \Rightarrow t = 3$, but $2 - t = 0 \Rightarrow t = 2$. Contradiction: $R \notin g$.

EXAMPLE 2 · TRACE POINTS

Find the trace points of the line $g$ from example 1.

$xy$-plane: $1 + 2t = 0$, $t = -\tfrac12$, $S_{xy}(0.5, 2.5, 0)$. $xz$-plane: $2 - t = 0$, $S_{xz}(3, 0, 5)$. $yz$-plane: $1 + t = 0$, $S_{yz}(0, 3, -1)$.

Does $P(5, 1, 7)$ lie on $g\colon \vec{x} = (1, 3, -1) + t \cdot (2, -1, 4)$?

For which $a$ does $Q(a, 4, 0)$ lie on the line through $A(1, 2, 2)$ and $B(2, 3, 1)$?

$\vec{x} = (1, 2, 2) + t \cdot (1, 1, -1)$. $z$: $2 - t = 0 \Rightarrow t = 2$, $y$: $2 + 2 = 4$ ✓, so $a = 1 + 2 = 3$.

A plane flies from $A(0, 0, 1)$ in a straight line in the direction $(10, 5, 0.5)$ (units in km). A mountain summit is at $G(30, 15, 2.4)$. Does it fly over it?

Above $G$, $t = 3$ (from $x$ and $y$), the flying height there is $1 + 1.5 = 2.5$ km. The plane flies 100 m above the summit.

2Planes in parametric form and the normal vector

The pane of glass contains $A(4, 0, 0)$. From there you reach every point of it by adding multiples of $\vec{AB} = (-4, 3, 0)$ and $\vec{AC} = (-4, 0, 2)$.

So a plane needs a point of support and two directions that are not parallel. Instead of two directions you can also give a single vector that is perpendicular to the plane: the normal vector. The cross product supplies it.

KEY POINT · PLANE AND CROSS PRODUCT

Parametric form: $E\colon \vec{x} = \vec{a} + r \cdot \vec{u} + s \cdot \vec{v}$ with non-parallel direction vectors $\vec{u}$, $\vec{v}$. Through three points: $\vec{x} = \vec{OA} + r\,\vec{AB} + s\,\vec{AC}$.

Cross product: $$\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2 \\ u_3 v_1 - u_1 v_3 \\ u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1 \end{pmatrix}$$ It is perpendicular to $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$, so it is a normal vector of the plane. Its magnitude is the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$.

Memory aid: for the first row cover up the first row and calculate “crosswise”, for the second the second row, for the third the third. The middle row gets the opposite sign.

Check: $\vec{n} \circ \vec{u} = 0$ and $\vec{n} \circ \vec{v} = 0$.

INTERACTIVE · PLANE AND NORMAL VECTOR

Enter the point of support and the direction vectors. The pink arrow is the normal vector. If the plane cuts all three axes, you see the trace triangle.

EXAMPLE 3 · CROSS PRODUCT

Calculate $\vec{u} \times \vec{v}$ for $\vec{u} = (-4, 3, 0)$ and $\vec{v} = (-4, 0, 2)$ and check.

$\begin{pmatrix} 3 \cdot 2 - 0 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot (-4) - (-4) \cdot 2 \\ (-4) \cdot 0 - 3 \cdot (-4) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix}$, simpler $\vec{n} = (3, 4, 6)$. Check: $-12 + 12 + 0 = 0$, $-12 + 0 + 12 = 0$ ✓

EXAMPLE 4 · AREA OF A TRIANGLE

How large is the triangle with the corners $(4, 0, 0)$, $(0, 3, 0)$, $(0, 0, 2)$?

Half the parallelogram: $\tfrac12 |(6, 8, 12)| = \tfrac12 \sqrt{36 + 64 + 144} = \tfrac12 \sqrt{244} \approx 7.81$.

Give a parametric equation of the plane through $A(1, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 1, 0)$, $C(0, 0, 1)$.

$\vec{x} = (1, 0, 0) + r \cdot (-1, 1, 0) + s \cdot (-1, 0, 1)$

Calculate $(1, 0, 0) \times (0, 1, 0)$ and $(1, 1, 0) \times (0, 1, 1)$. Compare with the exploration.

$(0, 0, 1)$ and $(1, -1, 1)$. Check: $(1, -1, 1) \circ (1, 1, 0) = 0$, $(1, -1, 1) \circ (0, 1, 1) = 0$ ✓

Find a normal vector of the plane from exercise 5 and the area of the triangle $ABC$.

$(-1, 1, 0) \times (-1, 0, 1) = (1, 1, 1)$. Area $\tfrac12 \sqrt3 \approx 0.87$.

Show: $\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = -(\vec{v} \times \vec{u})$, and $\vec{u} \times \vec{u} = \vec{0}$. What does the second mean geometrically?

Swapping changes both products in every row, that is the sign. $\vec{u} \times \vec{u}$: every row is $u_i u_j - u_j u_i = 0$. Parallel vectors do not span a parallelogram, the area is 0.

Give equations of the three coordinate axes as lines.

$x$-axis: $\vec{x} = t \cdot (1, 0, 0)$, $y$-axis: $t \cdot (0, 1, 0)$, $z$-axis: $t \cdot (0, 0, 1)$.

EXCURSIONGEOMETRY BECOMES CALCULATION

René Descartes and analytic geometry

Until the 17th century geometry and calculation were two separate worlds. In 1637 the French philosopher René Descartes published, as an appendix to his “Discours de la méthode”, the text “La Géométrie”. In it he translated geometric problems into equations: points became numbers, curves became formulas.

This gave rise to analytic geometry, which you learn in this elective. The coordinate systems that we call “Cartesian” bear his Latinised name Cartesius. Incidentally, Pierre de Fermat, whom you know from chapter 2.4 of Year 12, had very similar ideas at the same time.

Source: René Descartes: La Géométrie. Appendix to the Discours de la méthode, Leiden 1637.
