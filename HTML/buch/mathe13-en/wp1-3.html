UNIT 12 LESSONS

1Angles of intersection of lines and planes

You already know the angle between two lines: it is the angle between their direction vectors. For planes you take the normal vectors, because they turn along with the planes.

For a line and a plane it is a little different: the direction vector forms the angle $90° - \varphi$ with the normal vector. That is why the sine appears there instead of the cosine. Angles of intersection always lie between 0° and 90°, so you calculate with absolute values.

KEY POINT · ANGLES OF INTERSECTION

Line–line: $\cos \varphi = \dfrac{|\vec{u} \circ \vec{v}|}{|\vec{u}| \cdot |\vec{v}|}$

Line–plane: $\sin \varphi = \dfrac{|\vec{n} \circ \vec{u}|}{|\vec{n}| \cdot |\vec{u}|}$

Plane–plane: $\cos \varphi = \dfrac{|\vec{n}_1 \circ \vec{n}_2|}{|\vec{n}_1| \cdot |\vec{n}_2|}$

Perpendicular: a line perpendicular to a plane means $\vec{u} \parallel \vec{n}$, planes perpendicular to each other means $\vec{n}_1 \perp \vec{n}_2$.

In the widget from chapter E.2 (“A line intersects a plane”) the angle of intersection is shown as well.

EXAMPLE 1 · LINE AND PLANE

At what angle does the laser $\vec{x} = t\,(1, 2, 3)$ hit the wall $x + y + z = 6$?

$\sin \varphi = \dfrac{|1 + 2 + 3|}{\sqrt3 \cdot \sqrt{14}} = \dfrac{6}{\sqrt{42}} \approx 0.926$, $\varphi \approx 67.8°$.

EXAMPLE 2 · TWO PLANES

What angle do $x + y + z = 6$ and the $xy$-plane $z = 0$ form?

$\vec{n}_1 = (1, 1, 1)$, $\vec{n}_2 = (0, 0, 1)$: $\cos \varphi = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$, $\varphi \approx 54.7°$.

EXERCISES

Calculate the angle of intersection of the lines with the direction vectors $(1, 2, 2)$ and $(2, 1, 2)$.

$\cos \varphi = \tfrac{8}{9}$, $\varphi \approx 27.3°$.

At what angle does a line with direction $(1, 0, 1)$ cut the $xy$-plane?

$\sin \varphi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, $\varphi = 45°$.

Calculate the angle between $E_1\colon 2x + y + 2z = 6$ and $E_2\colon x - 2y + 2z = 1$.

$\cos \varphi = \tfrac{|2 - 2 + 4|}{3 \cdot 3} = \tfrac49$, $\varphi \approx 63.6°$.

A roof surface passes through the ridge points $(0, 0, 6)$, $(0, 10, 6)$ and the eaves point $(4, 0, 3)$. How steep is the roof (angle to the horizontal)?

Direction vectors $(0, 10, 0)$ and $(4, 0, -3)$, $\vec{n} = (-30, 0, -40) \parallel (3, 0, 4)$. With $z = 0$: $\cos \varphi = \tfrac{4}{5}$, $\varphi \approx 36.9°$.

2Surface areas and volumes of prisms and pyramids

The glass pyramid has the base $A(0, 0, 0)$, $B(4, 0, 0)$, $C(4, 4, 0)$, $D(0, 4, 0)$ and the apex $S(2, 2, 4)$.

Every side face is a triangle. Its area is half the parallelogram spanned by two edge vectors, that is $\tfrac12 |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|$. For the volume you only need the base area and the height, as you know from lower secondary school.

KEY POINT · AREAS AND VOLUMES WITH VECTORS

Parallelogram spanned by $\vec{a}$, $\vec{b}$: $A = |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|$ · Triangle: $A = \tfrac12 |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|$

Surface area = sum of all faces. Prism: 2 · base area + lateral surface (parallelograms). Pyramid: base area + lateral surface (triangles).

Volume: prism $V = G \cdot h$, pyramid $V = \tfrac13 G \cdot h$. The height $h$ is the perpendicular distance of the apex (or the top face) from the base.

Cavalieri: an oblique prism has the same volume as a right prism with the same base area and height. The surface area, however, does not.

INTERACTIVE · PYRAMID AND PRISM

Change the base edge and the height and move the apex. Watch what happens to the volume and the lateral surface.

EXAMPLE 3 · THE GLASS PYRAMID

Calculate the lateral surface, the surface area and the volume of the pyramid with $S(2, 2, 4)$.

$\vec{AB} \times \vec{AS} = (4, 0, 0) \times (2, 2, 4) = (0, -16, 8)$, triangle $\tfrac12 \sqrt{320} \approx 8.94$. Lateral surface $4 \cdot 8.94 \approx 35.8$ m². Surface area $\approx 51.8$ m², volume $\tfrac13 \cdot 16 \cdot 4 \approx 21.3$ m³.

EXAMPLE 4 · THE TRIANGULAR PYRAMID

Calculate the volume of the pyramid with $A(0, 0, 0)$, $B(4, 0, 0)$, $C(0, 4, 0)$, $D(0, 0, 4)$.

Base $ABC$ in the $xy$-plane: $\tfrac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 = 8$, height 4. $V = \tfrac13 \cdot 8 \cdot 4 = \tfrac{32}{3} \approx 10.7$.

EXERCISES

Calculate the area of the triangle $A(1, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 2, 0)$, $C(0, 0, 2)$.

$\vec{AB} = (-1, 2, 0)$, $\vec{AC} = (-1, 0, 2)$, cross product $(4, 2, 2)$, area $\tfrac12 \sqrt{24} \approx 2.45$.

A prism has the base $A(0, 0, 0)$, $B(3, 0, 0)$, $C(0, 4, 0)$ and the top face shifted by $(0, 0, 5)$. Calculate the volume and the surface area.

$G = 6$, $V = 30$. Lateral surface: rectangles $3 \cdot 5$, $4 \cdot 5$, $5 \cdot 5$ (the edge $BC$ is 5 long) $= 60$. $S = 2 \cdot 6 + 60 = 72$.

Show with the widget and explain: if you move the apex of a pyramid parallel to the base, the volume stays the same and the lateral surface gets larger.

The base area and the height do not change, so neither does $V = \tfrac13 G h$. The side faces become more slanted, some triangles clearly larger: the lateral surface is minimal when the apex is above the centre.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

At what angle does a side face of the glass pyramid stand to the base?

Normal vector of the face $ABS$: $(0, -16, 8) \parallel (0, -2, 1)$, base $(0, 0, 1)$: $\cos \varphi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}$, $\varphi \approx 63.4°$.

Connecting with chapter 3.2: use the principle “area times thickness” to explain why a pyramid has $\tfrac13$ of the prism. A cross-section at height $z$ has the area $G \cdot \left(1 - \tfrac{z}{h}\right)^2$.

$V = \int_0^h G\left(1 - \tfrac{z}{h}\right)^2 \mathrm{d}z = G \cdot \Big[-\tfrac{h}{3}\left(1 - \tfrac{z}{h}\right)^3\Big]_0^h = \tfrac13 G h$.

EXCURSIONVOLUME 3,800 YEARS AGO

The Moscow Papyrus

In the Pushkin Museum in Moscow there is an Egyptian papyrus from around 1850 BC. Problem 14 calculates the volume of a frustum of a pyramid: a pyramid whose top has been cut off horizontally. At the bottom the square is 4 cubits wide, at the top 2 cubits, the height is 6 cubits.

The scribe calculates: $\tfrac63 \cdot (4^2 + 4 \cdot 2 + 2^2) = 2 \cdot 28 = 56$. That is exactly today's formula $V = \tfrac{h}{3}\left(a^2 + ab + b^2\right)$. How the Egyptians arrived at it, we do not know. With the integral from chapter 3.2 you can prove it today.

Source: Moscow Mathematical Papyrus, problem 14 (around 1850 BC), Pushkin Museum, Moscow.
