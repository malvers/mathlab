Significance tests

A coin shows heads 33 times in 50 tosses. Is it biased, or was it chance? In a study a drug cures 72 out of 100 patients, the old one only 60%. Does it really work better? A significance test makes such decisions by a fixed rule and tells you exactly how often you will be wrong.

UNIT 15 LESSONS

1One-tailed significance tests

A baker says: at most $10\%$ of my rolls are too small. A customer suspects more and weighs 50 rolls. Even if the baker is right, there may be more than 5 rolls that are too small, by chance. From how many on may she contradict him without being wrong too often? She has to fix the rule for this before she weighs.

You test the null hypothesis $H_0\colon p = 0.1$ against the suspicion $H_1\colon p > 0.1$. The test statistic $X$ counts the rolls that are too small. If $H_0$ is true, $X$ is binomially distributed with $n = 50$ and $p = 0.1$. Large values of $X$ speak against $H_0$: the rejection region lies on the right. You choose it so that, if $H_0$ is true, it is hit with a probability of at most $\alpha$, the significance level.

A test can go wrong in two ways: $H_0$ is rejected although it is true (type I error, at most $\alpha$), or $H_0$ is kept although it is false (type II error).

KEY POINT · ONE-TAILED SIGNIFICANCE TEST

1. Fix $H_0\colon p = p_0$ and the direction. 2. Test statistic $X$, under $H_0$ binomially distributed with $n$ and $p_0$. 3. Choose the significance level $\alpha$.

4. Right-tailed: $K = \{k, \ldots, n\}$ with the smallest $k$ for which $P(X \ge k) \le \alpha$. Left-tailed: $K = \{0, \ldots, k\}$ with the largest $k$ for which $P(X \le k) \le \alpha$. $k$ is called the critical value.

5. Decision rule: if the sample result lies in $K$, $H_0$ is rejected, otherwise not.

Not rejected does not mean proved: if the result is not in $K$, the data are simply not enough to refute $H_0$.

On the calculator: many devices only know $P(X \le k)$. Then $P(X \ge k) = 1 - P(X \le k - 1)$.

INTERACTIVE · THE SIGNIFICANCE TEST

Set $n$, $p_0$, the direction and $\alpha$. Red is the rejection region. Switch on the type II error and change the true $p$: how often does the test miss that $H_0$ is false?

EXAMPLE 1 · RIGHT-TAILED: THE BAKER

$n = 50$, $H_0\colon p = 0.1$, $H_1\colon p > 0.1$, $\alpha = 5\%$. We have $P(X \ge 9) \approx 0.058$ and $P(X \ge 10) \approx 0.025$. The customer finds 9 rolls that are too small.

$k = 9$ is too small ($0.058 > 0.05$), $k = 10$ works: $K = \{10, \ldots, 50\}$.

9 is not in $K$: $H_0$ is not rejected. The data are not enough to contradict the baker.

EXAMPLE 2 · LEFT-TAILED: THE SEEDS

A dealer promises that at least $90\%$ of the seeds germinate. A gardener sows 100 seeds. $H_0\colon p = 0.9$, $H_1\colon p < 0.9$, $\alpha = 5\%$, with $P(X \le 84) \approx 0.040$ and $P(X \le 85) \approx 0.073$.

The rejection region lies on the left: the largest $k$ with $P(X \le k) \le 0.05$ is 84.

$K = \{0, \ldots, 84\}$: if at most 84 seeds germinate, the gardener contradicts the dealer.

2Two-tailed tests and statistical certainty

Is a coin fair? Too many tails are suspicious, but so are too few. The rejection region therefore consists of two parts, one on the left and one on the right. So that the test as a whole is wrong with a probability of at most $\alpha$, each side gets at most $\tfrac{\alpha}{2}$.

If $H_0$ is rejected, you say: the result is significant at the level $\alpha$. That only means: if $H_0$ were true, such an extreme result would rarely occur. It does not mean that $H_0$ is false with probability $1 - \alpha$, nor that the difference is large or important. How well a test detects a false hypothesis depends on the true $p$ and on the sample size.

KEY POINT · TWO-TAILED TEST

$H_0\colon p = p_0$, $H_1\colon p \neq p_0$. Rejection region $K = \{0, \ldots, k_l\} \cup \{k_r, \ldots, n\}$ with

the largest $k_l$ for which $P(X \le k_l) \le \tfrac{\alpha}{2}$, and the smallest $k_r$ for which $P(X \ge k_r) \le \tfrac{\alpha}{2}$.

Significant means: if $H_0$ were true, the result would have landed in $K$ with a probability of at most $\alpha$.

The direction matters: if you know beforehand in which direction you are looking, you test one-tailed and detect deviations in this direction more easily.

With the CAS: call up the table of the cumulative binomial distribution and read off the limits $k_l$ and $k_r$; the widget below calculates the same.

INTERACTIVE · CUMULATIVE BINOMIAL DISTRIBUTION

Set $n = 100$ and $p = 0.5$ and choose $P(X \le k)$. Look for the largest $k$ for which the probability is at most 0.025.

EXAMPLE 3 · IS THE COIN FAIR?

$n = 100$, $H_0\colon p = 0.5$, $H_1\colon p \neq 0.5$, $\alpha = 5\%$. We have $P(X \le 39) \approx 0.018$ and $P(X \le 40) \approx 0.028$.

Left: $k_l = 39$. Because of the symmetry, on the right $k_r = 61$.

$K = \{0, \ldots, 39\} \cup \{61, \ldots, 100\}$. The type I error is even only $2 \cdot 0.0176 \approx 3.5\%$.

EXAMPLE 4 · IF THE COIN IS BIASED

In truth the coin shows tails with $p = 0.6$. How likely is it that the test does not detect this?

Type II error: $P(40 \le X \le 60)$ for $p = 0.6$ $\approx 0.54$.

In more than half of the cases the coin goes undetected. For $p = 0.7$ it is only about $2\%$.

EXERCISES

Explain why the fairness of a coin is tested two-tailed, but the effectiveness of a new medicine one-tailed.

With the coin, deviations in both directions are suspicious. With the medicine, all that matters is whether it is better.

$n = 50$, $H_0\colon p = 0.5$, two-tailed, $\alpha = 5\%$. We have $P(X \le 17) \approx 0.016$ and $P(X \le 18) \approx 0.033$. Find $K$.

$k_l = 17$, by symmetry $k_r = 33$: $K = \{0, \ldots, 17\} \cup \{33, \ldots, 50\}$

A dice is rolled 120 times and tested two-tailed for the probability of a six, $\alpha = 5\%$. We have $P(X \le 11) \approx 0.014$, $P(X \le 12) \approx 0.028$, $P(X \ge 28) \approx 0.037$, $P(X \ge 29) \approx 0.022$. Find $K$. What is the decision with 27 sixes?

$K = \{0, \ldots, 11\} \cup \{29, \ldots, 120\}$. $27 \notin K$: $H_0$ is not rejected.

True or false? a) The result is significant, so $H_0$ is false with a probability of $95\%$. b) If $H_0$ is not rejected, it is proved. c) A smaller $\alpha$ makes the type II error larger.

a) false: $\alpha$ describes the behaviour of the test if $H_0$ is true, not a probability for $H_0$. b) false. c) true, the rejection region gets smaller.

A coin in truth has $p = 0.55$. In the two-tailed test with $\alpha = 5\%$ the type II error is about 0.86 for $n = 100$ and about 0.52 for $n = 400$. Interpret the numbers. What follows for planning a test?

Small deviations almost always go undetected with 100 tosses, and even with 400 in every second case. If you want to find small differences, you need very large samples; this has to be planned before the test.

Connecting with Year 12: use Bernoulli's formula to calculate $P(X \ge 9)$ for $n = 10$ and $p = 0.5$. Would you consider a coin biased with 9 hits out of 10 ($\alpha = 5\%$, right-tailed)?

$P(X = 9) + P(X = 10) = \tfrac{10 + 1}{1024} \approx 0.011 \le 0.05$: yes, significant at the $5\%$ level.

Explain why the rejection region has to be fixed before the sample is drawn.

If you only set the limit after seeing the result, you can place it so that the verdict you want comes out. Then the statement about the type I error no longer holds.

A party used to have $40\%$. After a scandal, 200 people are asked. Plan a test with which the party can check at $\alpha = 5\%$ whether its share has fallen. For $n = 200$, $p = 0.4$ the CAS gives $P(X \le 68) \approx 0.048$ and $P(X \le 69) \approx 0.064$.

Left-tailed, $H_0\colon p = 0.4$, $H_1\colon p < 0.4$, $K = \{0, \ldots, 68\}$. If at most 68 of the 200 people asked want to vote for the party, the fall is significant.

Connecting with chapter 4: a coin is tossed 1000 times and tested two-tailed at $\alpha = 5\%$. Find the range in which $H_0\colon p = 0.5$ is kept, using $\mu \pm 1.96\sigma$. Check it with the CAS values $P(X \le 468) \approx 0.0231$ and $P(X \le 469) \approx 0.0268$.

$\mu = 500$, $\sigma \approx 15.81$, $1.96\sigma \approx 31$: kept for 469 to 531. Check: $P(X \le 468) \le 0.025$, but $P(X \le 469) > 0.025$, so $K = \{0, \ldots, 468\} \cup \{532, \ldots, 1000\}$ ✔ The approximation is spot on.

A bakery claims that at most $10\%$ of its rolls are too light. An inspector weighs 400 rolls ($\alpha = 5\%$, right-tailed). Find the smallest number $k$ of rolls that are too light from which on she contradicts, first with the approximation $k \ge \mu + 1.645\,\sigma + 0.5$, then with $P(X \ge 50) \approx 0.060$ and $P(X \ge 51) \approx 0.044$.

$\mu = 40$, $\sigma = 6$: $k \ge 40 + 9.87 + 0.5 = 50.37$, so $k = 51$. Exactly: $P(X \ge 51) \le 0.05 < P(X \ge 50)$, $K = \{51, \ldots, 400\}$.

EXCURSIONTHE MOST FAMOUS TEST IN STATISTICS

The lady tasting tea

In his book on the design of experiments, the English statistician Ronald A. Fisher tells of a lady who claimed she could tell by the taste whether the milk or the tea had been poured into the cup first.

Fisher planned the test like this: eight cups, four of each kind, in random order. The lady is to pick out the four cups “milk first”. If she is only guessing, there are $\binom84 = 70$ equally likely choices, so all four correct only with probability $\tfrac{1}{70} \approx 1.4\%$. If she succeeds, you reject the null hypothesis “she is only guessing”. Fisher turned this into the basic pattern of every significance test.

Source: Ronald A. Fisher: The Design of Experiments. Oliver and Boyd, Edinburgh 1935, chapter 2.
