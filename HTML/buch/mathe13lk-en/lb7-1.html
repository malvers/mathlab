Biquadratic equations and families of functions

$x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = 0$ looks like an equation of degree four, but it is a quadratic equation in disguise. With a trick, substitution, you solve it without a CAS. After that a parameter sits in the function: a whole family of graphs whose maxima and minima move along a locus. And finally the advanced-course question par excellence: for which values of the parameter are there no, one, two or infinitely many solutions?

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Biquadratic equations

If you substitute the new variable $z$ for $x^2$ in $x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = 0$, you get $z^2 - 5z + 4 = 0$ with the solutions $z = 1$ and $z = 4$. Back: $x^2 = 1$ gives $x = \pm 1$, $x^2 = 4$ gives $x = \pm 2$. Four solutions, without a formula for equations of degree four.

Equations of the form $ax^4 + bx^2 + c = 0$ are called biquadratic. The substitution $z = x^2$ turns them into a quadratic equation. In the back-substitution the sign of each solution $z$ decides: a positive one gives two solutions $x$, zero gives one, a negative one none. That is why a biquadratic equation can have zero to four solutions. Its solutions lie symmetrically about 0, because together with $x$, $-x$ is also a solution.

KEY POINT · SUBSTITUTION

$ax^4 + bx^2 + c = 0$   with $z = x^2$:   solve $az^2 + bz + c = 0$.

Back-substitution: $z > 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm\sqrt z$ · $z = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$ · $z < 0 \Rightarrow$ no solution.

The same idea helps with $x^6 + px^3 + q = 0$ with $z = x^3$, and wherever a term and its square occur.

Graph: $f(x) = ax^4 + bx^2 + c$ is symmetric about the $y$-axis. So the zeros always come in pairs $\pm x_0$, except perhaps 0.

Careful: do not forget to substitute back. $z = 4$ is not a solution of $x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = 0$.

EXAMPLE 1 · TWO SOLUTIONS

Solve $x^4 + x^2 - 12 = 0$.

$z^2 + z - 12 = 0$: $z = 3$ or $z = -4$. Only $z = 3 > 0$ gives solutions.

$x = \pm\sqrt3 \approx \pm 1.73$

EXAMPLE 2 · ZEROS AND EXTREME POINTS

Find the zeros and the turning points of $f(x) = \tfrac14 x^4 - 2x^2 + 3$.

Times 4: $x^4 - 8x^2 + 12 = 0$, $z = 2$ or $z = 6$: $x = \pm\sqrt2$, $x = \pm\sqrt6$.

$f'(x) = x^3 - 4x = x(x^2 - 4)$: maximum $(0, 3)$, minima $(\pm 2, -1)$.

At which points do the graphs of $f(x) = x^4$ and $g(x) = 3x^2 + 4$ intersect?

$x^4 - 3x^2 - 4 = 0$: $x = \pm 2$ (from exercise 1 b). Points of intersection $(-2, 16)$ and $(2, 16)$.

Solve $x^6 - 9x^3 + 8 = 0$ with a suitable substitution. Why are there only two solutions here, although both $z$ are positive?

$z = x^3$: $z^2 - 9z + 8 = 0$, $z = 1$ or $z = 8$. $x^3 = 1$ and $x^3 = 8$ each have exactly one real solution: $x = 1$, $x = 2$. The cube root is unique, unlike the square root.

Calculate the area that the graph of $f(x) = 4 - \tfrac14 x^4$ encloses with the $x$-axis.

$x^4 = 16$, so $x = \pm 2$. $A = \int_{-2}^{2} \left(4 - \tfrac14 x^4\right)\mathrm{d}x = \left[4x - \tfrac{1}{20} x^5\right]_{-2}^{2} = 2\,(8 - 1.6) = 12.8$

Investigate how many solutions $x^4 - 2ax^2 + a^2 - 1 = 0$ has, depending on $a$. Give all cases.

The equation in $z$ can be factorised: $(z - a)^2 = 1$.

$z = a + 1$ or $z = a - 1$.

$a > 1$: both positive, four solutions $\pm\sqrt{a + 1}$, $\pm\sqrt{a - 1}$ · $a = 1$: $z = 2$, $z = 0$, three solutions · $-1 < a < 1$: only $a + 1 > 0$, two solutions · $a = -1$: $z = 0$, one solution $x = 0$ · $a < -1$: no solution.

UNIT 23 LESSONS

2Families of functions and loci

For every value of $a$, $f_a(x) = -x^2 + ax$ is a different parabola. All of them pass through the origin, because $f_a(0) = 0$. Their vertices are at $\left(\tfrac a2, \tfrac{a^2}{4}\right)$. If you set $x = \tfrac a2$, i.e. $a = 2x$, then $y = \tfrac{a^2}{4} = x^2$: all the vertices lie on the standard parabola.

Such a family is called a family of functions, and $a$ is its parameter. You investigate it like a single function, except that $a$ comes along as a fixed but unknown number. You often need to distinguish cases: for some $a$ there are extreme points, for others there are not. The curve on which all the extreme points (or points of inflection) lie is called the locus. A CAS with a slider helps with discovering; the justification comes from the calculation.

KEY POINT · FAMILIES OF FUNCTIONS

Common points: solve $f_a(x) = f_b(x)$ for $a \neq b$; the solutions do not depend on the parameter.

Special points: differentiate with respect to $x$, treat $a$ like a number; distinguish cases for $a$.

Locus: point $\big(x(a), y(a)\big)$; solve the equation $x = x(a)$ for $a$ and substitute into $y(a)$.

Notation: $f_a$ is a function of $x$; the index $a$ says which member of the family is meant. $f_a'$ is the derivative with respect to $x$.

Finding the parameter: write down the condition (point, gradient, area) and solve for $a$, as when finding a function from its properties in Year 12.

EXAMPLE 3 · A CUBIC FAMILY

Investigate $f_a(x) = x^3 - 3ax^2$ for $a > 0$ for extreme points and points of inflection. On which curve do the points of inflection lie?

$f_a'(x) = 3x(x - 2a)$: maximum point $(0, 0)$, minimum point $(2a, -4a^3)$. $f_a''(x) = 6x - 6a = 0$: point of inflection $(a, -2a^3)$.

Substituting $x = a$: locus of the points of inflection $y = -2x^3$

EXAMPLE 4 · PARAMETER FROM AN AREA

The graph of $f_t(x) = tx - x^2$ with $t > 0$ encloses an area with the $x$-axis. For which $t$ is it 36?

Zeros 0 and $t$: $\int_0^t (tx - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{t^3}{2} - \tfrac{t^3}{3} = \tfrac{t^3}{6}$.

$\tfrac{t^3}{6} = 36$: $t = 6$

EXERCISES

Find the vertex of $f_a(x) = x^2 - 2ax$ and the locus of all the vertices.

$f_a'(x) = 2x - 2a = 0$: vertex $(a, -a^2)$. Locus $y = -x^2$.

Which points do all the graphs of $f_a(x) = a\,x\,(x - 2)$ with $a \neq 0$ have in common?

$(a - b)\,x\,(x - 2) = 0$: $(0, 0)$ and $(2, 0)$

How many zeros does $f_a(x) = x^3 - ax$ have, depending on $a$? Where are the extreme points for $a > 0$?

$x(x^2 - a) = 0$: three for $a > 0$ (0, $\pm\sqrt a$), one for $a \le 0$. Extreme points: $3x^2 = a$, $x = \pm\sqrt{\tfrac a3}$.

For $f_a(x) = \mathrm{e}^x - ax$ with $a > 0$, find the minimum point and its locus. For which $a$ does the minimum point lie on the $x$-axis?

$\mathrm{e}^x = a$: $T(\ln a, a - a\ln a)$. With $a = \mathrm{e}^x$: locus $y = \mathrm{e}^x (1 - x)$. On the axis: $a(1 - \ln a) = 0$, $a = \mathrm{e}$.

Given $f_a(x) = x^2 - ax$ with $a > 0$. a) For which $a$ is the minimum point at $x = 3$? b) For which $a$ does the graph enclose the area 36 with the $x$-axis? What do you notice?

a) $2x - a = 0$ at $x = 3$: $a = 6$. b) Zeros 0 and $a$, area $\tfrac{a^3}{6} = 36$: $a = 6$. It is the same member of the family, and it is the mirror image of $f_6$ from Example 4 in the $x$-axis.

UNIT 32 LESSONS

3Parameters and the number of solutions

$x^2 = 2x + a$ leads to $x^2 - 2x - a = 0$ with the discriminant $1 + a$. For $a > -1$ the line cuts the parabola twice, for $a = -1$ it touches it (tangent), for $a < -1$ not at all.

A problem with a parameter is only solved when it is clear for every value of the parameter what happens. The parameter values at which the number of solutions changes are the critical values. They arise where something becomes zero: a discriminant, a leading coefficient, a denominator, a gradient. Between two critical values the situation stays the same. It is also important whether a condition is necessary or sufficient: $f'(x) = 0$ is necessary for an extreme point, but not sufficient.

KEY POINT · CASE ANALYSIS

1. Set up the equation and transform it in general as far as possible.   2. Look for critical values: where do you divide by zero, where does a coefficient or the discriminant become zero?

3. Investigate every critical value and every range in between separately.   4. Give the result as a complete overview; no case may be missing.

Most common mistake: dividing by a term with a parameter without treating the case “term equals zero” separately.

On the CAS: a slider for the parameter shows where something changes. You still have to justify it with a calculation.

EXAMPLE 5 · THE FORGOTTEN CASE

How many solutions does $ax^2 + 2x + 1 = 0$ have, depending on $a$?

$a = 0$: not a quadratic equation, but $2x + 1 = 0$, exactly one solution $x = -\tfrac12$.

$a \neq 0$: discriminant $4 - 4a$. $a < 1$, $a \neq 0$: two · $a = 1$: one ($x = -1$) · $a > 1$: none.

EXAMPLE 6 · A LINE AND THE EXPONENTIAL FUNCTION

How many points of intersection do $f(x) = \mathrm{e}^x$ and the line through the origin $g_a(x) = ax$ have?

Touching: $\mathrm{e}^x = ax$ and $\mathrm{e}^x = a$ at the same time, so $x = 1$, $a = \mathrm{e}$. For $a > 0$, $h(x) = \mathrm{e}^x - ax$ has the minimum $a(1 - \ln a)$ at $x = \ln a$.

$a < 0$: one · $0 \le a < \mathrm{e}$: none · $a = \mathrm{e}$: one (tangent) · $a > \mathrm{e}$: two.

EXERCISES

How many zeros does $f_a(x) = x^3 - 3x + a$ have, depending on $a$?

The turning points lie at $x = -1$ and $x = 1$. When do the maximum and the minimum lie on different sides of the $x$-axis?

$f_a(-1) = 2 + a$ (maximum), $f_a(1) = a - 2$ (minimum). $-2 < a < 2$: three · $a = \pm 2$: two (one double) · $|a| > 2$: one.

For which $a$ does $\dfrac{x + a}{x - 1} = 2$ have exactly one solution? Watch the domain.

$x + a = 2x - 2$, so $x = a + 2$. This is only allowed if $x \neq 1$, i.e. $a \neq -1$. Exactly one solution for $a \neq -1$, none for $a = -1$.

Open problem: invent an equation with a parameter $a$ that has exactly three solutions for $a = 1$, four for $a > 1$ and at most two for $a < 1$. Explain.

For example the equation from exercise 5: $x^4 - 2ax^2 + a^2 - 1 = 0$. For $a = 1$, $z = 0$ is a solution (giving $x = 0$) and so is $z = 2$ (giving $\pm\sqrt2$). Other examples are correct if the case analysis is right.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

Find the zeros, turning points and points of inflection of $f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$.

Zeros 0 and $\pm\sqrt2$. $f'(x) = 4x(x^2 - 1)$: maximum $(0, 0)$, minima $(\pm 1, -1)$. $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 4 = 0$: points of inflection at $x = \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$ with $y = -\tfrac59$.

Connecting with chapter 3.3: for which $a > 0$ does $f_a(x) = x^4 - a^2 x^2$ enclose an area of $\tfrac{16}{15}$ with the $x$-axis between its outer zeros?

Zeros 0, $\pm a$, between $-a$ and $a$ below the axis. $\int_0^a (a^2x^2 - x^4)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{a^5}{3} - \tfrac{a^5}{5} = \tfrac{2a^5}{15}$, both halves: $\tfrac{4a^5}{15} = \tfrac{16}{15}$, so $a^5 = 4$, $a = \sqrt[5]{4} \approx 1.32$.

Connecting with chapter 5.2: the rejection region of a test depends on the significance level $\alpha$, just as a solution depends on a parameter. Explain why a smaller $\alpha$ never makes the rejection region larger.

For a right-tailed test, $K = \{k, \ldots, n\}$ with the smallest $k$ for which $P(X \ge k) \le \alpha$. If $\alpha$ gets smaller, fewer $k$ satisfy this condition, and the smallest possible $k$ gets larger or stays the same.

Given the family $f_a(x) = x^4 - ax^2$ with $a > 0$. Find the minima and their locus. For which $a$ do the minima lie on the line $y = -4$?

$f_a'(x) = 4x^3 - 2ax = 0$: $x = 0$ or $x = \pm\sqrt{\tfrac a2}$. Minima $\left(\pm\sqrt{\tfrac a2}, -\tfrac{a^2}{4}\right)$. With $a = 2x^2$: locus $y = -x^4$. $-\tfrac{a^2}{4} = -4$: $a = 4$.

EXCURSIONMATHEMATICS TO MARVEL AT

A parabola made of straight lines

If you stretch threads between the two arms of a right angle, the first point on one arm to the last on the other, the second to the second-to-last and so on, a curved line appears, although every single thread is straight. The threads only touch the curve: it is the envelope of the family of lines.

For a simple family of lines you can check this: the lines $y = ax - \tfrac{a^2}{4}$ all touch the standard parabola, each at a different point. The curve of the string art picture is a parabola too. With the arms on the axes and threads from $(k, 0)$ to $(0, c - k)$, the envelope has the equation $\sqrt x + \sqrt y = \sqrt c$, and if you square twice, you get the equation of a parabola that lies at an angle to the axes.

To check: squaring $\sqrt x + \sqrt y = \sqrt c$ twice gives $(x - y)^2 - 2c(x + y) + c^2 = 0$, a parabola with the axis of symmetry $y = x$.
