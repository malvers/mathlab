Parameters and extreme values in space and economics

UNIT 12 LESSONS

1Systems of equations with a parameter

The system $x + 2y = 4$, $2x + ay = b$ has two lines as solution sets. Depending on $a$ and $b$ they intersect, are parallel or lie on top of each other.

With Gaussian elimination from Year 11 you calculate down to echelon form and then look at the last row. If a parameter stands in front of the unknown there, its value decides.

KEY POINT · SOLVABILITY

Last row of the echelon form: $c \cdot z = e$.

$c \neq 0$: exactly one solution · $c = 0$ and $e = 0$: infinitely many solutions · $c = 0$ and $e \neq 0$: no solution.

Whether $c$ or $e$ becomes zero depends on the parameter. These values are the special cases.

In matrix form $A_a \cdot \vec{x} = \vec{b}$: there is exactly one solution if $A_a$ is invertible. For $2 \times 2$ matrices this means $\det A_a \neq 0$; otherwise the CAS gives the determinant.

Geometrically: three equations with three unknowns are three planes. Infinitely many solutions means: they have a common line (or are the same).

EXAMPLE 1 · TWO EQUATIONS

Investigate $x + 2y = 4$, $2x + ay = b$ for solvability.

Second minus twice the first: $(a - 4)\,y = b - 8$. $a \neq 4$: exactly one solution. $a = 4$, $b = 8$: infinitely many (the same line). $a = 4$, $b \neq 8$: none (parallel lines).

EXAMPLE 2 · THREE EQUATIONS

$x + y + z = 3$, $x + 2y + 3z = 6$, $x + 3y + az = 9$. For which $a$ are there infinitely many solutions?

Gauss: $y + 2z = 3$, $2y + (a - 1)z = 6$, then $(a - 5)\,z = 0$. For $a \neq 5$: $z = 0$, $y = 3$, $x = 0$. For $a = 5$, $z$ is free: infinitely many solutions.

EXERCISES

For which $a$ does $2x - y = 1$, $ax + 3y = 4$ have no solution?

From the first $y = 2x - 1$, substituted: $(a + 6)\,x = 7$. For $a = -6$ you get $0 = 7$: no solution.

For which $a$ is $A_a = \begin{pmatrix} a & 2 \\ 2 & a - 3 \end{pmatrix}$ invertible? What does this mean for $A_a \cdot \vec{x} = \vec{b}$?

$\det A_a = a(a - 3) - 4 = a^2 - 3a - 4 = (a - 4)(a + 1)$. Invertible for $a \neq 4$ and $a \neq -1$: then the system has exactly one solution for every $\vec{b}$. For $a = 4$ or $a = -1$ none or infinitely many, depending on $\vec{b}$.

For which $a$ do the vectors $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ a \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ lie in one plane through the origin?

They lie in one plane if $r\,\vec{u} + s\,\vec{v} + t\,\vec{w} = \vec{0}$ has a solution that is not all zeros.

$r + 2s = 0$, $s + t = 0$, $r + as + t = 0$. With $r = -2s$ and $t = -s$: $-2s + as - s = (a - 3)\,s = 0$. For $a = 3$, $s$ is free, for example $s = 1$, $r = -2$, $t = -1$. Otherwise only the zero solution.

In the system from example 2 the right-hand side of the third equation is replaced by $c$. For which $a$ and $c$ is there no solution?

Last row $(a - 5)\,z = c - 9$: no solution for $a = 5$ and $c \neq 9$.

2Rotation matrices in the plane

If you rotate the point $(1, 0)$ through the angle $\varphi$ about the origin, it lands at $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$; the point $(0, 1)$ lands at $(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$. These two images are exactly the columns of the rotation matrix. Every other point is a combination of the two, so one multiplication “matrix times vector” is enough. The angle of rotation is the parameter of the matrix.

KEY POINT · ROTATION ABOUT THE ORIGIN

$\vec{p}\,{}' = D_\varphi \cdot \vec{p}$ with $D_\varphi = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix}$ (anticlockwise).

Two rotations one after the other: $D_\beta \cdot D_\alpha = D_{\alpha + \beta}$. Rotating back: $D_\varphi^{-1} = D_{-\varphi}$.

Preserves lengths: a rotation does not change any distance from the origin, because $\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1$.

EXAMPLE 3 · ROTATING A TRIANGLE

Rotate the triangle $A(1, 0)$, $B(4, 0)$, $C(4, 2)$ through $90°$ about the origin. Show that two rotations through $90°$ give a rotation through $180°$.

$D_{90°} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} -y \\ x \end{pmatrix}$: $A'(0, 1)$, $B'(0, 4)$, $C'(-2, 4)$.

$D_{90°} \cdot D_{90°} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = D_{180°}$: every point is reflected in the origin.

EXAMPLE 4 · FINDING THE ANGLE

Through what angle must you rotate $P(4, 0)$ so that $P'$ lands on the line $y = x$?

$P' = (4\cos\varphi, 4\sin\varphi)$ with $\sin\varphi = \cos\varphi$, so $\tan\varphi = 1$: $\varphi = 45°$ or $\varphi = 225°$.

EXERCISES

Give $D_{45°}$ exactly and rotate $P(2, 0)$. Check that the distance from the origin stays the same.

$D_{45°} = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $P'(\sqrt2, \sqrt2)$. Distance $\sqrt{2 + 2} = 2$, as before.

Use the addition theorems $\cos(\alpha + \beta) = \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta$ and $\sin(\alpha + \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta$ to show that $D_\alpha \cdot D_\beta = D_{\alpha + \beta}$. Conclude that rotations about the origin commute and that $D_\varphi^{-1} = D_{-\varphi}$.

$$D_\alpha \cdot D_\beta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta & -(\cos\alpha\sin\beta + \sin\alpha\cos\beta) \\ \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta & \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta \end{pmatrix} = D_{\alpha + \beta}$$

Because $\alpha + \beta = \beta + \alpha$, $D_\alpha D_\beta = D_\beta D_\alpha$. And $D_\varphi \cdot D_{-\varphi} = D_0 = I$.

3Distances in space as an optimisation problem

At time $t$ the drone is at $X(t, t, 0)$. Its distance from the top of the tower $P(2, 4, 2)$ is $d(t) = \sqrt{(2 - t)^2 + (4 - t)^2 + 2^2}$.

We want the minimum of $d$. Since the square root increases monotonically, it is enough to look for the minimum of $d^2$. That is a quadratic function of $t$, much easier to differentiate without the root.

KEY POINT · DISTANCE BETWEEN A POINT AND A LINE

For a general point $X(t)$ of the line: set up $d(t)^2 = |\vec{PX}(t)|^2$ and minimise it with $\left(d^2\right)' = 0$.

At the minimum $\vec{PX}$ is perpendicular to the direction vector: $\vec{PX} \circ \vec{u} = 0$. That is another way to find the foot of the perpendicular.

Three ways to the distance point–line: minimise $d^2(t)$, the orthogonality $\vec{PX} \circ \vec{u} = 0$, or an auxiliary plane through $P$ with the normal vector $\vec{u}$ (chapter E1.3).

EXAMPLE 5 · THE DRONE

When is the drone closest to the top of the tower, and how close?

$d^2(t) = (2 - t)^2 + (4 - t)^2 + 4 = 2t^2 - 12t + 24$. $(d^2)' = 4t - 12 = 0 \Rightarrow t = 3$. $d^2(3) = 6$, $d = \sqrt6 \approx 2.45$ at the point $(3, 3, 0)$.

EXAMPLE 6 · CHECKING WITH THE SCALAR PRODUCT

Check that $\vec{PX}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{u} = (1, 1, 0)$ at $t = 3$.

$\vec{PX} = (3 - 2, 3 - 4, 0 - 2) = (1, -1, -2)$, $\vec{PX} \circ \vec{u} = 1 - 1 + 0 = 0$ ✓

EXERCISES

Find the distance of the point $P(1, 2, 3)$ from the $x$-axis $\vec{x} = t\,(1, 0, 0)$.

$d^2 = (t - 1)^2 + 4 + 9$, minimal at $t = 1$: $d = \sqrt{13} \approx 3.61$.

Find the distance of $P(3, 0, 3)$ from the line $\vec{x} = (1, 1, 0) + t\,(1, 0, 1)$.

$\vec{PX} = (t - 2, 1, t - 3)$, $\vec{PX} \circ (1, 0, 1) = 2t - 5 = 0 \Rightarrow t = 2.5$. $\vec{PX} = (0.5, 1, -0.5)$, $d = \sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.22$.

Two aircraft fly in straight lines: $A(t) = (0, 0, 8) + t\,(4, 2, 0)$ and $B(t) = (20, 0, 9) + t\,(0, 4, 0)$ (in km, $t$ in minutes). How close do they come to each other, and when? Where do their flight paths cross most closely?

For the first question minimise the distance $|B(t) - A(t)|$ at the same time. The second question is a different one: there each aircraft may be at the spot at its own time.

$B(t) - A(t) = (20 - 4t,\ 2t,\ 1)$, $d^2(t) = (20 - 4t)^2 + 4t^2 + 1 = 20t^2 - 160t + 401$, minimal at $t = 4$: $d^2 = 81$, $d = 9$ km after 4 minutes.

The paths themselves: $(4s, 2s, 8)$ and $(20, 4u, 9)$ are one above the other at $s = 5$, $u = 2.5$, at a distance of 1 km in height. So the paths come within 1 km of each other, but the aircraft only within 9 km, because they are there at different times.

4Costs, revenue and profit

A business has the cost function $K(x) = 0.02x^3 - 1.2x^2 + 30x + 200$ and sells at a price of €30 per unit. When does it make a profit, and how much at most?

The revenue is $E(x) = 30x$, the profit $G(x) = E(x) - K(x)$. The zeros of $G$ bound the profit zone, the maximum of $G$ gives the best quantity.

KEY POINT · ECONOMIC FUNCTIONS

Costs $K(x) = K_v(x) + K_f$ (variable + fixed costs) · revenue $E(x) = p \cdot x$ · profit $G(x) = E(x) - K(x)$

Break-even point and profit limit: the zeros of $G$. Maximum profit: $G'(x) = 0$, that is $E'(x) = K'(x)$ (marginal revenue = marginal costs).

Unit costs $k(x) = \tfrac{K(x)}{x}$. Their minimum is called the optimum level of production and is the long-term lower price limit.

Costs following the law of diminishing returns: a cubic cost function rises more and more slowly at first, then faster and faster. The point of inflection is where the marginal costs are lowest.

INTERACTIVE · COSTS, REVENUE, PROFIT

Change the price and watch how the profit zone and the maximum profit move.

EXAMPLE 7 · PROFIT ZONE AND MAXIMUM

For $p = 30$, find the profit zone and the maximum profit.

$G(x) = -0.02x^3 + 1.2x^2 - 200$. Zeros with a CAS: $x \approx 14.9$ and $x \approx 56.9$. $G'(x) = -0.06x^2 + 2.4x = 0 \Rightarrow x = 40$. At most €440 at 40 units.

EXAMPLE 8 · LOWER PRICE LIMIT

How low may the price fall at most in the long run?

Minimum of the unit costs $k(x) = 0.02x^2 - 1.2x + 30 + \tfrac{200}{x}$ with a CAS: at $x \approx 34.3$ with $k \approx 18.20$ euros. Below this price there is no quantity that makes a profit.

EXERCISES

For $K(x) = 0.02x^3 - 1.2x^2 + 30x + 200$, find the fixed costs and the point of the lowest marginal costs.

Fixed costs $K(0) = 200$ euros. $K''(x) = 0.12x - 2.4 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 20$.

Use the widget to find the maximum profit for $p = 25$ and $p = 40$. What do you notice?

$p = 25$: about €245 at 38 units. $p = 40$: about €860 at 44 units. A higher price moves the optimum to larger quantities.

Price as a parameter: for which prices $p$ does the business with $K(x) = 0.02x^3 - 1.2x^2 + 30x + 200$ make any profit at all? Explain why the answer is the lower price limit from example 8.

$G_p(x) = px - K(x) > 0 \iff p > \tfrac{K(x)}{x} = k(x)$. Such an $x$ exists exactly when $p$ is greater than the minimum of the unit costs. A profit is possible for $p > 18.20$ euros (rounded); with $p$ exactly equal to the minimum, $G_p$ has only one zero (point of contact).

Monopoly: a supplier with $K(x) = 0.5x^2 + 10x + 300$ can set the price itself; demand follows $p(x) = 70 - 1.5x$. Find the maximum profit, the price and Cournot's point.

$E(x) = 70x - 1.5x^2$, $G(x) = -2x^2 + 60x - 300$, $G'(x) = -4x + 60 = 0$: $x = 15$, $G(15) = 150$ euros. Price $p(15) = 47.50$ euros. Cournot's point $C(15, 47.5)$, marginal revenue $E'(15) = 25 = K'(15)$ ✓

EXCURSIONTHE DERIVATIVE REACHES ECONOMICS

Cournot's point

In 1838 the French mathematician Antoine Augustin Cournot published a book on the mathematical principles of the theory of wealth. In it he was one of the first to calculate with demand functions and derivatives: a supplier who sets the price himself makes the greatest profit where the marginal revenue equals the marginal costs.

The corresponding point on the demand curve is still called Cournot's point today. At the time the book was hardly noticed, only decades later did Cournot become one of the founding fathers of mathematical economics.

Source: Antoine Augustin Cournot: Recherches sur les principes mathématiques de la théorie des richesses. Paris 1838.
