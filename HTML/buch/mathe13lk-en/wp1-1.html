Lines and planes

A laser beam, the flight path of a drone, a roof surface, a pane of glass: in space, lines and planes are described with vectors. A point to start from and one or two directions are enough. With the cross product you also find the vector that is perpendicular to a plane, and from it the coordinate equation and the Hesse normal form, which tells you at once how far a point is from the plane. Everything can be turned in real 3D.

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Lines: parametric equation and point test

After $t$ seconds the drone is at the point $X$ with $\vec{OX} = (1, 2, 1) + t \cdot (1, -1, 2)$. For all real $t$ together, negative ones included, you get a line.

Every point of the line is reached from the point of support by adding a multiple of the direction vector. Each point belongs to exactly one value of the parameter $t$.

KEY POINT · LINE IN SPACE

Parametric equation: $g\colon \vec{x} = \vec{p} + t \cdot \vec{u}$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$, with position vector $\vec{p}$ and direction vector $\vec{u} \neq \vec{0}$.

Line through $A$ and $B$: $\vec{x} = \vec{OA} + t \cdot \vec{AB}$.

Point test: $Q$ lies on $g$ if $\vec{OQ} = \vec{p} + t \cdot \vec{u}$ for one $t$, that is all three rows give the same $t$.

Trace points are the points of intersection with the coordinate planes: for example $z = 0$ for the $xy$-plane.

Not unique: any point of the line can be the point of support, any multiple ($\neq 0$) of $\vec{u}$ the direction vector.

Line segment: if you restrict $t$ to $0 \le t \le 1$, you get the line segment from $A$ to $B$.

INTERACTIVE · LINE, POINT TEST, TRACE POINTS

Change the point of support $P$, the direction vector $\vec{u}$ and the test point $Q$. With $t$ the golden point $X$ moves along the line. Red: trace points.

EXAMPLE 1 · POINT TEST

Do $Q(3, 0, 5)$ and $R(4, 0, 7)$ lie on $g\colon \vec{x} = (1, 2, 1) + t \cdot (1, -1, 2)$?

$Q$: $1 + t = 3$, $2 - t = 0$, $1 + 2t = 5$, each time $t = 2$: $Q \in g$.

$R$: $1 + t = 4 \Rightarrow t = 3$, but $2 - t = 0 \Rightarrow t = 2$. Contradiction: $R \notin g$.

EXAMPLE 2 · TRACE POINTS

Find the trace points of the line $g$ from example 1.

$xy$-plane: $1 + 2t = 0$, $t = -\tfrac12$, $S_{xy}(0.5, 2.5, 0)$. $xz$-plane: $2 - t = 0$, $S_{xz}(3, 0, 5)$. $yz$-plane: $1 + t = 0$, $S_{yz}(0, 3, -1)$.

Does $P(5, 1, 7)$ lie on $g\colon \vec{x} = (1, 3, -1) + t \cdot (2, -1, 4)$?

All rows give $t = 2$: yes.

$\vec{x} = (1, 2, 2) + t \cdot (1, 1, -1)$. $z$: $2 - t = 0 \Rightarrow t = 2$, $y$: $2 + 2 = 4$ ✓, so $a = 1 + 2 = 3$.

A plane flies from $A(0, 0, 1)$ in a straight line in the direction $(10, 5, 0.5)$ (units in km). A mountain summit is at $G(30, 15, 2.4)$. Does it fly over it?

Above $G$, $t = 3$ (from $x$ and $y$), the flying height there is $1 + 1.5 = 2.5$ km. The plane flies 100 m above the summit.

2Planes in parametric form and the normal vector

The pane of glass contains $A(4, 0, 0)$. From there you reach every point of it by adding multiples of $\vec{AB} = (-4, 3, 0)$ and $\vec{AC} = (-4, 0, 2)$.

So a plane needs a point of support and two directions that are not parallel. Instead of two directions you can also give a single vector that is perpendicular to the plane: the normal vector. The cross product supplies it.

KEY POINT · PLANE AND CROSS PRODUCT

Parametric form: $E\colon \vec{x} = \vec{a} + r \cdot \vec{u} + s \cdot \vec{v}$ with non-parallel direction vectors $\vec{u}$, $\vec{v}$. Through three points: $\vec{x} = \vec{OA} + r\,\vec{AB} + s\,\vec{AC}$.

Cross product: $$\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} u_2 v_3 - u_3 v_2 \\ u_3 v_1 - u_1 v_3 \\ u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1 \end{pmatrix}$$ It is perpendicular to $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$, so it is a normal vector of the plane. Its magnitude is the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$.

Memory aid: for the first row cover up the first row and calculate “crosswise”, for the second the second row, for the third the third. The middle row gets the opposite sign.

Check: $\vec{n} \circ \vec{u} = 0$ and $\vec{n} \circ \vec{v} = 0$.

INTERACTIVE · PLANE AND NORMAL VECTOR

Enter the point of support and the direction vectors. The pink arrow is the normal vector. If the plane cuts all three axes, you see the trace triangle.

EXAMPLE 3 · CROSS PRODUCT

Calculate $\vec{u} \times \vec{v}$ for $\vec{u} = (-4, 3, 0)$ and $\vec{v} = (-4, 0, 2)$ and check.

$\begin{pmatrix} 3 \cdot 2 - 0 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot (-4) - (-4) \cdot 2 \\ (-4) \cdot 0 - 3 \cdot (-4) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \\ 12 \end{pmatrix}$, simpler $\vec{n} = (3, 4, 6)$. Check: $-12 + 12 + 0 = 0$, $-12 + 0 + 12 = 0$ ✓

EXAMPLE 4 · AREA OF A TRIANGLE

How large is the triangle with the corners $(4, 0, 0)$, $(0, 3, 0)$, $(0, 0, 2)$?

Half the parallelogram: $\tfrac12 |(6, 8, 12)| = \tfrac12 \sqrt{36 + 64 + 144} = \tfrac12 \sqrt{244} \approx 7.81$.

Give a parametric equation of the plane through $A(1, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 1, 0)$, $C(0, 0, 1)$.

$\vec{x} = (1, 0, 0) + r \cdot (-1, 1, 0) + s \cdot (-1, 0, 1)$

Calculate $(1, 0, 0) \times (0, 1, 0)$ and $(1, 1, 0) \times (0, 1, 1)$. Compare with the exploration.

$(0, 0, 1)$ and $(1, -1, 1)$. Check: $(1, -1, 1) \circ (1, 1, 0) = 0$, $(1, -1, 1) \circ (0, 1, 1) = 0$ ✓

Find a normal vector of the plane from exercise 5 and the area of the triangle $ABC$.

$(-1, 1, 0) \times (-1, 0, 1) = (1, 1, 1)$. Area $\tfrac12 \sqrt3 \approx 0.87$.

Show: $\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = -(\vec{v} \times \vec{u})$, and $\vec{u} \times \vec{u} = \vec{0}$. What does the second mean geometrically?

Swapping changes both products in every row, that is the sign. $\vec{u} \times \vec{u}$: every row is $u_i u_j - u_j u_i = 0$. Parallel vectors do not span a parallelogram, the area is 0.

3Coordinate equation and Hesse normal form

For all three corners of the pane, $3x + 4y + 6z$ gives the value 12. That is no coincidence: for every point $X$ of the plane, $\vec{AX}$ is perpendicular to $\vec{n} = (3, 4, 6)$.

$\vec{n} \circ (\vec{x} - \vec{a}) = 0$ written out means $3(x - 4) + 4y + 6z = 0$, so $3x + 4y + 6z = 12$. The coefficients of $x$, $y$, $z$ are exactly the components of the normal vector.

If you divide the equation by the length of $\vec{n}$, the normal vector becomes a unit vector $\vec{n}_0$. If you then substitute a point, you get its distance from the plane, with a sign: $(\vec{x} - \vec{a}) \circ \vec{n}_0$ is the length of the projection of $\vec{AX}$ onto the normal direction. This form is called the Hesse normal form, after Otto Hesse.

KEY POINT · COORDINATE EQUATION

$E\colon n_1 x + n_2 y + n_3 z = d$ with the normal vector $\vec{n} = (n_1, n_2, n_3)$ and $d = \vec{n} \circ \vec{a}$ for a point $A$ of the plane.

From the parametric form: find $\vec{n} = \vec{u} \times \vec{v}$, then substitute a point to find $d$. Point test: substitute the coordinates.

Trace points with the axes: set two coordinates to zero. If a variable is missing, the plane is parallel to this axis.

KEY POINT · HESSE NORMAL FORM

$(\vec{x} - \vec{a}) \circ \vec{n}_0 = 0$ with $|\vec{n}_0| = 1$. From the coordinate equation: $$\frac{n_1 x + n_2 y + n_3 z - d}{\sqrt{n_1^2 + n_2^2 + n_3^2}} = 0$$

Substituting a point $Q$ gives its distance with a sign. Same sign: same side of the plane. If you choose the sign so that $d \ge 0$, then $d$ itself is the distance of the origin, and negative values lie on its side.

Intercept form: $\tfrac{x}{4} + \tfrac{y}{3} + \tfrac{z}{2} = 1$ is the same plane as $3x + 4y + 6z = 12$. The denominators are the trace points.

Otto Hesse (1811–1874) taught in Königsberg, Heidelberg and Munich. The normal form appears in his Vorlesungen über analytische Geometrie des Raumes (Leipzig 1861). Source: MacTutor, “Otto Hesse”.

Outlook: in chapter E1.3 the Hesse normal form gives all distances to planes.

INTERACTIVE · THE IDEA OF THE HESSE NORMAL FORM, IN THE PLANE

In the plane, a line plays the role of the plane. Drag the base point $A$ and the point $P$ and rotate the normal vector: the value of the left-hand side is the distance from $P$ to the line, with a sign. In space it works just the same, only with three coordinates.

EXAMPLE 5 · FROM THE PARAMETRIC FORM TO THE COORDINATE EQUATION

$E\colon \vec{x} = (1, 2, 0) + r\,(1, 0, 1) + s\,(0, 1, 2)$. Find the coordinate equation.

$\vec{n} = (1, 0, 1) \times (0, 1, 2) = (-1, -2, 1)$. $d = \vec{n} \circ (1, 2, 0) = -5$. $-x - 2y + z = -5$, or more neatly $x + 2y - z = 5$.

EXAMPLE 6 · HESSE NORMAL FORM

$E\colon 2x - y + 2z = -9$. Find the Hesse normal form, the distance of the origin and the distance of $Q(1, 2, 3)$.

Multiply by −1 so that $d \ge 0$: $-2x + y - 2z = 9$, $|\vec{n}| = 3$, HNF $\tfrac13(-2x + y - 2z - 9) = 0$. Origin: distance 3.

$Q$: $\tfrac13(-2 + 2 - 6 - 9) = -5$: $d(Q, E) = 5$, on the same side as the origin.

EXERCISES

Do $P(1, 1, 1)$ and $Q(3, 2, 0)$ lie in $E\colon 2x + 3y + 6z = 12$? Also find the trace points of $E$.

$P$: $2 + 3 + 6 = 11 \neq 12$, no. $Q$: $6 + 6 + 0 = 12$, yes. Trace points $S_x(6, 0, 0)$, $S_y(0, 4, 0)$, $S_z(0, 0, 2)$.

Find the coordinate equation of the plane through $A(1, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 1, 0)$, $C(0, 0, 1)$ and its Hesse normal form. How far is it from the origin?

$\vec{n} = (1, 1, 1)$ (exercise 7), $d = 1$: $x + y + z = 1$. HNF $\tfrac{x + y + z - 1}{\sqrt3} = 0$, distance of the origin $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3} \approx 0.577$.

Give the Hesse normal form of $E\colon 2x + 2y - z = 6$. How far are the origin and $P(4, 4, 1)$ from $E$? Do they lie on the same side?

$\tfrac13(2x + 2y - z - 6) = 0$. Origin: $\tfrac{-6}{3} = -2$, distance 2. $P$: $\tfrac13(8 + 8 - 1 - 6) = 3$: distance 3, on the other side from the origin.

Find the two planes that are parallel to $E\colon 2x + y + 2z = 6$ and at distance 2 from it.

Same normal vector, $|\vec{n}| = 3$: $\tfrac{|d - 6|}{3} = 2$, so $d = 12$ or $d = 0$: $2x + y + 2z = 12$ and $2x + y + 2z = 0$.

Find the set of all points that are equally far from $E_1\colon x + 2y + 2z = 3$ and $E_2\colon 2x - y + 2z = 6$.

Set the Hesse normal forms equal in absolute value: $\tfrac{x + 2y + 2z - 3}{3} = \pm \tfrac{2x - y + 2z - 6}{3}$.

With $+$: $-x + 3y + 3 = 0$, i.e. $x - 3y = 3$. With $-$: $3x + y + 4z = 9$. Two planes that bisect the angles between $E_1$ and $E_2$. They are perpendicular to each other: $(1, -3, 0) \circ (3, 1, 4) = 0$.

Give equations of the three coordinate axes as lines.

$x$-axis: $\vec{x} = t \cdot (1, 0, 0)$, $y$-axis: $t \cdot (0, 1, 0)$, $z$-axis: $t \cdot (0, 0, 1)$.

Connecting with Year 12: use vectors to check whether $A(1, 1, 1)$, $B(3, 2, 3)$, $C(7, 4, 7)$ lie on one line.

$\vec{AB} = (2, 1, 2)$, $\vec{AC} = (6, 3, 6) = 3\vec{AB}$: parallel with the common point $A$, so yes.

Calculate the area of the parallelogram with the corners $A(1, 0, 2)$, $B(4, 1, 2)$, $C(5, 3, 4)$, $D(2, 2, 4)$.

$\vec{AB} = (3, 1, 0)$, $\vec{AD} = (1, 2, 2)$, $\vec{AB} \times \vec{AD} = (2, -6, 5)$, area $\sqrt{4 + 36 + 25} = \sqrt{65} \approx 8.06$.

Open problem: give three different planes that all pass through $A(1, 1, 1)$ and $B(3, 1, 2)$, each once in parametric form, as a coordinate equation and in Hesse normal form. What do all these planes have in common?

All of them contain the line $AB$. As the second direction vector you can take any vector that is not parallel to $\vec{AB} = (2, 0, 1)$.

For example with $\vec{v} = (0, 1, 0)$: $\vec{n} = (2, 0, 1) \times (0, 1, 0) = (-1, 0, 2)$, $-x + 2z = 1$, HNF $\tfrac{-x + 2z - 1}{\sqrt5} = 0$. With $\vec{v} = (0, 0, 1)$: $\vec{n} = (0, -2, 0)$, so $y = 1$.

All planes through $A$ and $B$ contain the line $AB$: they form a “sheaf of planes” that turns about this line like the pages of an open book about its spine.

EXCURSIONGEOMETRY BECOMES CALCULATION

René Descartes and analytic geometry

Until the 17th century geometry and calculation were two separate worlds. In 1637 the French philosopher René Descartes published, as an appendix to his “Discours de la méthode”, the text “La Géométrie”. In it he translated geometric problems into equations: points became numbers, curves became formulas.

This gave rise to analytic geometry, which you learn in this elective. The coordinate systems that we call “Cartesian” bear his Latinised name Cartesius. Incidentally, Pierre de Fermat had very similar ideas at the same time.

Source: René Descartes: La Géométrie. Appendix to the Discours de la méthode, Leiden 1637.
