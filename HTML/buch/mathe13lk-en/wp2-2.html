Interdependence and the Leontief model

A muesli maker first mixes two base blends from oats, nuts and dried fruit, and then two kinds of muesli from those. How much raw material does an order need, what does a bag cost, is production worth it? With the Gozinto graph and the matrix product you calculate this in a few lines. And a whole economy in which every sector also supplies the others is described by the Leontief model with a single matrix equation.

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Gozinto graphs and multi-stage production

A bag of Classic contains 2 units of Base with 4 units of oats each and 1 unit of Fruit with 2 units of oats: $2 \cdot 4 + 1 \cdot 2 = 10$ units. That is row $R_1$ of the raw material–intermediate product matrix times column $F_1$ of the intermediate–final product matrix, one entry of the matrix product.

The numbers on the arrows of a Gozinto graph are collected in requirement matrices: the rows belong to what goes in, the columns to what is made. A production plan $\vec{p}$ states how many final products are made. Multiplying it from the left by the requirement matrices gives what is needed at every stage.

KEY POINT · TWO-STAGE PRODUCTION

$M_{RI}$: raw materials per intermediate product · $M_{IF}$: intermediate products per final product · total requirement matrix $M_{RF} = M_{RI} \cdot M_{IF}$: raw materials per final product.

Needed for the production plan $\vec{p}$: intermediate products $\vec{z} = M_{IF} \cdot \vec{p}$, raw materials $\vec{r} = M_{RF} \cdot \vec{p} = M_{RI} \cdot \vec{z}$.

Backwards: from the stocks $\vec{z}$ it follows that $\vec{p} = M_{IF}^{-1} \cdot \vec{z}$, if $M_{IF}$ is square and invertible. Otherwise solve a system of equations and check whether it works out.

The name: “Gozinto” is a joke by the operations researcher Andrew Vazsonyi, who quoted an invented Italian mathematician “Zepartzat Gozinto”: the part that goes into.

Order: $M_{RI} \cdot M_{IF}$ is compatible (intermediate products in the middle), $M_{IF} \cdot M_{RI}$ is not.

INTERACTIVE · A GOZINTO GRAPH WITH A PRODUCTION PLAN

A second example, a chocolate factory. Set the production plan: the graph shows what is needed at every stage. In the widget the matrices are called $A = M_{RI}$, $B = M_{IF}$ and $C = M_{RF}$ for short.

EXAMPLE 1 · THE ORDER

The maker is to deliver 30 bags of Classic and 20 bags of fruit muesli. Find $M_{RF}$ and the amounts needed.

$M_{RI} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $M_{IF} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $M_{RF} = M_{RI} \cdot M_{IF} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 8 \\ 3 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$.

$\vec{z} = M_{IF} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 30 \\ 20 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 80 \\ 70 \end{pmatrix}$, $\vec{r} = M_{RF} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 30 \\ 20 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 460 \\ 150 \\ 140 \end{pmatrix}$ units of oats, nuts, fruit.

EXAMPLE 2 · DOES THE STOCK WORK OUT?

The store holds 520 units of oats, 165 units of nuts and 170 units of fruit. Can everything be made into muesli with nothing left over?

$M_{RF} \cdot \vec{p} = \vec{r}$: from $10a + 8b = 520$ and $3a + 3b = 165$ it follows that $a = 40$, $b = 15$.

Third row: $2 \cdot 40 + 4 \cdot 15 = 140 \neq 170$. Not completely: 30 units of fruit are left over. Three equations, two unknowns: the system is overdetermined.

Draw a Gozinto graph for $M_{RI} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $M_{IF} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$. How many raw materials, intermediate and final products are there? Calculate $M_{RF}$.

For which stocks $r_1$, $r_2$, $r_3$ of oats, nuts and fruit does the store work out with nothing left over? Derive a condition by eliminating $a$ and $b$ from $\vec{r} = M_{RF} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix}$.

2Cost calculation for linear interdependence

Raw material costs per bag: oats, nuts and fruit cost €0.50, €2 and €1.50 per unit. For a bag of Classic that is $0.5 \cdot 10 + 2 \cdot 3 + 1.5 \cdot 2 = 14$ euros: the cost row vector times the column $F_1$ of $M_{RF}$.

Costs arise at every stage: for the raw materials, for making the intermediate products and for finishing the final products. Cost vectors are written as rows, because they are multiplied from the left by the requirement matrices. This gives the variable unit costs of every final product. On top of that come fixed costs, which arise regardless of the quantity.

KEY POINT · COSTS, REVENUE, PROFIT

Variable costs per unit: $\vec{k}_v = \vec{k}_R \cdot M_{RF} + \vec{k}_I \cdot M_{IF} + \vec{k}_F$ (all row vectors)

Total costs $K = \vec{k}_v \cdot \vec{p} + K_{\text{fix}}$ · revenue $E = \vec{e} \cdot \vec{p}$ with the price vector $\vec{e}$ · profit $G = E - K$

Contribution margin per unit: $\vec{d} = \vec{e} - \vec{k}_v$, it “covers” the fixed costs. $G = \vec{d} \cdot \vec{p} - K_{\text{fix}}$.

Row or column? Quantities are columns (they are attached on the right), prices and costs are rows (they stand on the left). Then all the products are compatible.

EXAMPLE 3 · UNIT COSTS

Production costs: Base €1, Fruit €1.50 per unit; filling: Classic €2, fruit muesli €2.50 per bag. Find $\vec{k}_v$.

$\vec{k}_R \cdot M_{RF} = (0.5 \;\; 2 \;\; 1.5) \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 8 \\ 3 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = (14 \;\; 16)$, $\vec{k}_I \cdot M_{IF} = (1 \;\; 1.5) \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = (3.5 \;\; 4)$.

$\vec{k}_v = (14 + 3.5 + 2 \;\;\; 16 + 4 + 2.5) = (19.5 \;\; 22.5)$ euros per bag.

EXAMPLE 4 · PROFIT

Prices €25 and €30, fixed costs €250. How large is the profit on the order for 30 and 20 bags?

Contribution margins $\vec{d} = (25 - 19.5 \;\;\; 30 - 22.5) = (5.5 \;\; 7.5)$.

$G = 5.5 \cdot 30 + 7.5 \cdot 20 - 250 = 165 + 150 - 250 = $ €65. Check: revenue €1350, costs $19.5 \cdot 30 + 22.5 \cdot 20 + 250 = 1285$ euros.

Classic: $5.5 \cdot a > 250 \Rightarrow a \ge 46$. Fruit muesli: $7.5 \cdot b > 250 \Rightarrow b \ge 34$.

The price of nuts rises from €2 to €3 per unit. By how much do the variable costs per bag rise? Read the answer directly from $M_{RF}$.

Row “nuts” of $M_{RF}$ is $(3 \;\; 3)$: every bag gets $3 \cdot 1 = 3$ euros more expensive, $\vec{k}_v = (22.5 \;\; 25.5)$.

The maker can get at most 520 units of oats per week; everything else is plentiful. It can sell any amount. Which production plan brings the largest profit? Explain with the contribution margin per unit of oats.

Classic needs 10 units of oats and brings a contribution margin of €5.50, fruit muesli 8 units and €7.50.

Per unit of oats: Classic $\tfrac{5.5}{10} = 0.55$ euros, fruit muesli $\tfrac{7.5}{8} \approx 0.94$ euros. So only fruit muesli: $b = 520 : 8 = 65$ bags, $G = 7.5 \cdot 65 - 250 = 237.50$ euros. With Classic alone it would only be $5.5 \cdot 52 - 250 = 36$ euros.

3The Leontief model

Two sectors, energy $S_1$ and industry $S_2$, supply each other and the market. In one year (in units):

from ↓ to →$S_1$$S_2$ConsumptionProduction
$S_1$2060120200
$S_2$403080150

Every row balances: $20 + 60 + 120 = 200$. Of the 200 units of energy only 120 are left for the market, the rest is used up in the production process itself.

Wassily Leontief assumed that every sector needs its inputs in proportion to its own production. For 150 units of industry, $S_2$ needs 60 units of energy, so for one unit $a_{12} = \tfrac{60}{150} = 0.4$. These proportions form the technology matrix $A$. The production $\vec{x}$ then consists of the internal consumption $A \cdot \vec{x}$ and what goes to the market, the final demand $\vec{y}$.

KEY POINT · LEONTIEF MODEL

Technology matrix: $a_{ij} = \dfrac{x_{ij}}{x_j}$, the delivery from $S_i$ to $S_j$ divided by the production of the receiving sector $S_j$ (divide column by column).

$\vec{x} = A \cdot \vec{x} + \vec{y} \iff (I - A) \cdot \vec{x} = \vec{y}$ · If $I - A$ is invertible: $\vec{x} = (I - A)^{-1} \cdot \vec{y}$ with the Leontief inverse $(I - A)^{-1}$.

Its entry $l_{ij}$ states how much more $S_i$ has to produce if the final demand for $S_j$ rises by one unit.

Trap: if you divide row by row by the sector's own production, you get a completely different matrix. You divide by the production of the sector above the column.

Final demand, consumption, market supply mean the same thing: what leaves the production cycle.

INTERACTIVE · AN ECONOMY WITH THREE SECTORS

Set the final demand. The widget calculates the production needed with the Leontief inverse and fills in the input-output table. Watch how a higher demand in one sector drives all three.

EXAMPLE 5 · THE TECHNOLOGY MATRIX

Find $A$ from the table and check $(I - A) \cdot \vec{x} = \vec{y}$.

Column $S_1$ divided by 200, column $S_2$ by 150: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 & 0.4 \\ 0.2 & 0.2 \end{pmatrix}$.

$\begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & -0.4 \\ -0.2 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 200 \\ 150 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 180 - 60 \\ -40 + 120 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 120 \\ 80 \end{pmatrix}$ ✓ exactly the consumption column

EXAMPLE 6 · NEW DEMAND

Next year the market wants 150 units of energy and 100 units of industry. How much has to be produced?

$\det(I - A) = 0.72 - 0.08 = 0.64$, $(I - A)^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{0.64} \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & 0.4 \\ 0.2 & 0.9 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.25 & 0.625 \\ 0.3125 & 1.40625 \end{pmatrix}$.

$\vec{x} = (I - A)^{-1} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 150 \\ 100 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 250 \\ 187.5 \end{pmatrix}$. For 30 more units of energy on the market, $S_1$ has to generate 50 more units.

EXERCISES

Interpret the entries $a_{21} = 0.2$ and $l_{12} = 0.625$ in context.

$a_{21}$: for one unit of energy, $S_1$ needs 0.2 units from industry. $l_{12}$: if the final demand for industrial goods rises by one unit, energy production has to rise by 0.625 units, because industry needs energy, and energy itself again needs a little.

Set up the input-output table for the new year from example 6: deliveries $x_{ij} = a_{ij} \cdot x_j$, consumption, production. Check that every row balances.

$S_1$: $0.1 \cdot 250 = 25$, $0.4 \cdot 187.5 = 75$, consumption 150, total 250 ✓. $S_2$: $0.2 \cdot 250 = 50$, $0.2 \cdot 187.5 = 37.5$, consumption 100, total 187.5 ✓.

The energy sector can produce at most 300 units, industry has enough capacity. The demand for industrial goods stays at 100 units. How much energy can go to the market at most?

$x_1 = 1.25 y_1 + 0.625 \cdot 100 \le 300 \Rightarrow y_1 \le 190$. At most 190 units of energy. Then $x_2 = 0.3125 \cdot 190 + 140.625 = 200$.

Show in general for two sectors: if every column sum of $A$ is less than 1 and all $a_{ij} \ge 0$, then $\det(I - A) > 0$, and all entries of the Leontief inverse are non-negative. What does this mean economically?

$\det(I - A) = (1 - a_{11})(1 - a_{22}) - a_{12} a_{21}$, and $a_{12} < 1 - a_{22}$, $a_{21} < 1 - a_{11}$.

From $a_{12} < 1 - a_{22}$ and $a_{21} < 1 - a_{11}$ it follows that $a_{12} a_{21} < (1 - a_{11})(1 - a_{22})$, so $\det(I - A) > 0$. The inverse $\tfrac{1}{\det} \begin{pmatrix} 1 - a_{22} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & 1 - a_{11} \end{pmatrix}$ has only entries $\ge 0$.

Economically: for one unit, every sector needs less than one unit of inputs in total. Then every non-negative demand can be met with a non-negative production; the economy is “productive”.

Connecting with chapter E2.1: explain why $\vec{k}_R \cdot (M_{RI} \cdot \vec{z}) = (\vec{k}_R \cdot M_{RI}) \cdot \vec{z}$ holds and what the two sides mean in context.

The associative law of matrix multiplication. Left: first the raw materials needed $\vec{r}$, then their costs. Right: first the raw material costs per intermediate product, then times the number of pieces. Both are the raw material costs.

Connecting with learning area 7: the price of fruit muesli is lowered to $e_2 = 30 - 0.1\,b$ euros if $b$ bags are to be sold (demand). For which $b$ is the profit from fruit muesli alone largest?

$G(b) = (30 - 0.1 b - 22.5) \cdot b - 250 = 7.5 b - 0.1 b^2 - 250$, $G'(b) = 7.5 - 0.2 b = 0$: $b = 37.5$, so 37 or 38 bags, $G \approx 390.63 - 250 = 140.6$ euros.

EXCURSIONONE MATRIX FOR A WHOLE ECONOMY

Wassily Leontief

Wassily Leontief was born in Munich in 1905, grew up in Saint Petersburg, earned his doctorate in Berlin and went to the USA in 1931. In 1936 he published a table in which the economy of the United States was split into many sectors, with all the deliveries between them. To calculate with it, you had to solve large systems of equations, a task for the first calculating machines.

Leontief received the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences in 1973 for input-output analysis. Today statistical offices, in Germany the Federal Statistical Office, regularly publish such tables. With them one estimates, for example, how many jobs or how much CO₂ depend on an additional demand.

Source: Wassily Leontief: Quantitative Input and Output Relations in the Economic System of the United States. The Review of Economic Statistics 18 (3), 1936, pp. 105–125 · Nobel Prize archive, Economic Sciences 1973.
