Calculating with decimals and quantities

What does the shopping cost, how much change do you get, how long is a piece of board, what is your average mark? In this chapter you add and subtract decimals, multiply and divide them, work out an average and calculate with money, length, mass and time.

WHAT YOU WILL LEARN IN THIS CHAPTER
  • add and subtract decimals in columns: decimal point under decimal point
  • multiply and divide by 10, 100, 1,000 and by one-digit numbers
  • work out the average, for example an average mark
  • write quantities in different ways and convert them
  • check results with an estimate and give them sensibly accurately

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Adding and subtracting

Lena buys an exercise book for €1.49, a pen for €2.75 and a rubber for €0.80. She pays with a €10 note. How much change does she get? With money, euros go with euros and cents with cents, that is ones with ones and hundredths with hundredths.

You add and subtract decimals in columns just like natural numbers. The only important thing is that the same places are one below the other: decimal point under decimal point. Fill missing places after the decimal point with zeros; then you can see everything better.

An estimate with whole numbers protects you from putting the decimal point in the wrong place: $12.75 + 8.4 \approx 13 + 8 = 21$.

KEY POINT · DECIMAL POINT UNDER DECIMAL POINT

Write the numbers one below the other so that the decimal points line up. Fill missing places with zeros.

Calculate as with natural numbers and put the decimal point in the result exactly below the others: $12.75 + 8.40 = 21.15$.

Change in your head: from €5.04 to €6, €0.96 is missing, from €6 to €10 another €4. Together €4.96.

Typical mistake: lining the numbers up on the right. Then tenths end up under hundredths.

UNIT 25 LESSONS

2Multiplying, dividing and the average

An exercise book costs €1.35. Six exercise books cost $6 \times \text{€}1.35 = \text{€}8.10$. And ten exercise books? You do not even have to calculate, the decimal point just moves: €13.50.

When you multiply by 10, 100, 1,000, every digit becomes worth 10, 100 or 1,000 times as much. It moves 1, 2 or 3 places to the left; in short you say: the decimal point moves to the right. When you divide by 10, 100, 1,000, it moves to the left.

Multiplying by a one-digit number: first calculate without the decimal point, then the result gets as many places after the decimal point as the decimal had. Dividing by a one-digit number: divide in writing and put the decimal point in the result as soon as you pass the decimal point while dividing.

You find the average (the arithmetic mean) of several values like this: add all the values and divide by how many there are.

KEY POINT · MULTIPLYING, DIVIDING, AVERAGE

By 10, 100, 1,000: $24.5 \times 1{,}000 = 24{,}500$ (decimal point 3 places to the right)  ·  $24.5 \div 1{,}000 = 0.0245$ (3 places to the left)

By one-digit numbers: $2.4 \times 6$: $24 \times 6 = 144$, one place after the decimal point, so $14.4$.   $2.4 \div 6 = 0.4$, check $0.4 \times 6 = 2.4$.

Average = sum of all the values ÷ number of values

Adding zeros: $7.5 \div 100$: the decimal point moves two places to the left, and for that you need zeros at the front: 0.075.

Average mark: it is often a decimal, even if all the marks are whole numbers. In German schools the marks go from 1 (very good) to 6.

INTERACTIVE · THE AVERAGE

EXAMPLE 3 · MULTIPLYING WITH A DECIMAL POINT

A four-pack of yoghurts costs €3.75. What do 4 such packs cost?

Without the decimal point: $375 \times 4 = 1{,}500$. Two places after the decimal point: 15.00.

$3.75 \times 4 = 15.00$, so €15. Estimate: $4 \times 4 = 16$.

EXAMPLE 4 · AVERAGE MARK

In five tests Ali got the marks 2, 3, 1, 4 and 2. What is his average?

Sum: $2 + 3 + 1 + 4 + 2 = 12$. Number of marks: 5. Average: $12 \div 5 = 2.4$.

The average mark is 2.4.

UNIT 35 LESSONS

3Quantities and units

For a cake you need 0.5 kg of flour, 125 g of butter and 0.25 l of milk. There is 1 kg of flour in the packet. How much is left over? To be able to calculate, you need the same unit: $1{,}000\text{ g} - 500\text{ g} = 500\text{ g}$.

A quantity consists of a number and a unit: 2.5 m, 750 g, 45 min. You can write the same quantity in different ways: $\text{€}4\ 3\text{c} = \text{€}4.03$, $1\text{ m}\ 20\text{ cm} = 1.2\text{ m}$, $1\text{ kg}\ 20\text{ g} = 1.02\text{ kg}$.

The prefixes give away the conversion: kilo means a thousand, deci a tenth, centi a hundredth, milli a thousandth. A centimetre is a hundredth of a metre, a milligram a thousandth of a gram. Only with time do you calculate with 60 and 24.

KEY POINT · CONVERTING UNITS

Length: 1 km = 1,000 m · 1 m = 10 dm = 100 cm = 1,000 mm

Mass: 1 t = 1,000 kg · 1 kg = 1,000 g · 1 g = 1,000 mg

Time: 1 d = 24 h · 1 h = 60 min · 1 min = 60 s  ·  Money: €1 = 100 cents

Into the smaller unit: multiply. Into the larger unit: divide. $0.5\text{ h} + 15\text{ min} = 30\text{ min} + 15\text{ min} = 45\text{ min} = 0.75\text{ h}$

Time is different: 1.5 h is 1 h 30 min, not 1 h 50 min.

Sensibly accurate: you give the length of a walk in km with at most one place after the decimal point, not to the nearest millimetre.

EXAMPLE 6 · THE LORRY

A lorry may carry 7.5 t. It has already been loaded with 12 pallets of 480 kg each. How much more may be added?

$12 \times 480\text{ kg} = 5{,}760\text{ kg} = 5.76\text{ t}$. Then $7.50\text{ t} - 5.76\text{ t} = 1.74\text{ t}$.

Another 1.74 t, that is 1,740 kg, may be added.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

At the baker’s: Two loaves at €2.85 each and three bread rolls at €0.45 each. You pay with €10. How much change do you get?

Loaves $2 \times 2.85 = 5.70$, rolls $3 \times 0.45 = 1.35$, together €7.05. Change: €2.95.

Relay: Four runners each run 100 m, in 13.2 s, 12.85 s, 13.07 s and 12.9 s. How long does the relay team take altogether? How long did each runner take on average?

$13.20 + 12.85 + 13.07 + 12.90 = 52.02$ s. Average: $52.02 \div 4 = 13.005$ s, so about 13.0 s.

A pile of paper: One sheet is 0.1 mm thick. How high is a pile of 500 sheets? Of 10,000 sheets?

$500 \times 0.1\text{ mm} = 50\text{ mm} = 5\text{ cm}$. $10{,}000 \times 0.1\text{ mm} = 1{,}000\text{ mm} = 1\text{ m}$.

Rounding sensibly: Three friends share €10. For $10 \div 3$ the calculator shows the number 3.333333333. How much does each of them really get?

Rounded to the nearest cent, €3.33. But three times €3.33 is only €9.99. The remaining cent goes to one of them: twice €3.33 and once €3.34.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

From the ell to the metre

Before there was the metre, people in Saxony measured with the ell, the foot and the inch. A Dresden ell was about 56.6 cm long; in Leipzig, Berlin or Munich yet other ells applied. Anyone who traded in cloth had to convert all the time, and not always honestly.

In France a new measure was fixed after the Revolution: the metre was to be the ten-millionth part of the distance from the North Pole to the equator. With it came the litre and the kilogram and the prefixes kilo, deci, centi and milli. On 17 August 1868 the North German Confederation, to which Saxony also belonged, passed a new law on weights and measures. Since 1 January 1872 the metric system has applied throughout Germany.

Try it yourself: A trader sold 12 Dresden ells of cloth. About how many metres is that? Use 1 ell = 56.6 cm.

$12 \times 56.6\text{ cm} = 679.2\text{ cm} \approx 6.79\text{ m}$. About 6.8 m of cloth.

Sources: Deutsches Historisches Museum, LeMO, chapter “Vereinheitlichungen” (dhm.de/lemo): Maß- und Gewichtsordnung (law on weights and measures) of 17 August 1868, in force from 1 January 1872.
