Representing fractions

Three pizzas for four people: the numbers 0, 1, 2, 3 … will not get you any further. Each person gets $\tfrac34$ of a pizza. In this chapter you learn what fractions are, how to show them on the number line, how to expand and simplify them, how to write them as decimals and how to put them in order of size.

UNIT 15 LESSONS

1Sets and fractions

With the natural numbers 0, 1, 2, 3 … you can count. But $3 : 4$ has no result there. That is why we need new numbers: the fractions. $3 : 4 = \tfrac34$ – a fraction is a division you do not have to work out.

Things that belong together are collected in a set, written in curly brackets: $T = \{1, 2, 3, 6\}$ is the set of the factors of 6. The things in the set are called its elements.

If every element of one set is also in a second set, the first set is a subset of the second. The natural numbers are a subset of the fractions, because every natural number can be written as a fraction: $5 = \tfrac51$.

KEY POINT · SETS AND NUMBERS

A set is written with curly brackets: $\{2, 4, 6\}$. Its parts are called elements.

$A$ is a subset of $B$ if every element of $A$ is also in $B$.

Every natural number is also a fraction. With fractions every division works, except division by 0.

Zero counts as a natural number at school.

The same, just written differently: $\tfrac{12}{4}$ and 3 are the same number.

INTERACTIVE · NATURAL OR ONLY A FRACTION?

Decide for each number where it belongs. It then lands in the set diagram.

EXAMPLE 1 · SETS OF FACTORS

Write down the set of the factors of 12. Is $\{2, 3\}$ a subset of it?

$12 = 1 \cdot 12 = 2 \cdot 6 = 3 \cdot 4$, so $T_{12} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\}$.

2 and 3 are both in it: $\{2, 3\}$ is a subset.

EXAMPLE 2 · SHARING FAIRLY

5 litres of juice are shared equally between 4 bottles. How much goes into each bottle?

$5 : 4 = \tfrac54 = 1\tfrac14$

Each bottle gets $1\tfrac14$ litres, that is 1.25 litres.

UNIT 25 LESSONS

2Number line, expanding and simplifying

Half a pizza, two quarters or four eighths: it is always the same amount of pizza, just cut differently. The fractions $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac24$ and $\tfrac48$ are equal. On the number line they are at the same place.

On the number line you divide the distance from 0 to 1 into as many equal parts as the denominator says. The numerator says how many parts you go from 0.

Expanding means multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the same number. Simplifying means dividing the numerator and the denominator by the same number. Only the division into parts changes, never the value of the fraction.

KEY POINT · EXPANDING AND SIMPLIFYING

Expanding by 3: $\tfrac{2}{5} = \tfrac{2 \cdot 3}{5 \cdot 3} = \tfrac{6}{15}$

Simplifying by 4: $\tfrac{12}{16} = \tfrac{12 : 4}{16 : 4} = \tfrac{3}{4}$

A fraction is fully simplified when the numerator and the denominator have no common factor other than 1. You can do it in one step with the highest common factor.

Mixed number: $\tfrac74 = 1\tfrac34$ – one whole and three quarters.

Careful: always multiply or divide the numerator and the denominator by the same number. Changing only the top or only the bottom changes the value.

INTERACTIVE · EXPANDING AND SIMPLIFYING

EXAMPLE 3 · EXPANDING TO A GIVEN DENOMINATOR

Expand $\tfrac34$ to the denominator 20.

From 4 to 20: $20 : 4 = 5$, so expand by 5.

$\tfrac34 = \tfrac{3 \cdot 5}{4 \cdot 5} = \tfrac{15}{20}$

EXAMPLE 4 · SIMPLIFYING FULLY

Simplify $\tfrac{18}{24}$ fully.

Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18. Of these, 1, 2, 3 and 6 also divide 24. The highest is 6.

$\tfrac{18}{24} = \tfrac{18 : 6}{24 : 6} = \tfrac34$

UNIT 35 LESSONS

3Fraction and decimal

The receipt says 0.75 kg of cherries. That is three quarters of a kilo: $\tfrac34 = \tfrac{75}{100} = 0.75$. Decimals are fractions with the denominator 10, 100, 1000 …, just written differently.

There are two ways to turn a fraction into a decimal: expanding to 10, 100 or 1000 – or dividing the numerator by the denominator.

Sometimes the division never stops: $1 : 3 = 0.333\ldots$ The remainder is always 1. Such decimals are called recurring. The repeating sequence of digits is called the period; you write a bar over it: $0.\overline{3}$.

KEY POINT · CONVERTING

Fraction → decimal: expand to 10, 100, 1000 … or numerator : denominator.   $\tfrac{3}{8} = 3 : 8 = 0.375$

Decimal → fraction: count the decimal places, then simplify.   $0.45 = \tfrac{45}{100} = \tfrac{9}{20}$

The decimal is terminating if the simplified denominator has no prime factors other than 2 and 5. Otherwise it is recurring: $\tfrac23 = 0.\overline{6}$, $\tfrac56 = 0.8\overline{3}$.

To learn by heart: $\tfrac12 = 0.5$ · $\tfrac14 = 0.25$ · $\tfrac34 = 0.75$ · $\tfrac15 = 0.2$ · $\tfrac18 = 0.125$ · $\tfrac13 = 0.\overline{3}$

The calculator rounds: $2 : 3$ shows 0.6666667. The correct value is $0.\overline{6}$.

INTERACTIVE · TERMINATING OR RECURRING?

EXAMPLE 5 · EXPANDING

EXAMPLE 6 · DIVIDING

Write $\tfrac{5}{11}$ as a decimal.

UNIT 45 LESSONS

4Comparing, ordering and rounding

Which is more: $\tfrac35$ or $\tfrac23$ of a bar of chocolate? With different denominators you cannot see it straight away. If you give the fractions a common denominator, it becomes easy: $\tfrac{9}{15}$ against $\tfrac{10}{15}$.

Fractions with the same denominator are compared by their numerators. If they have different denominators, you expand both to a common denominator – or you turn both into decimals and compare them digit by digit.

When rounding, you only look at the first digit that is dropped: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 – round down; 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 – round up.

KEY POINT · COMPARING

Same denominator: the larger numerator wins. $\tfrac{5}{8} > \tfrac{3}{8}$

Same numerator: the smaller denominator wins (larger pieces). $\tfrac{2}{5} > \tfrac{2}{7}$

Otherwise: find a common denominator or turn them into decimals. The further to the right on the number line, the larger.

More digits does not mean larger: $0.7 > 0.699$. Fill up with zeros: 0.700 against 0.699.

Comparing with one half: $\tfrac{4}{7}$ is more than half, because 4 is more than half of 7.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Simon Stevin and decimals

For a long time, merchants in Europe calculated only with ordinary fractions – with denominators like 12, 16 or 60. That was laborious. In 1585 the Flemish engineer Simon Stevin published a small booklet called “De Thiende” (“The Tenth”). In it he showed how convenient calculating is if you only use tenths, hundredths and thousandths.

Stevin still wrote the places with small circles and digits above them, for example “27 ⓪ 8 ① 4 ② 7 ③” for 27.847. The decimal comma and the decimal point only came later. He even demanded that coins, measures and weights be divided up in the decimal system – an idea that became reality with the metre about 200 years later.

Try it yourself: Write Stevin’s number “3 ⓪ 0 ① 7 ② 5 ③” as a decimal and as a simplified fraction.

3.075 $= 3\tfrac{75}{1000} = 3\tfrac{3}{40}$

Source: Simon Stevin, “De Thiende”, Leiden 1585 (in French as “La Disme” in the same year). The metric system was introduced in France from 1795.
