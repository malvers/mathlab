UNIT 15 LESSONS

1Adding and subtracting

Half a litre is two quarters of a litre. Together with a quarter of a litre of cream, that makes three quarters: $\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac24 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34$. You can only add pieces of the same size.

If two fractions have the same denominator (like fractions), you only add or subtract the numerators. The denominator stays. If they are unlike fractions, you first give them the same denominator – best of all the smallest one (the lowest common denominator).

With decimals, you write the decimal points under each other and calculate as with natural numbers. An estimate with rounded numbers shows whether the result can be right.

KEY POINT · PLUS AND MINUS

Same denominator: $\tfrac{2}{7} + \tfrac{3}{7} = \tfrac{5}{7}$   add the numerators, keep the denominator.

Different denominators: first find a common denominator. $\tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{8}{12} - \tfrac{3}{12} = \tfrac{5}{12}$

Decimals: decimal point under decimal point, fill missing places with zeros: $5.4 - 2.75 = 5.40 - 2.75 = 2.65$

Common mistake: $\tfrac12 + \tfrac13$ is not $\tfrac25$. Denominators are never added.

Mixed numbers: add the wholes and the fractions separately: $2\tfrac12 + 1\tfrac14 = 3\tfrac34$.

2Multiplying and dividing

“Half of a third” is $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac13 = \tfrac16$. With fractions, the word “of” means times. And how many times does half a litre go into three litres? Six times: $3 : \tfrac12 = 6$.

You multiply two fractions by working out numerator times numerator and denominator times denominator. Simplifying first saves work.

You divide by a fraction by multiplying by its reciprocal. You get the reciprocal by swapping the numerator and the denominator: the reciprocal of $\tfrac23$ is $\tfrac32$.

With decimals, you first calculate without the decimal point. The result gets as many decimal places as both factors together.

KEY POINT · TIMES AND DIVIDED BY

Times: $\tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{4}{5} = \tfrac{2 \cdot 4}{3 \cdot 5} = \tfrac{8}{15}$   ·   Divided by: $\tfrac{3}{4} : \tfrac{2}{5} = \tfrac{3}{4} \cdot \tfrac{5}{2} = \tfrac{15}{8}$

Multiplying decimals: $0.6 \cdot 0.12$: $6 \cdot 12 = 72$, 1 + 2 = 3 places, so 0.072.

Dividing decimals: move the decimal point the same number of places in both numbers until the divisor is a whole number: $3.6 : 0.4 = 36 : 4 = 9$.

Less than 1: if you multiply by a number between 0 and 1, you get a smaller result: $8 \cdot 0.5 = 4$.

Times 10, 100, 1000: the decimal point moves 1, 2, 3 places to the right. Divided by 10, 100, 1000: to the left.

INTERACTIVE · THE DECIMAL POINT MOVES

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Where does the fraction bar come from?

The ancient Egyptians wrote fractions almost only with the numerator 1. Our way of writing them, with numerator, bar and denominator, comes from the Arab world: the mathematician al-Hassar from Morocco used the horizontal fraction bar in the 12th century.

It was brought to Europe by Leonardo of Pisa, known as Fibonacci. As a young man he had been in North Africa with his father, a merchant, and in 1202 he wrote the “Liber Abaci”, the book of calculation. It contains the Hindu–Arabic numerals 0 to 9 and fractions with a fraction bar – and many calculation problems for merchants.

Try it yourself: A merchant sells $\tfrac13$ of his goods on Monday and $\tfrac14$ on Tuesday. What fraction is still left?

$\tfrac13 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac{4}{12} + \tfrac{3}{12} = \tfrac{7}{12}$ sold, so $\tfrac{5}{12}$ is left.

Sources: Abu Bakr al-Hassar, book of arithmetic (Maghreb, 12th century); Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci), “Liber Abaci”, 1202, second version 1228.

TIMES

DIVIDED BY
