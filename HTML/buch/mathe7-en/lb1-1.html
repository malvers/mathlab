Percentages

“30% off”, “battery 15%”, “8 of 25 votes”: you come across percentages every day. One per cent is a hundredth – that is all there is to it. In this chapter you calculate with convenient percentages in your head, get to know the base value, the percentage amount and the percentage rate, solve the three basic problems and draw pie charts.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Per cent as hundredths

“Per cento” is Italian for “for a hundred”. So 7% means: 7 out of 100, that is $\tfrac{7}{100} = 0.07$. Percentages make shares easy to compare, because they are all brought to the same denominator 100.

Every fraction can be written as a percentage: expand or simplify to the denominator 100 – or first turn it into a decimal and then move the decimal point two places to the right.

Some percentages come up so often that you know them by heart. With them you calculate in your head: 25% of €48 is a quarter of it, so €12.

KEY POINT · PER CENT

$1\% = \tfrac{1}{100} = 0.01$   ·   $p\% = \tfrac{p}{100}$

Convenient percentages: $50\% = \tfrac12$ · $25\% = \tfrac14$ · $75\% = \tfrac34$ · $20\% = \tfrac15$ · $10\% = \tfrac{1}{10}$ · $1\% = \tfrac{1}{100}$

$100\%$ is the whole.

Quick calculating: 10% of 340 is 34, so 5% is half of that: 17.

Per mille: ‰ means “out of a thousand”. $1\,‰ = \tfrac{1}{1000} = 0.1\%$.

INTERACTIVE · HUNDRED SQUARE AND PERCENTAGE STRIP

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Base value, percentage amount, percentage rate

“18% of the 250 students cycle.” There are three quantities in this: the whole (250), the share as a percentage (18%) and the part you are looking for. They have fixed names.

The base value $G$ is the whole, that is 100%. The percentage rate $p\%$ gives the share. The percentage amount $W$ is the part that belongs to this share.

You find the percentage amount with the rule of three: divide from 100% down to 1%, then multiply up to $p\%$. The formula is quicker.

KEY POINT · PERCENTAGE AMOUNT

Base value $G$: the whole (100%) · percentage rate $p\%$ · percentage amount $W$: the part

$W = G \cdot \dfrac{p}{100}$

Rule of three: $100\% \to G$   ·   $1\% \to G : 100$   ·   $p\% \to G : 100 \cdot p$

Over 100%: the percentage amount can be bigger than the base value. 150% of 40 is 60.

Check with an estimate: 18% is just under a fifth. A fifth of 250 is 50 – the result has to be a little smaller.

EXAMPLE 3 · WITH THE RULE OF THREE

18% of the 250 students cycle. How many is that?

100% → 250   ·   1% → 2.5   ·   18% → $2.5 \cdot 18 = 45$

45 students

EXAMPLE 4 · WITH THE FORMULA

The class trip costs €420. A deposit of 35% is due. How big is the deposit?

$W = 420 \cdot \dfrac{35}{100} = 4.2 \cdot 35 = 147$

€147

A football stadium has 12,000 seats. At the match 85% are taken. How many seats stay empty?

Taken: $12{,}000 \cdot 0.85 = 10{,}200$. Empty: 1800 seats (that is the other 15%).

Milk contains about 3.5% fat. How much fat is in 2 litres? Calculate with 1 litre of milk ≈ 1000 g.

2 litres ≈ 2000 g, $2000 \cdot 0.035 = 70$ g of fat.

Lena says: “5% of 80 is just as much as 80% of 5.” Is that true? Give a reason and find an example of your own.

Yes: $80 \cdot \tfrac{5}{100} = \tfrac{400}{100} = 4$ and $5 \cdot \tfrac{80}{100} = \tfrac{400}{100} = 4$. You may swap the factors; for example 4% of 50 is the same as 50% of 4, namely 2.

UNIT 34 LESSONS

3Calculating the percentage rate and the base value

Mia scores 12 of 20 shots, Tom 14 of 25. Who is better? To decide, you convert both shares into percentages: Mia $\tfrac{12}{20} = 60\%$, Tom $\tfrac{14}{25} = 56\%$. Mia was better, although she has fewer hits.

There are three basic problems: once $W$ is required, once $p\%$, once $G$. The other two quantities are given each time. Read the problem carefully: what is the whole? What is the part?

If the base value is required, you know a part and its share. With the rule of three you go from the share to 1% and then to 100%.

KEY POINT · THE THREE BASIC PROBLEMS

Percentage amount: $W = G \cdot \dfrac{p}{100}$   ·   Percentage rate: $p\% = \dfrac{W}{G}$   ·   Base value: $G = W \cdot \dfrac{100}{p}$

Rule of three for the base value: $p\% \to W$   ·   $1\% \to W : p$   ·   $100\% \to W : p \cdot 100$

Comparing fairly: with different base values, the absolute numbers say little. Percentages make the comparison fair.

Check: with the base value you have calculated, you must get the percentage amount again.

EXAMPLE 5 · PERCENTAGE RATE

In the test, Paul gets 12 of 48 questions wrong. What percentage is that?

$p\% = \dfrac{12}{48} = \dfrac{1}{4}$

25%

EXAMPLE 6 · BASE VALUE

30% of the students at a school play an instrument. That is 54. How many attend the school?

30% → 54   ·   1% → 1.8   ·   100% → 180. Check: 30% of 180 is 54 ✓

180

UNIT 44 LESSONS

4Pie charts

A pie chart shows at a glance how a whole is divided up: the whole circle is 100%, each piece a share. Half a circle is one half, a quarter circle 25%.

The full angle has 360°. Because $360 : 100 = 3.6$, 1% corresponds to an angle of 3.6°. For $p\%$ you multiply the percentage rate by 3.6.

This is how you draw a pie chart: calculate the shares as percentages, work out each angle, mark them one after the other with a set square, label them. Check: all the angles together add up to 360°.

KEY POINT · PIE CHART

$100\% \to 360^\circ$   ·   $1\% \to 3.6^\circ$   ·   angle $= p \cdot 3.6^\circ$

Back: percentage rate $=$ angle $: 3.6^\circ$

Head instead of calculator: 25% → 90°, 50% → 180°, 10% → 36°, 5% → 18°.

Bar or pie? A pie chart fits when parts of a whole are shown. For changes over time you use bars or lines.

INTERACTIVE · THE WAY TO SCHOOL AS A PIE CHART

30 people asked: 12 come by bus, 9 by bike, 6 on foot, 3 by car. Calculate the angles.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

Of 600 raffle tickets, 15% are prizes. How many blanks are there?

Prizes: 90. Blanks: 510 (85%).

In a class of 25 people, 60% have a pet. Of these, 40% have a dog. How many have a dog? What percentage of the whole class is that?

Pet: 15. Dog: 40% of 15 = 6. $\tfrac{6}{25} = 24\%$ of the class.

A newspaper writes: “The number of cyclists has risen from 200 to 250 – an increase of 50%.” Check the statement.

Wrong. The increase is 50, relative to the old value 200: $\tfrac{50}{200} = 25\%$. The base value is always the starting value.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

How the per cent sign came about

Italian merchants already calculated with shares “per cento”, for a hundred, in the Middle Ages. They abbreviated it: “per 100”, “p 100” or “p cento”.

In an Italian manuscript on arithmetic from around 1425, a sign of its own appears for it for the first time: a “p” and a “c” with a little loop, just as in Italian “secondo” is abbreviated as “2°”. As it was copied again and again, the c with the loop gradually turned into a little fraction bar with two circles, and by around 1650 it read “per $\tfrac{o}{o}$”. Later the “per” was dropped and the bar became slanted: %.

So the sign has nothing to do with zero – it is a worn-down abbreviation. In the same way ‰ later came about for “per mille”, for a thousand.

Source: Florian Cajori, “A History of Mathematical Notations”, volume 1, Chicago 1928, § 274, after a find by David Eugene Smith (1908).
