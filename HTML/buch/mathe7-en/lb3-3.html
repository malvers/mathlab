UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Setting up terms and equations

For $n$ squares in a row you need $3n + 1$ matchsticks: the first match on the left, then 3 more for each square. $3n + 1$ is a term, $n$ a variable. If you substitute a number, you get the value of the term: for $n = 10$ it is 31.

A term is an expression made of numbers, variables and operation signs. When you substitute, the order of operations applies. Negative numbers are substituted in brackets: for $x = -2$, $3x + 4$ becomes $3 \cdot (-2) + 4 = -2$.

If you connect two terms with an equals sign, you get an equation. Many word problems become easier if you first call the number you are looking for $x$ and translate the text bit by bit.

KEY POINT · TERMS AND EQUATIONS

Term: an expression with numbers and variables, for example $3x + 4$ · value of the term: the result after substituting

Equation: two terms with “=”, for example $3x - 5 = 16$

Translating: double → $2x$ · decreased by 5 → $- 5$ · half → $x : 2$ · the sum of $x$ and 3 → $(x + 3)$

Multiplication dot: between a number and a variable it is left out: $3x = 3 \cdot x$.

Brackets for negatives: $x^2$ for $x = -3$ is $(-3)^2 = 9$, not $-9$.

INTERACTIVE · THE CALCULATION TREE OF A TERM

Substitute different numbers for $x$, negative ones too. The tree shows the order in which you calculate.

EXAMPLE 1 · VALUE OF A TERM

Calculate the value of $2x^2 - 3x$ for $x = -1$.

$2 \cdot (-1)^2 - 3 \cdot (-1) = 2 \cdot 1 + 3 = 5$

5

EXAMPLE 2 · SETTING UP

A rectangle is twice as long as it is wide and has a perimeter of 30 cm. Set up an equation for the width $b$.

Length $2b$. Perimeter: $2 \cdot 2b + 2 \cdot b = 30$, so $6b = 30$.

$6b = 30$, so $b = 5$ cm

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Solving equations and rearranging formulas

An equation is like a balance that is level. If you take the same away on both sides, or divide both sides by the same number, it stays level. That way you get to “$x = \ldots$” step by step.

You can also solve simple equations by reasoning or by working backwards. For harder ones you use equivalence transformations: add or subtract the same number on both sides, multiply or divide by the same number (not 0). At the end comes the check.

A proportion such as $x : 12 = 3 : 4$ is solved via the products: $x \cdot 4 = 12 \cdot 3$. And a formula is rearranged in exactly the same way as an equation.

KEY POINT · SOLVING EQUATIONS

Equivalence transformations: do the same on both sides: $+\,a$, $-\,a$, $\cdot\, a$, $:\, a$ (with $a \neq 0$)

Check: substitute the solution into the original equation; both sides must be equal

Proportion: $a : b = c : d \;\Rightarrow\; a \cdot d = b \cdot c$

Notation: the transformation is written on the right after a vertical bar: $3x - 5 = 16 \;\mid +5$.

Physics: from $v = \tfrac{s}{t}$ you get $s = v \cdot t$ and $t = \tfrac{s}{v}$.

INTERACTIVE · TRANSFORMING AS ON A BALANCE

Type in a transformation, for example “−3”, “:4” or “−2x”, until $x$ stands on its own.

INTERACTIVE · REARRANGING FORMULAS

Choose a formula and the quantity you are looking for. Every step is a transformation on both sides.

EXAMPLE 3 · TWO STEPS

Solve $4x + 7 = x - 8$.

$\mid -x$: $3x + 7 = -8$   $\mid -7$: $3x = -15$   $\mid :3$: $x = -5$. Check: $-20 + 7 = -13$ and $-5 - 8 = -13$ ✓

$x = -5$

EXAMPLE 4 · PROPORTION

For 4 people you need 250 g of flour. How much for 12 people?

$x : 12 = 250 : 4$, so $x \cdot 4 = 12 \cdot 250 = 3000$, $x = 750$.

750 g

On a map with a scale of 1 : 25,000, two places are 6 cm apart. How far is that in reality?

The sum of three consecutive integers is −24. What are they?

$x + (x + 1) + (x + 2) = -24$, $3x + 3 = -24$, $x = -9$: −9, −8, −7.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Where the word algebra comes from

Around 825 in Baghdad, the Persian scholar Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi wrote a textbook on solving equations. Its title contains two technical terms: al-jabr, “completing”, and al-muqabala, “balancing”.

“Completing” means: if something is subtracted on one side, you add it on both sides – exactly our $\mid +5$. “Balancing” means: the same on both sides is taken away. From “al-jabr” came our word algebra, and from the name al-Khwarizmi the word algorithm.

Al-Khwarizmi did not yet use letters like $x$. He wrote everything in words: “a thing and ten dirhams are equal to …”. Letters only came about 800 years later.

Source: Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, “Al-kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala”, Baghdad around 825.
