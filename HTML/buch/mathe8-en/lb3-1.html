The circle

Wheels, pizzas, clocks and the round rose windows of old churches: circles are everywhere. With compasses you draw not only circles but also rosettes and stars. You learn how a line can meet a circle and why every triangle in a semicircle has a right angle, and you work out the circumference and the area with one of the most famous numbers in the world: $\pi$.

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Circle and line, circle ornaments

A bicycle stands on the road. The road touches each wheel at exactly one point. A spoke, on the other hand, connects two points of the wheel, and a stick pushed through the wheel meets the tyre twice. A line can meet a circle in three ways.

KEY POINT · CIRCLE AND LINE

A circle with the centre $M$ and the radius $r$ consists of all the points that are at the distance $r$ from $M$. The diameter is twice as long as the radius.

A chord connects two points of the circle. The longest chord is the diameter.

If a line is at the distance $d$ from the centre, it is an external line ($d > r$, no common point), a tangent ($d = r$, exactly one point of contact) or a secant ($d < r$, two points of intersection).

The tangent is perpendicular to the radius to the point of contact.

Drawing a tangent: draw the radius to the point of contact you want, then the perpendicular there with the set square.

Careful: in this unit $d$ stands for the distance of the line from the centre, not for the diameter.

INTERACTIVE · EXTERNAL LINE, TANGENT OR SECANT?

Drag the line and compare its distance $d$ from the centre with the radius $r$.

INTERACTIVE · A ROSETTE WITH COMPASSES

Each petal consists of two circular arcs with the radius of the circle. Change the number of petals and switch on the construction lines.

EXAMPLE 1 · WHICH LINE IS IT?

A circle has the radius 3 cm. The lines $g$, $h$ and $k$ are at the distances 2 cm, 3 cm and 4.5 cm from the centre.

$g$: $2\,\text{cm} < 3\,\text{cm}$, secant.   $h$: $d = r$, tangent.   $k$: $4.5\,\text{cm} > 3\,\text{cm}$, external line.

$g$ cuts the circle twice, $h$ touches it, $k$ misses it.

EXAMPLE 2 · THE SIX-PETAL ROSETTE

Describe how to construct a rosette with six petals.

Draw a circle around $M$ with the radius $r$. Mark off the radius six times around the circle, starting from one point. Around each of the six points, draw an arc with the radius $r$ through $M$.

The radius fits exactly six times, because the six triangles around $M$ are equilateral.

EXERCISES

A circle has the radius 5 cm. Line $a$ is at the distance 3 cm from the centre, $b$ at 5 cm, $c$ at 7 cm. Name the lines.

$a$ secant, $b$ tangent, $c$ external line

Draw a circle with $r = 3\,\text{cm}$, a chord 4 cm long and the diameter. At the end point of the diameter, draw the tangent.

At the end point, the tangent is perpendicular to the diameter. Every other chord is shorter than the diameter of 6 cm.

Construct the six-petal rosette with $r = 4\,\text{cm}$. Then connect every second one of the six points. Which figure appears? And if you connect all six points in order?

Every second point: an equilateral triangle, with the other three points a second one, together a six-pointed star. All in order: a regular hexagon with a side length of 4 cm.

A paper cut-out: fold a round sheet in half twice and then once more, so that a narrow sector is formed. Cut a pattern into it and unfold. How many equal parts does the pattern have? What is the angle of each part?

Halved three times: 8 equal parts, each $360^\circ : 8 = 45^\circ$. Neighbouring parts are mirror images of each other, the fold lines are lines of symmetry.

From a point $P$ outside a circle, two tangents go to the circle. Why are the two segments from $P$ to the points of contact the same length?

The radii to the points of contact are the same length and perpendicular to the tangents. The figure is symmetrical about the line $PM$, so both tangent segments are the same length.

UNIT 23 LESSONS

2Thales’ theorem

Wherever you put the point $C$ on the semicircle above $\overline{AB}$, the angle at $C$ measures $90^\circ$ every time. That is no coincidence, but a theorem that can be proved. It bears the name of the Greek scholar Thales of Miletus.

KEY POINT · THALES’ THEOREM

If the point $C$ lies on the circle with the diameter $\overline{AB}$ ($C \neq A$, $C \neq B$), then the triangle $ABC$ has a right angle at $C$.

Converse: if the triangle $ABC$ has a right angle at $C$, then $C$ lies on the circle with the diameter $\overline{AB}$.

This circle is called the Thales circle above $\overline{AB}$. Its centre is the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$.

Idea of the proof: $\overline{MA}$, $\overline{MB}$ and $\overline{MC}$ are radii. The segment $\overline{MC}$ splits the triangle into two isosceles triangles.

If-then: the if part is called the hypothesis, the then part the conclusion. In the converse the two swap places.

INTERACTIVE · THE ANGLE IN A SEMICIRCLE

Drag the point $C$ around the circle. “Show the idea of the proof” shows you the two isosceles triangles.

EXAMPLE 3 · THE PROOF

Prove Thales’ theorem.

Let $M$ be the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$. Because $\overline{MA} = \overline{MC}$, the triangle $AMC$ is isosceles, and its base angles are equal: $\alpha = \gamma_1$. In the same way $MBC$ is isosceles: $\beta = \gamma_2$.

Angle sum in the triangle $ABC$: $\alpha + \beta + \gamma_1 + \gamma_2 = 180^\circ$, so $2\gamma_1 + 2\gamma_2 = 180^\circ$.

$\gamma = \gamma_1 + \gamma_2 = 90^\circ$

EXAMPLE 4 · CONSTRUCTING TANGENTS

Construct the tangents from a point $P$ outside a circle to the circle.

Find the midpoint $H$ of $\overline{PM}$ and draw the Thales circle above $\overline{PM}$. It cuts the circle at $B_1$ and $B_2$. By Thales, the angles at $B_1$ and $B_2$ are right angles: there the radius is perpendicular to $\overline{PB_1}$ and $\overline{PB_2}$.

The lines $PB_1$ and $PB_2$ are the tangents.

EXERCISES

Construct a right-angled triangle with the hypotenuse $c = 7\,\text{cm}$ and the leg $b = 4\,\text{cm}$ with the help of the Thales circle.

Segment $\overline{AB}$ of 7 cm, the Thales circle above it, a circle around $A$ with 4 cm. The point of intersection is $C$. The angle at $C$ is a right angle.

In the Thales circle above $\overline{AB}$, $C$ lies so that $\alpha = 35^\circ$. How big are $\beta$ and $\gamma$?

$\gamma = 90^\circ$, $\beta = 180^\circ - 90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ$

State Thales’ theorem and its converse, each in if-then form. Underline the hypothesis and the conclusion.

If $C$ lies on the circle above $\overline{AB}$, then $\gamma = 90^\circ$. Converse: if $\gamma = 90^\circ$, then $C$ lies on the circle above $\overline{AB}$. The hypothesis is always in the if part.

Draw a circle with $r = 2.5\,\text{cm}$ and a point $P$ that is 6 cm from the centre. Construct the two tangents from $P$ to the circle. Measure the length of the tangent segments.

Construction as in example 4. About 5.5 cm when measured. (Exactly: $\sqrt{36 - 6.25} \approx 5.45$ cm; you will learn this in Year 9 with Pythagoras’ theorem.)

A carpenter wants to check whether a half-round workpiece is exactly a semicircle. He puts the right-angled corner of a set square against the arc at different places, with its sides through the two ends. Why does this work?

By the converse of Thales’ theorem, every point at which the right angle fits exactly lies on the circle with the diameter between the ends. If the angle fits everywhere, the arc is a semicircle.

$90^\circ - 28^\circ = 62^\circ$

The hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle is 9 cm long. What is the radius of its Thales circle?

The hypotenuse is the diameter: $r = 4.5\,\text{cm}$.

UNIT 33 LESSONS

3The circumference and the number $\pi$

For every tin, every plate and every coin, the circumference is a little more than three times as long as the diameter. The quotient is always the same number, however big the circle is. It is called pi and is written with the Greek letter $\pi$.

KEY POINT · CIRCUMFERENCE OF THE CIRCLE

For every circle, $\dfrac{u}{d} = \pi \approx 3.14159$.

$u = \pi \cdot d \qquad u = 2 \cdot \pi \cdot r$

$\pi$ is not a rational number: it cannot be written as a fraction, its decimal places never end and never repeat. For calculating you use the $\pi$ key of the calculator.

Backwards: $d = \dfrac{u}{\pi}$ and $r = \dfrac{u}{2 \cdot \pi}$.

Estimate: the circumference is a good three times as long as the diameter. A wheel with a diameter of 70 cm covers a little more than 2.10 m per turn.

Rounding: only at the end, usually to two decimal places.

INTERACTIVE · π BETWEEN TWO POLYGONS

One polygon inside and one outside: the circumference of the circle lies between their perimeters. Double the vertices, as Archimedes did more than 2200 years ago.

EXAMPLE 5 · WORKING OUT THE CIRCUMFERENCE

A bicycle tyre has a diameter of 70 cm. How far does the wheel roll in one turn? How many turns does it need for 1 km?

$u = \pi \cdot 70\,\text{cm} \approx 219.9\,\text{cm} \approx 2.20\,\text{m}$. For 1000 m: $1000 : 2.199 \approx 455$.

About 2.20 m per turn, roughly 455 turns per kilometre.

EXAMPLE 6 · WORKING BACK TO THE RADIUS

A tree trunk has a circumference of 2.50 m. How thick is it?

$d = \dfrac{u}{\pi} = \dfrac{2.50\,\text{m}}{\pi} \approx 0.80\,\text{m}$

The trunk has a diameter of about 80 cm.

A circle has a circumference of 50 cm. Work out the diameter and the radius.

$d = \tfrac{50}{\pi} \approx 15.92\,\text{cm}$, $r \approx 7.96\,\text{cm}$

At the equator, the Earth has a circumference of about 40,000 km. Roughly how big is its diameter?

$d = \tfrac{40{,}000\,\text{km}}{\pi} \approx 12{,}732\,\text{km}$, so roughly 12,700 km.

A big wheel has a diameter of 50 m. One round takes 10 minutes. How far does a gondola travel in one round? How many metres is that per minute?

$u = \pi \cdot 50\,\text{m} \approx 157\,\text{m}$, so about 15.7 m per minute.

A rope lies tight around the equator. It is made 1 m longer and lifted so that it is the same distance from the ground everywhere. Can a mouse get through underneath? Calculate with $u = 2\pi r$ and think about how much the radius grows.

From $u + 1 = 2\pi \cdot (r + x)$ it follows that $1 = 2\pi x$, so $x = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\,\text{m} \approx 16\,\text{cm}$. Yes, and that is true for every circle, whether the Earth or a football.

4Area and annulus

If you cut a circle into many narrow pieces and lay them side by side alternately, you get almost a rectangle. It is as high as the radius and half as long as the circumference. The more pieces, the more exactly it fits.

KEY POINT · AREA AND ANNULUS

Area of the circle: $A = \pi \cdot r^2$, because the rectangle has the area $r \cdot \pi \cdot r$.

An annulus lies between two circles with the same centre, the outer radius $r_a$ and the inner radius $r_i$:

$A = \pi \cdot r_a^2 - \pi \cdot r_i^2 = \pi \cdot (r_a^2 - r_i^2)$

Backwards: $r^2 = \dfrac{A}{\pi}$. The square root key of the calculator then gives the radius.

Watch the square: double the radius means four times the area, not double.

Diameter given? Halve it first: with $d = 12\,\text{cm}$, $r = 6\,\text{cm}$ and $A = \pi \cdot 36\,\text{cm}^2$.

INTERACTIVE · THE CIRCLE BECOMES A RECTANGLE

Increase the number of pieces. The rectangle becomes more and more exact: height $r$, length $\pi \cdot r$.

EXAMPLE 7 · ONE BIG OR TWO SMALL PIZZAS?

One pizza with a diameter of 30 cm or two pizzas with a diameter of 20 cm each: where is there more pizza?

Big: $\pi \cdot 15^2 \approx 706.9\,\text{cm}^2$. Small: $2 \cdot \pi \cdot 10^2 \approx 628.3\,\text{cm}^2$.

The one big pizza is bigger, by about $79\,\text{cm}^2$.

EXAMPLE 8 · A WASHER

A washer has an outer diameter of 24 mm and an inner diameter of 10 mm. How big is its area?

$r_a = 12\,\text{mm}$, $r_i = 5\,\text{mm}$: $A = \pi \cdot (144 - 25)\,\text{mm}^2 = \pi \cdot 119\,\text{mm}^2 \approx 373.8\,\text{mm}^2$

The washer has about $374\,\text{mm}^2$, that is roughly $3.7\,\text{cm}^2$.

A round pond has a diameter of 8 m. A path 1 m wide is paved around it. How many square metres of paving are needed?

$r_i = 4\,\text{m}$, $r_a = 5\,\text{m}$: $A = \pi \cdot (25 - 16)\,\text{m}^2 = 9\pi\,\text{m}^2 \approx 28.3\,\text{m}^2$

What happens to the area of a circle if you double, triple or halve the radius? Give a reason with the formula.

$\pi \cdot (2r)^2 = 4 \cdot \pi r^2$: four times. Tripled: nine times. Halved: a quarter.

A lawn sprinkler waters a circular area of $200\,\text{m}^2$. How far does it spray? Use the square root key.

$r^2 = \tfrac{200}{\pi} \approx 63.66$, so $r \approx 7.98\,\text{m}$. It sprays just under 8 m.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

A circle has the radius 6 cm. Work out the circumference and the area.

$u = 12\pi\,\text{cm} \approx 37.70\,\text{cm}$, $A = 36\pi\,\text{cm}^2 \approx 113.10\,\text{cm}^2$

A square has the side length 10 cm. Inside it lies the largest possible circle. What percentage of the square does the circle cover?

$r = 5\,\text{cm}$, $A \approx 78.54\,\text{cm}^2$ of $100\,\text{cm}^2$, so about 78.5 %. That is true for every square: $\tfrac{\pi}{4} \approx 78.5\,\%$.

Connect with chapter 2.1: is the circumference of a circle proportional to the diameter? Is the area proportional to the radius? Give a reason with tables of values.

Circumference: yes, $u = \pi \cdot d$ is a proportional function with $m = \pi$. Area: no, with double the radius it becomes four times as big.

A 400 m running track consists of two straight sections of 84.39 m each and two semicircles. What is the radius of the semicircles on the inside line?

For both semicircles together, $400 - 2 \cdot 84.39 = 231.22$ m are left. That is a whole circumference: $r = \tfrac{231.22}{2\pi} \approx 36.8$ m.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

4000 years of hunting for $\pi$

In the Egyptian Rhind Papyrus (around 1650 BC) the area of a round field with the diameter 9 is worked out like this: you take away a ninth and square the rest, that is 8. This corresponds to $\pi \approx \tfrac{256}{81} \approx 3.16$.

Around 250 BC, Archimedes of Syracuse put polygons with 6, 12, 24, 48 and 96 vertices inside and around a circle and so proved $3\tfrac{10}{71} < \pi < 3\tfrac{1}{7}$. That is where the approximation $\tfrac{22}{7}$ comes from.

In the 5th century, the Chinese astronomer Zu Chongzhi found $\pi \approx \tfrac{355}{113} = 3.1415929\ldots$, correct to six decimal places. Today computers know trillions of digits. For a circle as big as the visible universe, about 40 of them are enough.

Sources: Rhind Papyrus, problem 50 (British Museum). Archimedes, Measurement of a Circle, proposition 3. J. Arndt, C. Haenel: Pi Unleashed, Springer 2001.
