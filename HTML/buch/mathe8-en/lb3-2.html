The cylinder

Food tins, pipes, advertising columns, tea lights and rain barrels have the same shape: a cylinder, sometimes hollow inside. You draw nets and oblique drawings of cylinders and work out how much sheet metal a tin needs, how much fits into it and how heavy a steel pipe is.

  • work out the curved surface and the surface area
  • work out the volume and the mass of cylinders and hollow cylinders
  • work back to the radius or the height from the volume

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Net, oblique drawing and surface area

The label cut open is a rectangle. It is as high as the tin and exactly as long as its circumference. Together with the bottom and the lid it makes up the net of the cylinder.

KEY POINT · CYLINDER

A right cylinder has two parallel circles of the same size as its base and top and a curved lateral surface. The height $h$ is the distance between the two circles.

Net: two circles with the radius $r$ and a rectangle with the sides $u = 2\pi r$ and $h$.

Representing it: seen from the front a rectangle, from above a circle. In the oblique drawing the circles become flat ellipses, hidden lines are dashed.

$A_G = \pi r^2 \qquad A_M = 2\pi r \cdot h \qquad O = 2 \cdot A_G + A_M$

Sketching an oblique drawing: first the lower ellipse, solid at the front and dashed at the back, then the two vertical edges, then the whole upper ellipse.

Without a lid: for a container open at the top, the base counts only once: $O = A_G + A_M$.

INTERACTIVE · NET AND OBLIQUE DRAWING

Switch between the net and the oblique drawing and change the radius and the height. The table works out the base, the curved surface and the surface area as you go.

EXAMPLE 1 · THE LABEL

A tin has a diameter of 7.4 cm and is 11 cm high. How big is its label if it covers the whole curved surface?

Length: $u = \pi \cdot 7.4\,\text{cm} \approx 23.25\,\text{cm}$, height 11 cm.

$A_M = 23.25\,\text{cm} \cdot 11\,\text{cm} \approx 255.7\,\text{cm}^2$

The label is about 23.3 cm long and has roughly $256\,\text{cm}^2$.

EXAMPLE 2 · THE SURFACE AREA

Work out the surface area of a cylinder with $r = 4\,\text{cm}$ and $h = 10\,\text{cm}$.

$A_G = \pi \cdot 16\,\text{cm}^2 \approx 50.27\,\text{cm}^2$, $\;A_M = 2\pi \cdot 4 \cdot 10\,\text{cm}^2 \approx 251.33\,\text{cm}^2$

$O = 2 \cdot 50.27\,\text{cm}^2 + 251.33\,\text{cm}^2$

$O \approx 351.9\,\text{cm}^2$

UNIT 23 LESSONS

2Volume and mass

A stack of coins is a cylinder. Every coin has the same base. Twice as many coins make a stack twice as high and twice the volume. As with the prism: volume equals base times height.

KEY POINT · VOLUME AND MASS

Cylinder: $V = A_G \cdot h = \pi \cdot r^2 \cdot h$

Hollow cylinder with the outer radius $r_a$ and the inner radius $r_i$: the base is an annulus, $V = \pi \cdot (r_a^2 - r_i^2) \cdot h$.

Mass: $m = \varrho \cdot V$, where $\varrho$ (rho) is the density of the material.

Backwards: $h = \dfrac{V}{\pi r^2}$ and $r^2 = \dfrac{V}{\pi h}$.

Densities in $\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^3}$: water 1, wood about 0.7, aluminium 2.7, steel 7.8.

Units: convert all lengths to cm first, then $V$ comes out in $\text{cm}^3$. $1\,\text{cm}^3 = 1\,\text{m}\ell$, $1\,\text{dm}^3 = 1\,\ell$.

INTERACTIVE · CYLINDER AND HOLLOW CYLINDER

Choose a hollow cylinder or a cylinder and a material. The radius $r$ is the outer radius, $r_i$ the inner one.

EXAMPLE 3 · WHAT FITS INTO THE TIN?

Inside, the tin from example 1 has a diameter of about 7.4 cm and is 11 cm high. How much fits into it?

$V = \pi \cdot (3.7\,\text{cm})^2 \cdot 11\,\text{cm} = \pi \cdot 150.59\,\text{cm}^3 \approx 473\,\text{cm}^3$

About 473 ml fit into it, just under half a litre.

EXAMPLE 4 · A STEEL PIPE

A steel pipe is 2 m long, has an outer diameter of 6 cm and a wall 5 mm thick. How heavy is it? ($\varrho = 7.8\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^3}$)

$r_a = 3\,\text{cm}$, $r_i = 2.5\,\text{cm}$, $h = 200\,\text{cm}$.

$V = \pi \cdot (9 - 6.25) \cdot 200\,\text{cm}^3 = 550\pi\,\text{cm}^3 \approx 1728\,\text{cm}^3$, $\;m = 7.8 \cdot 1728\,\text{g} \approx 13{,}478\,\text{g}$

The pipe weighs about 13.5 kg.

Inside, a barrel has a diameter of 60 cm and is 90 cm high. How many litres does it hold?

$V = \pi \cdot 900 \cdot 90\,\text{cm}^3 \approx 254{,}469\,\text{cm}^3$, so about 254 litres.

A hollow cylinder made of aluminium has $r_a = 4\,\text{cm}$, $r_i = 3\,\text{cm}$ and $h = 10\,\text{cm}$. Work out the volume and the mass.

$V = \pi \cdot (16 - 9) \cdot 10\,\text{cm}^3 = 70\pi\,\text{cm}^3 \approx 219.9\,\text{cm}^3$, $m = 2.7 \cdot 219.9\,\text{g} \approx 594\,\text{g}$

A cylindrical rain barrel has an inner diameter of 70 cm and is to hold 300 litres. How high must it be at least?

$h = \dfrac{300{,}000\,\text{cm}^3}{\pi \cdot 35^2\,\text{cm}^2} \approx 78\,\text{cm}$

In a cylindrical glass with an inner diameter of 6 cm, the water is 8 cm high. It is poured into a glass with an inner diameter of 4 cm. How high is it there? Calculate without a calculator.

The volume stays the same: $\pi \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = \pi \cdot 4 \cdot h$, so $h = 18\,\text{cm}$.

Annulus: $\pi \cdot (25 - 16) = 9\pi \approx 28.27\,\text{cm}^2$. For 100 cm: about $2827\,\text{cm}^3$.

Tin A has $d = 10\,\text{cm}$ and $h = 5\,\text{cm}$, tin B has $d = 5\,\text{cm}$ and $h = 10\,\text{cm}$. Which holds more? Calculate with $\pi$ as a factor.

A tin is to hold exactly 1 litre. For $r = 4\,\text{cm}$, $r = 5.4\,\text{cm}$ and $r = 7\,\text{cm}$ work out the height and the sheet metal needed, $O$, each time. What do you notice about the most economical tin?

$r = 4$: $h \approx 19.9$, $O \approx 600.5\,\text{cm}^2$. $\;r = 5.4$: $h \approx 10.9$, $O \approx 553.6\,\text{cm}^2$. $\;r = 7$: $h \approx 6.5$, $O \approx 593.6\,\text{cm}^2$. The tin with $r = 5.4\,\text{cm}$ is the most economical: its height is about the same as its diameter.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

A sphere in a cylinder: the tomb of Archimedes

Archimedes of Syracuse (around 287 to 212 BC) was particularly proud of one discovery: if a sphere fits exactly into a cylinder, it has two thirds of its volume and also two thirds of its surface area. He wished for this figure to be put on his tomb.

About 140 years later, in the year 75 BC, the Roman Cicero looked for the tomb in Syracuse. He found it overgrown with thorns, but recognised it by a small column with a sphere and a cylinder. He tells this himself in his Tusculan Disputations.

You will learn how to work out the volume of a sphere in Year 9. You can already do the cylinder: for $r = 3\,\text{cm}$ and $h = 6\,\text{cm}$, $V = 54\pi\,\text{cm}^3$, so the sphere inside it has $36\pi\,\text{cm}^3$.

Sources: Cicero, Tusculanae disputationes V, 64–66. Archimedes, On the Sphere and Cylinder, book I.
