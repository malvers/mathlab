UNIT 1ELECTIVE 1 · EXCURSION

1Complex numbers

Ten in two parts with the product 40: $x(10 - x) = 40$ leads to $x^2 - 10x + 40 = 0$ and $x = 5 \pm \sqrt{-15}$. Not a real number. If you carry on calculating anyway, the sum is $10$ and the product $25 - (-15) = 40$. The “impossible” numbers obey the rules of calculation.

As in chapter 1.1, a number set is extended because a calculation is impossible in the old one. You invent a number $\mathrm{i}$ with $\mathrm{i}^2 = -1$. With it, every quadratic equation becomes solvable. Since Carl Friedrich Gauss, such numbers have been represented as points of a plane.

KEY POINT · COMPLEX NUMBERS

The imaginary unit $\mathrm{i}$ is defined by $\mathrm{i}^2 = -1$.

A complex number is $z = a + b\,\mathrm{i}$ with the real part $a$ and the imaginary part $b$. All complex numbers form $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C}$.

Calculate as with terms, then replace $\mathrm{i}^2 = -1$: $(a + b\mathrm{i})(c + d\mathrm{i}) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)\,\mathrm{i}$.

Complex plane: $z = a + b\mathrm{i}$ is the point $(a, b)$. Absolute value $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, conjugate $\overline{z} = a - b\mathrm{i}$, $\;z \cdot \overline{z} = a^2 + b^2$.

Powers of i: $\mathrm{i}^1 = \mathrm{i}$, $\mathrm{i}^2 = -1$, $\mathrm{i}^3 = -\mathrm{i}$, $\mathrm{i}^4 = 1$, then it repeats.

In engineering, electrical engineers calculate with complex numbers to describe alternating currents. There the imaginary unit is often called $\mathrm{j}$.

2Switching algebra

Two switches one after the other: light only if both are closed. Two switches side by side: light if at least one is closed. These are exactly the words “and” and “or”. With 1 for “closed, current flows” and 0 for “open”, every circuit becomes an expression you can calculate with.

This calculation with only two values is called switching algebra or Boolean algebra. Its rules look like the ones for events: $\cup$ becomes $\vee$, $\cap$ becomes $\wedge$, the complementary event becomes negation.

KEY POINT · AND, OR, NOT

Conjunction $a \wedge b$ (AND, series circuit): only $1$ if $a = 1$ and $b = 1$.

Disjunction $a \vee b$ (OR, parallel circuit): $1$ if at least one is $1$.

Negation $\overline{a}$ (NOT): reverses the value. As a switch, a break contact that interrupts the circuit when it is pressed.

Rules: $a \wedge (b \vee c) = (a \wedge b) \vee (a \wedge c)$ · $a \vee \overline{a} = 1$ · $a \wedge \overline{a} = 0$ · De Morgan: $\overline{a \vee b} = \overline{a} \wedge \overline{b}$, $\overline{a \wedge b} = \overline{a} \vee \overline{b}$

Truth table: with $n$ switches there are $2^n$ settings, i.e. $2^n$ rows. The counting principle from chapter 2.3.

A half adder adds two binary digits $a$ and $b$. The sum digit is $s = (a \wedge \overline{b}) \vee (\overline{a} \wedge b)$, the carry $c = a \wedge b$. Set up the table and check $1 + 1 = 10$.

$00$: $s = 0$, $c = 0$ · $01$ and $10$: $s = 1$, $c = 0$ · $11$: $s = 0$, $c = 1$. So for $1 + 1$ we get carry 1 and sum 0, binary $10$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY OF COMPUTER SCIENCE

From Boole to Shannon

In 1854 the English mathematician George Boole published a book in which he captured logical thinking in rules of calculation: statements have only two values, and “and”, “or”, “not” become symbols of calculation. For more than eighty years this was regarded as a game for philosophers.

In 1937 the student Claude Shannon showed in his master’s thesis that Boole’s algebra describes exactly how relays and switches are wired. This made it possible to calculate and simplify circuits before building them. Today every processor consists of billions of such switches, in the form of transistors.

Sources: George Boole, “An Investigation of the Laws of Thought” (London 1854). · Claude E. Shannon, “A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits”, master’s thesis at MIT 1937, printed in the Transactions of the AIEE 57 (1938).
