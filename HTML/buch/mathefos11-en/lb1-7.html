Polynomial functions

If you fold a box out of a sheet of cardboard, its volume depends on the cube of the folding edge. Functions with $x^3$, $x^4$ and higher powers are called polynomial functions. You learn to calculate their zeros, to recognise their graphs by the multiplicity of the zeros and by their behaviour at infinity, and to sketch them without a calculator.

UNIT 12 LESSONS

1Zeros and factorisation into linear factors

The box has the base $(16 - 2x)(10 - 2x)$ and the height $x$: $V(x) = x(16 - 2x)(10 - 2x)$. Multiplied out, the leading term is $4x^3$, a function of degree three. The linear factors reveal the zeros $0$, $5$ and $8$. Only $0 < x < 5$ makes sense.

For higher degrees there is no simple formula for the zeros like the one for quadratic equations. With two tricks, however, you nearly always get through at school: factor out a factor and then use the zero product property, or substitute in equations with $x^4$ and $x^2$.

KEY POINT · POLYNOMIAL FUNCTIONS AND THEIR ZEROS

$f(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \ldots + a_1 x + a_0$ with $a_n \neq 0$ is called a polynomial function of degree $n$. It has at most $n$ zeros.

Factoring out: if the constant term $a_0$ is missing, you can factor out $x$: $x^3 - 4x = x(x^2 - 4)$.

Substitution for $ax^4 + bx^2 + c = 0$: set $z = x^2$, solve $az^2 + bz + c = 0$, then $x = \pm\sqrt{z}$ for every $z \geq 0$.

Factorisation into linear factors: $f(x) = a_n (x - x_1)(x - x_2) \cdots$ with the zeros $x_1, x_2, \ldots$, possibly with a remainder that has no zeros.

Careful with substitution: a negative $z$ gives no zero, because $x^2$ is never negative.

With the graphing calculator: let it calculate the zeros on the graph, even for terms that cannot be factorised.

PICTURE · THE VOLUME OF THE BOX

$V(x) = x(16 - 2x)(10 - 2x)$ with the zeros $0$, $5$, $8$. The graphing calculator finds the highest point in the sensible range $0 < x < 5$.

EXAMPLE 1 · FACTORING OUT

Determine the zeros of $f(x) = x^3 - 4x$ and factorise into linear factors.

$x(x^2 - 4) = x(x - 2)(x + 2) = 0$.

$x_1 = 0$, $x_2 = 2$, $x_3 = -2$

EXAMPLE 2 · SUBSTITUTION

Solve $x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = 0$.

$z = x^2$: $z^2 - 5z + 4 = 0$, so $z = 1$ or $z = 4$. Back again: $x = \pm 1$ and $x = \pm 2$.

$x^4 - 5x^2 + 4 = (x - 1)(x + 1)(x - 2)(x + 2)$

UNIT 21 LESSON

2Multiplicity, symmetry and behaviour at infinity

The graph in the opener belongs to $f(x) = 0.5(x + 2)(x - 1)^2(x - 3)$. At $-2$ and $3$ it cuts the $x$-axis, at $1$ it only touches it. The reason: $(x - 1)^2$ is never negative, so the sign of $f$ does not change there.

Far out, for very large or very small $x$, only the term with the highest power matters. For $f(x) = 0.5x^4 + \ldots$ both ends go up, because $x^4$ is positive for positive and negative $x$.

KEY POINT · READING THE GRAPH

Multiplicity of a zero = exponent of its linear factor. Odd: change of sign, the graph cuts the $x$-axis (flat from 3 upwards). Even: no change of sign, the graph touches the $x$-axis.

The behaviour at infinity is determined by $a_n x^n$: $n$ even and $a_n > 0$: both ends up · $n$ odd and $a_n > 0$: from bottom left to top right · for $a_n < 0$ the other way round in each case.

Symmetry: only even exponents → symmetric about the $y$-axis, $f(-x) = f(x)$ · only odd exponents → point-symmetric about the origin, $f(-x) = -f(x)$.

A constant term counts as $x^0$, i.e. as an even exponent: $x^4 + 3$ is symmetric about the axis, $x^3 + 1$ has neither of these symmetries.

Sketching: mark the zeros with their multiplicity, draw the ends, then draw through from left to right.

EXAMPLE 3 · A SKETCH WITHOUT A CALCULATOR

Sketch $f(x) = x^3 - 4x$.

Zeros $-2$, $0$, $2$, all single. Only odd exponents: point-symmetric about the origin.

Degree 3, $a_3 = 1 > 0$: from bottom left to top right, in between one arc above and one below the $x$-axis.

EXAMPLE 4 · FROM THE GRAPH TO THE EQUATION

A graph of degree three cuts the $x$-axis at $-1$, touches it at $2$ and passes through $(0, -2)$. State the equation.

Approach $f(x) = a(x + 1)(x - 2)^2$, then $f(0) = 4a = -2$.

$a = -0.5$, so $f(x) = -0.5(x + 1)(x - 2)^2$. On the right it goes down.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

The quarrel over the formula

For quadratic equations there has been a formula for centuries. For equations of degree three it was only found by Italian masters of arithmetic in the 16th century: Scipione del Ferro and, independently of him, Niccolò Tartaglia, who passed his method on to Girolamo Cardano only in return for a promise of secrecy. Cardano published it anyway, naming both of them. Since then the formula has been named after him.

A formula for degree four was soon found as well. For degree five and higher, however, there is no general formula with roots. Niels Henrik Abel proved this in 1824. That is why today we work with tricks such as factoring out and, when they do not help, with the calculator.

Source: Girolamo Cardano, “Ars Magna” (Nuremberg 1545), chapter 11: equations of the form “cube and things equal to a number”, $x^3 + px = q$.

LOOKING BACKTHE ESSENTIALS AT A GLANCE

POLYNOMIAL

$a_n x^n + \ldots + a_0$, degree $n$, at most $n$ zeros.

$V(x) = x(16 - 2x)(10 - 2x)$: degree 3

FACTORING OUT

If $a_0$ is missing, factor out $x$, then the zero product property.

$x^3 - 4x = x(x - 2)(x + 2)$

SUBSTITUTION

$z = x^2$ for $x^4$ and $x^2$, reject negative $z$.

$x^4 - 5x^2 + 4$: $z = 1, 4$, $x = \pm 1, \pm 2$

MULTIPLICITY

Odd: change of sign, cuts. Even: touches.

$(x - 1)^2(x + 2)$: touches at 1

ENDS AND SYMMETRY

The highest term decides the ends. Only even exponents: axis symmetry, only odd: point symmetry.

$-x^4 + 5x$: both ends down
