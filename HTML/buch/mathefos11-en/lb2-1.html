Chance and probability

Is the component faulty? Will the coin land on heads? Will my ticket win? Nobody can predict a single random event. But if you repeat it very often, astonishingly stable patterns appear. In this chapter you describe random experiments with sets, learn what a probability is and combine events with each other.

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Random experiments, outcomes and events

If you roll two dice, one outcome is, for example, $(2, 5)$: first a 2, then a 5. There are $6 \cdot 6 = 36$ such pairs. The question “Is the total score 7?” combines six of them. Such a combination is called an event.

Random experiments do not only occur in games. In quality control a component is fine or faulty, in a survey someone answers yes or no. What always matters is: you know all the possible results, but you do not know beforehand which one will occur.

KEY POINT · TERMS

A random experiment has several possible results, can be repeated under the same conditions, and its result cannot be predicted.

Every possible result is an outcome $\omega$. All outcomes form the sample space $\Omega$.

An event $E$ is a subset of $\Omega$. It occurs if one of its outcomes occurs. $\Omega$ is called the certain event, $\emptyset$ the impossible event.

The set of events contains all events. If $\Omega$ has exactly $n$ outcomes, there are $2^n$ events.

Multi-stage: for several throws, outcomes are written as ordered pairs or triples, for example $(H, T, H)$.

Single-stage or multi-stage? Rolling two dice at the same time is the same as rolling twice in a row.

2Relative frequency and probability

A drawing pin is not symmetrical, so you cannot work out its probability. So you throw it: in 1,000 throws it landed point up 620 times. The relative frequency $\tfrac{620}{1000} = 0.62$ is a good estimate of the probability.

After a few throws the relative frequency varies a lot. The more throws, the calmer it becomes and it settles down. For symmetrical devices such as dice and coins, you can work out beforehand the value it approaches.

KEY POINT · PROBABILITY

If $E$ occurs $H$ times in $n$ trials, $h = \tfrac{H}{n}$ is the relative frequency.

Law of large numbers: as $n$ grows, $h$ varies less and less around a fixed value, the probability $P(E)$.

Laplace experiment: if all outcomes are equally likely, $P(E) = \dfrac{|E|}{|\Omega|} = \dfrac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{number of all outcomes}}$.

Always: $0 \leq P(E) \leq 1$, $P(\Omega) = 1$, and for mutually exclusive events $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$. From this it follows that $P(\overline{E}) = 1 - P(E)$.

Axioms: in 1933 Andrey Kolmogorov laid down the three rules at the end of the key-point box as the foundation of the whole of probability theory.

No memory: after red five times in roulette, black is not “due”. The law of large numbers does not even things out, it dilutes them.

INTERACTIVE · THE LAW OF LARGE NUMBERS

Choose an experiment and run it 1, 10, 100 or 1,000 times. The curve shows the relative frequency, the dashed line the probability.

EXAMPLE 3 · LAPLACE WITH TWO DICE

Calculate the probabilities of “total score 7”, “doubles” and “total score at least 10”.

All 36 pairs are equally likely. Total score 7: 6 pairs, $P = \tfrac{6}{36} = \tfrac16$. Doubles: 6 pairs, also $\tfrac16$.

At least 10: totals 10, 11, 12 with $3 + 2 + 1 = 6$ pairs: $\tfrac{6}{36} = \tfrac16$.

EXAMPLE 4 · FROM PRODUCTION

Of 200 components checked, 7 are faulty. Estimate the probability of a faulty part. How many faulty parts can be expected among 5,000?

$h = \tfrac{7}{200} = 0.035$, i.e. about 3.5%.

$5000 \cdot 0.035 = $ about 175 faulty parts.

3Combining events

A spinner has 12 equal sectors. $A$: “even number” has 6 sectors, $B$: “multiple of 3” has 4 sectors. How likely is “$A$ or $B$”? Not $\tfrac{6 + 4}{12}$: 6 and 12 belong to both and would be counted twice. The correct answer is $\tfrac{8}{12}$.

Events are sets, so they can be combined like sets. The word “or” becomes the union, “and” the intersection, “not” the complementary event. A Venn diagram shows at once what would be counted twice.

KEY POINT · COMBINATIONS, ADDITION RULE, DE MORGAN

$A \cup B$: “$A$ or $B$”, at least one of them occurs · $A \cap B$: “$A$ and $B$”, both occur · $\overline{A}$: “not $A$”, the complementary event

$A$ and $B$ are called mutually exclusive if $A \cap B = \emptyset$.

Addition rule: $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$

De Morgan’s laws: $\overline{A \cup B} = \overline{A} \cap \overline{B}$ (“neither $A$ nor $B$”) and $\overline{A \cap B} = \overline{A} \cup \overline{B}$ (“not both”)

Or in mathematics never means “either … or”: $A \cup B$ includes the case that both occur.

The complementary event as a shortcut: “at least one” is the opposite of “none”.

INTERACTIVE · EVENTS AS SETS

EXAMPLE 5 · THE SPINNER

$A$: “even”, $B$: “multiple of 3” on the spinner with 12 sectors. Calculate $P(A \cup B)$ with the addition rule.

$A \cap B = \{6, 12\}$. $P(A \cup B) = \tfrac{6}{12} + \tfrac{4}{12} - \tfrac{2}{12} = \tfrac{8}{12}$, so $\tfrac23$.

Check by counting: $A \cup B = \{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12\}$ has 8 elements.

EXAMPLE 6 · FOOTBALL AND VOLLEYBALL

Of 28 people, 12 play football ($F$), 9 volleyball ($V$) and 4 both. One person is chosen at random. Calculate $P(F \cup V)$ and $P(\text{neither})$.

$P(F \cup V) = \tfrac{12 + 9 - 4}{28} = \tfrac{17}{28}$.

Neither, by De Morgan $\overline{F} \cap \overline{V} = \overline{F \cup V}$: $1 - \tfrac{17}{28} = \tfrac{11}{28}$

EXERCISES

Dice: $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$, $B = \{2, 4, 6\}$. State $A \cup B$, $A \cap B$, $\overline{A}$ and $\overline{B}$.

$\{1, 2, 3, 4, 6\}$ · $\{2\}$ · $\{4, 5, 6\}$ · $\{1, 3, 5\}$

In a bakery: $A$: “the customer pays cash”, $B$: “the customer buys bread”. Describe in words: $A \cap \overline{B}$ and $\overline{A \cup B}$.

$A \cap \overline{B}$: pays cash and does not buy bread. $\overline{A \cup B}$: does not pay cash and does not buy bread.

We have $P(A) = 0.5$, $P(B) = 0.3$ and $P(A \cap B) = 0.1$. Calculate $P(A \cup B)$ and $P(\overline{A} \cap \overline{B})$.

$P(A \cup B) = 0.5 + 0.3 - 0.1 = 0.7$, by De Morgan $P(\overline{A} \cap \overline{B}) = 1 - 0.7 = 0.3$.

Check De Morgan’s first law for a dice with $A = \{1, 2\}$ and $B = \{2, 3\}$.

$\overline{A \cup B} = \overline{\{1, 2, 3\}} = \{4, 5, 6\}$. $\overline{A} \cap \overline{B} = \{3, 4, 5, 6\} \cap \{1, 4, 5, 6\} = \{4, 5, 6\}$. Equal.

Use a Venn diagram to justify: $P(A \cup B) \leq P(A) + P(B)$. When does equality hold?

The intersection is counted twice in $P(A) + P(B)$, so the sum is at least as large. Equality holds exactly when $P(A \cap B) = 0$, for example for mutually exclusive events.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

The Chevalier de Méré and the double six

The French nobleman Antoine Gombaud, known as the Chevalier de Méré, liked to bet: in four rolls of one dice, at least one six comes up. In the long run he won. Then he bet that in 24 rolls of two dice at least one double six comes up, and in the long run he lost. Yet the ratio seemed the same to him: $\tfrac46 = \tfrac{24}{36}$.

With the complementary event it is quickly explained: $1 - \left(\tfrac56\right)^4 \approx 0.518$, but $1 - \left(\tfrac{35}{36}\right)^{24} \approx 0.491$. Blaise Pascal wrote about it to Pierre de Fermat in 1654. Probability theory grew out of this correspondence. In the widget above you can run the experiment “De Méré” a thousand times yourself.

Source: Letter from Blaise Pascal to Pierre de Fermat of 29 July 1654, in which Pascal describes de Méré’s dice problem.
