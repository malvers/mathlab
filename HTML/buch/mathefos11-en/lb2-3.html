UNIT 13 LESSONS

1The counting principle and permutations

A canteen offers 3 starters, 4 main courses and 2 desserts. Each starter goes with 4 main courses, each of these combinations with 2 desserts: $3 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 = 24$ different menus. You do not have to write them down to count them.

The same principle is behind the tree diagram: the number of paths is the product of the branchings. At the cinema from the exploration the first seat has 4 possibilities, the second 3 more, the third 2, the last 1: $4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 24$ seating arrangements.

KEY POINT · COUNTING PRINCIPLE AND PERMUTATIONS

General counting principle: if a process has $k$ stages with $n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_k$ possibilities, there are $n_1 \cdot n_2 \cdot \ldots \cdot n_k$ possibilities altogether.

Permutation: $n$ different objects can be arranged in $n! = n \cdot (n - 1) \cdot \ldots \cdot 2 \cdot 1$ ways ($n$ factorial, $0! = 1$).

Permutation with repetition: if among the $n$ objects there are $k_1$, $k_2$, … identical ones, there are $\dfrac{n!}{k_1! \cdot k_2! \cdot \ldots}$ arrangements.

Factorials grow fast: $10! = 3\,628\,800$, and $70!$ is already greater than $10^{100}$.

Graphing calculator: the symbol $!$ is usually in the menu for probability (PRB or PROB).

INTERACTIVE · ALL ARRANGEMENTS

EXAMPLE 1 · CODES

An access code consists of 2 letters (26 possibilities) followed by 3 digits. How many codes are there?

Five stages: $26 \cdot 26 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 = 26^2 \cdot 10^3$.

$676\,000$ codes

EXAMPLE 2 · ANAGRAMS

How many different “words” can be made from the letters of OTTO and of BANANA?

OTTO: $\dfrac{4!}{2! \cdot 2!} = \dfrac{24}{4} = 6$ (O twice, T twice).

BANANA: $\dfrac{6!}{3! \cdot 2!} = \dfrac{720}{12} = $ $60$ (A three times, N twice).

UNIT 23 LESSONS

2Variation and combination

Gold, silver, bronze with eight runners: $8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 = 336$ possibilities. For the final, on the other hand, only who is in it counts, not in what order. Every group of three was counted $3! = 6$ times for the podium. So there are $\tfrac{336}{6} = 56$ possible finals.

The decisive question for every selection is: Does the order matter? If yes, it is called a variation; if no, a combination. The number of combinations has a name of its own: the binomial coefficient.

KEY POINT · CHOOSING $k$ FROM $n$

Variation (order matters), without repetition: $\dfrac{n!}{(n - k)!} = n \cdot (n - 1) \cdot \ldots \cdot (n - k + 1)$ · with repetition: $n^k$

Combination (order does not matter), without repetition: $\dbinom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k! \cdot (n - k)!}$, read “$n$ choose $k$”

$\binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} = 1$ · $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n - k}$ · $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n - 1}{k - 1} + \binom{n - 1}{k}$

Graphing calculator: variations without repetition with nPr, combinations with nCr. $8$ nPr $3 = 336$, $8$ nCr $3 = 56$.

Rule of thumb: roles, places, codes → order matters. Groups, teams, lottery numbers → order does not matter.

INTERACTIVE · PASCAL’S TRIANGLE

Every number is the sum of the two above it. Tap a number or use the sliders: it is the binomial coefficient $\binom{n}{k}$.

EXAMPLE 3 · LOTTERY 6 FROM 49

How many tickets are there in the German lottery “6 from 49”? How likely are six correct numbers with one ticket?

Order does not matter, no number twice: $\binom{49}{6} = 13\,983\,816$.

$P(\text{6 correct}) = $ $\tfrac{1}{13\,983\,816}$, without the bonus number.

EXAMPLE 4 · TEAM OR COMMITTEE?

From 12 people a) a team of 3 people, b) a chair, a deputy and a treasurer are chosen. How many possibilities are there in each case?

a) Combination: $\binom{12}{3} = 220$.

b) Variation: $12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10 = $ $1320$, i.e. $3! = 6$ times as many.

UNIT 33 LESSONS

3The urn model

The lottery is like drawing 6 balls out of 49 without replacement, the order does not count. A combination lock with 4 wheels is like 4 draws from 10 balls with replacement, the order counts. A great many counting problems turn into a question about an urn in this way.

Two questions decide the case: does the order matter? Is there replacement, i.e. may something be repeated? This gives four basic cases.

KEY POINT · THE FOUR BASIC CASES ($k$ DRAWS FROM $n$ BALLS)
with replacementwithout replacement
with order$n^k$$\dfrac{n!}{(n - k)!}$
without order$\dbinom{n + k - 1}{k}$$\dbinom{n}{k}$

Drawing without replacement with two kinds: if $M$ of $N$ balls are red, the probability of exactly $r$ red ones among $n$ drawn is $\dfrac{\binom{M}{r} \cdot \binom{N - M}{n - r}}{\binom{N}{n}}$.

Laplace with counting: $P = \tfrac{\text{favourable}}{\text{possible}}$, count both with the same urn model.

The rare case “without order, with replacement” describes, for example, scoops of ice cream from several flavours.

INTERACTIVE · THE URN

Set $n$, $k$, order and replacement. You see the basic case, the formula, an example and, as long as there are not too many, all the outcomes. “Draw once” marks a random one.

EXAMPLE 5 · THREE CORRECT

How likely are exactly three correct numbers in the lottery “6 from 49”?

3 of the 6 winning numbers and 3 of the 43 others: $\binom{6}{3} \cdot \binom{43}{3} = 20 \cdot 12\,341 = 246\,820$ favourable tickets.

$P = \tfrac{246\,820}{13\,983\,816} \approx$ $0.0177$, i.e. about 1.8%.

EXAMPLE 6 · A SAMPLE FROM A DELIVERY

A delivery contains 20 parts, 3 of them faulty. 4 parts are checked without replacement. How likely is none of them faulty?

$P = \dfrac{\binom{17}{4}}{\binom{20}{4}} = \dfrac{2380}{4845} \approx 0.491$.

So with probability $\approx 0.509$ at least one faulty part is noticed.

UNIT 43 LESSONS

4Bernoulli chains

When guessing in the test, each question is correct with $p = 0.25$. A path with exactly 2 correct and 3 wrong answers has the probability $0.25^2 \cdot 0.75^3$. How many such paths are there? You choose which 2 of the 5 questions are correct: $\binom{5}{2} = 10$. So $P = 10 \cdot 0.25^2 \cdot 0.75^3 \approx 0.264$.

Whenever a trial has only two results and is repeated independently with the same probability, this calculation works. It is so important that it has a name.

KEY POINT · BERNOULLI’S FORMULA

A Bernoulli experiment has exactly two results: a hit with probability $p$ and a miss with $1 - p$.

If it is repeated $n$ times independently and with the same $p$, the result is a Bernoulli chain of length $n$.

For the number $X$ of hits: $$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} \cdot p^k \cdot (1 - p)^{n - k}$$

$\binom{n}{k}$ counts the paths with exactly $k$ hits. For “at least one hit”: $P(X \geq 1) = 1 - (1 - p)^n$.

Graphing calculator: $P(X = k)$ with binompdf$(n, p, k)$, $P(X \leq k)$ with binomcdf$(n, p, k)$. The names may differ a little depending on the calculator.

Not a Bernoulli chain: drawing without replacement from a small urn, because then $p$ changes from draw to draw.

INTERACTIVE · THE TREE OF A BERNOULLI CHAIN

The bar chart shows $P(X = k)$ for all $k$. It is called the binomial distribution and comes back in Year 12.

5 questions, 4 answers each, pure guessing. How likely are exactly 2 correct?

$n = 5$, $p = 0.25$, $k = 2$: $\binom{5}{2} \cdot 0.25^2 \cdot 0.75^3 = 10 \cdot 0.0625 \cdot 0.421875$.

$\approx 0.264$

EXAMPLE 8 · TWO SIXES

A dice is rolled 10 times. How likely are exactly two sixes?

$\binom{10}{2} \cdot \left(\tfrac16\right)^2 \cdot \left(\tfrac56\right)^8 = 45 \cdot \tfrac{1}{36} \cdot 0.2326\ldots$

$\approx 0.291$

Calculate $\binom{8}{3}$ and $\dfrac{8!}{5!}$. By what factor do they differ, and why?

A spinner with $P(\text{red}) = \tfrac13$ is spun 6 times. How likely is red at most once?

$\left(\tfrac23\right)^6 + 6 \cdot \tfrac13 \cdot \left(\tfrac23\right)^5 = \tfrac{64}{729} + \tfrac{192}{729} = \tfrac{256}{729} \approx 0.351$

A password has 8 characters from 26 lower-case letters, 26 capital letters and 10 digits. A computer tries 10 billion passwords per second. What is the longest it needs?

$62^8 \approx 2.18 \cdot 10^{14}$ passwords. $\tfrac{2.18 \cdot 10^{14}}{10^{10}} \approx 21\,800$ s, i.e. just over 6 hours. Longer passwords help enormously: with 12 characters it would take thousands of years.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

A triangle with many names

In Europe the triangle of numbers from the widget is named after Blaise Pascal, who wrote a whole treatise on it in 1654 and used it for probabilities. But it was known much earlier: in China it is named after Yang Hui, and in 1303 Zhu Shijie showed it in his book “Jade Mirror of the Four Unknowns”, for the powers up to the eighth. Persian and Indian mathematicians knew it too.

The rows give the numbers $\binom{n}{k}$, i.e. the number of paths with $k$ hits in a Bernoulli chain. And they are hidden in the binomial formulas: $(a + b)^3 = 1a^3 + 3a^2b + 3ab^2 + 1b^3$, exactly the third row $1, 3, 3, 1$.

Sources: Blaise Pascal, “Traité du triangle arithmétique” (written 1654, printed 1665). · Zhu Shijie, “Siyuan yujian” (Jade Mirror of the Four Unknowns), 1303.
