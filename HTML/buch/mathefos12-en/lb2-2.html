The derivative

How steep is a curve at exactly one point? The derivative answers this for every point at once. You learn to understand it as a limit, to work it out quickly with a few rules and to use it to set up tangents and normals. At the end you can differentiate every polynomial function, powers with negative and fractional exponents as well, and composite functions such as $(2x + 1)^3$.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Derivative at a point and derivative function

For $f(x) = x^2$ the difference quotient at any point $x_0$ is: $\dfrac{(x_0 + h)^2 - x_0^2}{h} = \dfrac{2x_0 h + h^2}{h} = 2x_0 + h$. For $h \to 0$, $2x_0$ remains. So at the point 1 the gradient is 2, at the point $-2$ it is $-4$. The gradient at every point gives a new function: $f'(x) = 2x$.

The limit of the difference quotient is called the differential quotient or the derivative at the point $x_0$. If it exists, $f$ is called differentiable there. If you assign to every point its derivative, you get the derivative function $f'$. Its graph shows where $f$ increases ($f' > 0$), decreases ($f' < 0$) and is horizontal ($f' = 0$).

KEY POINT · THE DERIVATIVE

$$f'(x_0) = \lim\limits_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}$$

Geometrically: the gradient of the tangent to the graph at the point $P(x_0, f(x_0))$ · as a rate: the instantaneous rate of change

Derivative function $f'\colon x \mapsto f'(x)$. Notations: $f'(x)$, $y'$, $\tfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}$

Not differentiable everywhere: $f(x) = |x|$ has a kink at 0. From the left the gradient is $-1$, from the right $+1$; there is no limit.

With GeoGebra: a point on the graph, the tangent, the gradient as a point $(x, m)$ with trace. That is how the graph of $f'$ appears point by point, as in the widget.

EXAMPLE 1 · H-METHOD FOR X³

Calculate $f'(1)$ for $f(x) = x^3$.

$\dfrac{(1 + h)^3 - 1}{h} = \dfrac{3h + 3h^2 + h^3}{h} = 3 + 3h + h^2$

For $h \to 0$: $f'(1) = 3$

EXAMPLE 2 · H-METHOD FOR 1/X

Calculate $f'(2)$ for $f(x) = \tfrac1x$.

$\dfrac{\frac{1}{2 + h} - \frac12}{h} = \dfrac{2 - (2 + h)}{2(2 + h)\,h} = \dfrac{-1}{2(2 + h)}$

For $h \to 0$: $f'(2) = -\tfrac14$. The hyperbola is decreasing there.

EXERCISES

Use the h-method to calculate the derivative of $f(x) = 3x^2$ at the point $x_0 = 1$.

$\dfrac{3(1 + h)^2 - 3}{h} = 6 + 3h \to 6$

Show: for $f(x) = 4x + 1$ the difference quotient is always 4. What does this mean?

$\tfrac{4(x_0 + h) + 1 - 4x_0 - 1}{h} = 4$. A straight line has the same gradient everywhere, $f'(x) = 4$.

For $f(x) = x^2 - 4x$ find the derivative at a general point $x_0$. Where does the graph have a horizontal tangent?

$\dfrac{(x_0 + h)^2 - 4(x_0 + h) - x_0^2 + 4x_0}{h} = 2x_0 + h - 4 \to 2x_0 - 4$. Horizontal at $x_0 = 2$, the vertex.

A cubic function with a positive leading coefficient has a maximum at $x = -1$ and a minimum at $x = 3$. Sketch the graph of $f'$.

$f'$ is an upward-opening parabola with the zeros $-1$ and $3$: positive to the left of $-1$, negative in between, positive to the right of $3$, vertex at $x = 1$.

Calculate $f'(4)$ for $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ with the h-method.

Multiply $\dfrac{\sqrt{4 + h} - 2}{h}$ top and bottom by $\sqrt{4 + h} + 2$ (difference of two squares).

$\dfrac{(4 + h) - 4}{h\left(\sqrt{4 + h} + 2\right)} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4 + h} + 2} \to$ $\tfrac14$

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Rules of differentiation and higher derivatives

With the h-method: $(x^2)' = 2x$, $(x^3)' = 3x^2$, because $\dfrac{(x + h)^3 - x^3}{h} = 3x^2 + 3xh + h^2 \to 3x^2$, and likewise $(x^4)' = 4x^3$. The exponent moves to the front as a factor and becomes 1 smaller. That is the power rule, and with it you no longer need the h-method.

Together with three simple rules you can now differentiate every polynomial function term by term. If you differentiate the derivative once more, you get the second derivative $f''$. It describes how the gradient changes and becomes important in chapter 2.3 for curvature and points of inflection.

KEY POINT · RULES OF DIFFERENTIATION

Constant rule: $(c)' = 0$ · power rule: $(x^n)' = n \cdot x^{n - 1}$, also for negative and fractional $n$

Constant multiple rule: $(c \cdot f)' = c \cdot f'$ · sum rule: $(f + g)' = f' + g'$

Higher derivatives: $f'' = (f')'$, $f''' = (f'')'$ and so on

Rewrite first: $\tfrac{1}{x^2} = x^{-2}$, $\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}$. Then $\left(\tfrac{1}{x}\right)' = -\tfrac{1}{x^2}$ and $(\sqrt{x})' = \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$.

In physics: distance $s(t)$, velocity $v(t) = s'(t)$, acceleration $a(t) = s''(t)$.

EXAMPLE 3 · DIFFERENTIATING THREE TIMES

Find $f'$, $f''$ and $f'''$ for $f(x) = 2x^4 - 3x^2 + 5x - 7$.

$f'(x) = 8x^3 - 6x + 5$,   $f''(x) = 24x^2 - 6$,   $f'''(x) = 48x$

The constant $-7$ drops out at the first differentiation, the term $5x$ at the second.

EXAMPLE 4 · REWRITE FIRST

Differentiate $f(x) = \dfrac{4}{x^2} + 3\sqrt{x}$.

$f(x) = 4x^{-2} + 3x^{1/2}$, so $f'(x) = -8x^{-3} + \tfrac32 x^{-1/2}$.

$f'(x) = -\dfrac{8}{x^3} + \dfrac{3}{2\sqrt{x}}$

UNIT 34 LESSONS

3Chain rule, tangent and normal

You could multiply out $f(x) = (2x + 1)^3$: $8x^3 + 12x^2 + 6x + 1$, differentiated $24x^2 + 24x + 6 = 6(2x + 1)^2$. Quicker: differentiate the bracket as if it were a variable, $3(2x + 1)^2$, and multiply by the derivative of the bracket, 2. That is the chain rule.

If you know the gradient, you can set up the tangent at a point: the line through $P$ with the gradient $f'(x_0)$. The normal also passes through $P$ and is perpendicular to the tangent. You know from Year 11: perpendicular lines have gradients with $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$.

KEY POINT · CHAIN RULE, TANGENT, NORMAL

Chain rule: $\left[(ax + b)^n\right]' = n \cdot (ax + b)^{n - 1} \cdot a$, in general “outer derivative times inner derivative”

Tangent at $P(x_0, f(x_0))$: $t(x) = f'(x_0) \cdot (x - x_0) + f(x_0)$

Normal: $n(x) = -\dfrac{1}{f'(x_0)} \cdot (x - x_0) + f(x_0)$ for $f'(x_0) \neq 0$

Angle of intersection with the $x$-axis at a zero $x_0$: $\tan \alpha = |f'(x_0)|$

Graph of f and f′: $f$ increases where $f' > 0$. Horizontal tangents of $f$ are zeros of $f'$. The steepest point of $f$ is a turning point of $f'$.

If $f'(x_0) = 0$, the tangent is horizontal and the normal is the vertical line $x = x_0$.

EXAMPLE 6 · TANGENT, NORMAL, ANGLE

$f(x) = 0.5x^2 - x$ has a zero at $x_0 = 2$. Set up the tangent and the normal and calculate the angle of intersection with the $x$-axis.

$f'(x) = x - 1$, $f'(2) = 1$: tangent $y = x - 2$, normal $y = -x + 2$.

$\tan \alpha = 1$: $\alpha = 45^\circ$

Set up the normal to $f(x) = x^2$ at $x_0 = 1$. Where does it intersect the parabola a second time?

Gradient $-\tfrac12$: $n(x) = -0.5x + 1.5$. $x^2 = -0.5x + 1.5$ gives $2x^2 + x - 3 = 0$: $x = 1$ or $x = -1.5$, so $(-1.5, 2.25)$.

At what angle does the graph of $f(x) = x^2 - 4$ intersect the $x$-axis?

Zeros $\pm 2$, $|f'(\pm 2)| = 4$: $\alpha = \tan^{-1} 4 \approx 76.0^\circ$, at both points.

From the point $Q(0, -4)$ tangents are drawn to the standard parabola. At which points do they touch it, and what are their equations?

Set up the tangent at the unknown point $(a, a^2)$ and substitute $Q$.

$t(x) = 2a(x - a) + a^2 = 2ax - a^2$. Through $Q$: $-a^2 = -4$, $a = \pm 2$. Points of contact $(2, 4)$ and $(-2, 4)$, tangents $y = 4x - 4$ and $y = -4x - 4$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Newton versus Leibniz

Isaac Newton developed his “method of fluxions” as early as 1665 and 1666, but did not publish it for a long time. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz arrived at the same ideas independently and printed them first: in October 1684 in the Leipzig journal “Acta Eruditorum” under the title “Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis”, a new method for maxima and minima.

This led to one of the most bitter disputes in the history of science. In 1712 a report by the Royal Society declared Newton the inventor, and Newton had helped to write this report himself. Today it is accepted that both invented differential calculus independently of each other. The notation we calculate with, $\tfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}$ and $\int$, comes from Leibniz.

Source: G. W. Leibniz, “Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis”, Acta Eruditorum, October 1684, pp. 467–473; Royal Society, “Commercium epistolicum”, London 1712.
