Where is the summit of a bike tour, where the steepest point, where does a left bend tip over into a right bend? UNIT 12 LESSONS

1Zeros, multiplicity and symmetry

$f(x) = x^3 - 4x = x(x - 2)(x + 2)$ has the zeros $-2$, $0$ and $2$; at each of them the sign changes. $g(x) = x^2(x - 3)$ has the zeros $0$ and $3$. At 0 the factor $x$ appears twice: $g$ is negative to the left and to the right of it, the graph only touches the $x$-axis.

You find zeros by factoring out and the zero product rule, with the quadratic formula, by substitution or with the graphing calculator. How often a linear factor occurs is revealed by the shape of the graph at the zero. And from the exponents you can see straight away whether the graph is symmetric.

KEY POINT · ZEROS AND SYMMETRY

Multiplicity: odd (single, triple): the graph crosses the $x$-axis with a change of sign. Even (double): it touches it.

Symmetric about the $y$-axis: only even exponents, $f(-x) = f(x)$

Point-symmetric about the origin: only odd exponents, $f(-x) = -f(x)$

Substitution for $ax^4 + bx^2 + c = 0$: set $z = x^2$, solve the quadratic equation, go back with $x = \pm\sqrt{z}$.

Watch out for constants: a number such as $+2$ counts as $2x^0$, so it has an even exponent. That is why $x^3 + 2$ is not point-symmetric.

Guessing: with whole-number coefficients, whole-number zeros are divisors of the constant term (if it is not zero). After that, polynomial division or clever factoring out.

INTERACTIVE · ZEROS AND THEIR MULTIPLICITY

Drag the zeros along the axis and change their multiplicity. Watch when the graph crosses and when it touches, and how the factor $a$ determines the ends.

EXAMPLE 1 · SUBSTITUTION

Find the zeros and the symmetry of $f(x) = x^4 - 5x^2 + 4$.

$z = x^2$: $z^2 - 5z + 4 = 0$, $z = 1$ or $z = 4$.

$x = \pm 1$ and $x = \pm 2$, all single. Only even exponents: symmetric about the $y$-axis.

EXAMPLE 2 · FACTORING OUT

Find the zeros of $f(x) = 0.5x^3 - 2x^2 + 2x$ and their multiplicity.

$0.5x(x^2 - 4x + 4) = 0.5x(x - 2)^2$

$x = 0$ single, $x = 2$ double: at 2 the graph touches the axis. Mixed exponents: no symmetry.

UNIT 23 LESSONS

2Monotonicity and turning points

For the bike tour $h(x) = -5x^3 + 30x^2 + 200$, $h'(x) = -15x^2 + 60x = -15x(x - 4)$. On $(0, 4)$, $h' > 0$: the route goes uphill, after that downhill. At the summit the tangent is horizontal: $h'(4) = 0$. It is reached after 4 km at an altitude of 360 m.

At a maximum or minimum the tangent is horizontal, so the derivative is zero. That is necessary, but not enough: $x^3$ also has a horizontal tangent at 0 and still no extremum. Only a change of sign of $f'$ or the sign of $f''$ decides.

KEY POINT · MONOTONICITY AND EXTREMA

Monotonicity: $f'(x) > 0$ on an interval: strictly increasing · $f'(x) < 0$: strictly decreasing

Necessary condition: $f'(x_E) = 0$

Sufficient: $f'$ changes sign from $+$ to $-$ (maximum) or from $-$ to $+$ (minimum), or $f''(x_E) < 0$: maximum, $f''(x_E) > 0$: minimum

If $f'(x_E) = 0$ without a change of sign, there is a saddle point.

Local and global: a maximum is only highest in its neighbourhood. On an interval the values at the ends can be even larger or smaller.

If $f''(x_E) = 0$, the second derivative tells you nothing. Then check the change of sign of $f'$.

INTERACTIVE · TURNING POINTS WITH F′ AND F″

Switch on $f'$: where the dashed graph crosses the axis, the turning points of $f$ lie. With the sliders you will also find cases without turning points.

EXAMPLE 3 · MAXIMUM AND MINIMUM

Find the turning points and the monotonicity of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 5$.

$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9 = 3(x + 1)(x - 3)$, $f''(x) = 6x - 6$.

$f''(-1) = -12 < 0$: $H(-1, 10)$ · $f''(3) = 12 > 0$: $T(3, -22)$. Increasing for $x < -1$ and $x > 3$, decreasing in between.

EXAMPLE 4 · NO EXTREMUM

Does $f(x) = x^3$ have an extremum at 0?

$f'(0) = 0$, but $f'(x) = 3x^2 \ge 0$ on both sides: no change of sign.

Saddle point $(0, 0)$, no extremum. $f''(0) = 0$ would not have decided here.

EXERCISES

Find the turning point and the monotonicity of $f(x) = x^2 - 6x + 5$.

$f'(x) = 2x - 6 = 0$: $x = 3$, $f''(3) = 2 > 0$: $T(3, -4)$. Decreasing for $x < 3$, increasing for $x > 3$.

Find the turning points of $f(x) = -x^3 + 3x$.

$f'(x) = -3x^2 + 3 = 0$: $x = \pm 1$. $f''(x) = -6x$: $H(1, 2)$, $T(-1, -2)$

Find the turning points of $f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$.

$f'(x) = 4x(x^2 - 1)$: $x = 0$, $\pm 1$. $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 4$: $H(0, 0)$, $T(-1, -1)$, $T(1, -1)$

Investigate $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3$ for turning points. What is going on at $x = 0$?

$f'(x) = 4x^2(x - 3)$. What sign does $f'$ have just to the left and right of 0?

$x = 3$: $f''(3) = 36 > 0$, $T(3, -27)$. At 0, $f'$ is negative on both sides: no change of sign, saddle point $(0, 0)$.

For the bike tour $h(x) = -5x^3 + 30x^2 + 200$, $0 \le x \le 5$, find the highest and the lowest point of the route.

Summit $(4, 360)$. Values at the ends $h(0) = 200$ and $h(5) = 325$. The lowest point is the start at an altitude of 200 m; the local minimum at 0 lies exactly at the end of the interval.

UNIT 33 LESSONS

3Curvature, points of inflection and complete curve sketching

For the bike tour $h''(x) = -30x + 60$. Up to $x = 2$, $h'' > 0$: the gradient increases, the route is a left bend. After that it decreases, a right bend. At $x = 2$ the climb is steepest: $h'(2) = 60$ m per km, that is 6 %. The point $W(2, 280)$ is a point of inflection.

The second derivative describes how the gradient changes, and so the curvature. At a point of inflection the curvature changes; the gradient is largest or smallest there. With all the building blocks together you get a fixed scheme for the whole investigation.

KEY POINT · CURVATURE AND POINTS OF INFLECTION

$f''(x) > 0$: convex (left bend) · $f''(x) < 0$: concave (right bend)

Point of inflection: necessary $f''(x_W) = 0$, sufficient a change of sign of $f''$ or $f'''(x_W) \neq 0$

Inflectional tangent: the tangent at the point of inflection · saddle point: a point of inflection with a horizontal tangent

Scheme: 1. domain · 2. symmetry · 3. behaviour at infinity · 4. zeros · 5. turning points · 6. points of inflection · 7. table of values and graph

Memory aid: a left bend like the mouth of a smiling face ($f'' > 0$), a right bend like a sad mouth ($f'' < 0$).

Check with the graphing calculator: draw the graph, let it calculate zeros and extrema. You find points of inflection as extrema of the graph of $f'$.

PICTURE · F, F′ AND F″ ONE ABOVE THE OTHER

$f(x) = -0.25x^4 + 2x^2$ (yellow), $f'$ (light blue) and $f''$ (green). The zeros of $f'$ lie below the turning points, those of $f''$ below the points of inflection.

EXAMPLE 5 · THE COMPLETE INVESTIGATION

Investigate $f(x) = -0.25x^4 + 2x^2$ completely.

$D = \mathbb{R}$, only even exponents: symmetric about the $y$-axis. For $x \to \pm\infty$, $f(x) \to -\infty$. Zeros: $x^2(2 - 0.25x^2) = 0$, so $0$ double and $\pm\sqrt8 \approx \pm 2.83$.

$f'(x) = -x^3 + 4x$, $f''(x) = -3x^2 + 4$: $T(0, 0)$, $H(\pm 2, 4)$. $f''(x) = 0$ at $x = \pm\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3} \approx \pm 1.15$, $f'''(x) = -6x \neq 0$: $W(\pm 1.15, 2.22)$

EXAMPLE 6 · INFLECTIONAL TANGENT

Find the point of inflection and the inflectional tangent of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 2$.

$f''(x) = 6x - 6 = 0$: $x = 1$, $f'''(1) = 6 \neq 0$, $W(1, 0)$.

$f'(1) = 3 - 6 = -3$: $t(x) = -3x + 3$

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Extrema before the derivative

Pierre de Fermat, a lawyer in Toulouse, was already finding maxima and minima around 1636, fifty years before Leibniz printed the derivative. His example: a line segment of length $b$ is to be divided so that the product of the parts $x(b - x)$ is as large as possible.

Fermat replaced $x$ by $x + e$ and set the two products “approximately equal”: $(x + e)(b - x - e) \approx x(b - x)$. Multiplied out, $be - 2xe - e^2 \approx 0$ remains. He divided by $e$ and then left out $e$: $b - 2x = 0$, so $x = \tfrac{b}{2}$. Step by step, that is the h-method and the condition $f'(x) = 0$.

Source: P. de Fermat, “Methodus ad disquirendam maximam et minimam”, manuscript around 1636; French translation by P. Tannery in “Œuvres de Fermat”, volume III, Paris 1896.
