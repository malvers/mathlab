Integral calculus

The distance from the speed, the amount of water from the rate of inflow, the area under a curve from the curve itself: integral calculus retraces the path of the derivative. You learn to find antiderivatives, to approximate areas with rectangles and to calculate them exactly with the fundamental theorem, also between two graphs.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Antiderivative and indefinite integral

Which function has the derivative $2x$? $F(x) = x^2$, but also $x^2 + 1$ and $x^2 - 7$. When you differentiate, every constant disappears; when you work backwards, you do not know it. All the solutions have the form $x^2 + C$; their graphs are shifted up and down relative to each other.

A function $F$ with $F' = f$ is called an antiderivative of $f$. You find it by applying the rules of differentiation backwards: increase the exponent by 1, divide by the new exponent. The set of all antiderivatives is written as the indefinite integral.

KEY POINT · ANTIDERIVATIVES

$F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ if $F'(x) = f(x)$. All antiderivatives: $F(x) + C$ with the constant of integration $C$.

Indefinite integral: $\displaystyle\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = F(x) + C$

Basic integral: $\displaystyle\int x^n\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$ for $n \neq -1$ · constant factor and sum rule as for differentiating

Check: Differentiate the antiderivative; you must get $f$. That is always quick.

Negative exponents too: $\int \tfrac{1}{x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x = \int x^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{x^{-1}}{-1} + C = -\tfrac1x + C$. Only $\tfrac1x$ itself does not work this way.

INTERACTIVE · F AND ITS ANTIDERIVATIVES

At the top an antiderivative $F$ with a movable constant $C$ and its tangent, below $f$. The gradient of $F$ at every point is the value of $f$ there, however large $C$ is.

$F(x) = \tfrac13 x^3 - x + C$, $F(3) = 9 - 3 + C = 5$, so $C = -1$.

2The definite integral and the fundamental theorem

If the inflow rises steadily according to $r(t) = 0.5t$ (in l/min), then in ten minutes as many litres flow in as the area of the triangle under the graph: $\tfrac12 \cdot 10 \cdot 5 = 25$. Remarkably, $R(t) = 0.25t^2$ is an antiderivative of $r$, and $R(10) - R(0) = 25$. The area comes from the antiderivative.

For curved graphs you approximate the area with narrow rectangles: the lower sum lies below, the upper sum above. The narrower the strips, the closer both come to the same limit, the definite integral. The fundamental theorem tells you how to calculate it without strips.

KEY POINT · FUNDAMENTAL THEOREM OF CALCULUS

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b = F(b) - F(a)$$ for every antiderivative $F$ of a continuous function $f$

The integral is a signed area: areas above the $x$-axis count as positive, areas below as negative.

$\int_a^a f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0$ · $\int_a^b + \int_b^c = \int_a^c$ · $\int_b^a f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = -\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$

The sign: Leibniz wrote $\int$ as an elongated S for “sum”. $\mathrm{d}x$ is a reminder of the width of the infinitely narrow strips.

On the graphing calculator: let the integral command (fnInt) do the calculation. The constant $C$ always cancels out in $F(b) - F(a)$.

EXAMPLE 3 · LOWER SUM, UPPER SUM, FUNDAMENTAL THEOREM

Approximate $\int_0^2 x^2\,\mathrm{d}x$ with four strips of width 0.5 and calculate the exact value.

Lower sum $0.5(0 + 0.25 + 1 + 2.25) = 1.75$, upper sum $0.5(0.25 + 1 + 2.25 + 4) = 3.75$.

Exact: $\left[\tfrac13 x^3\right]_0^2 = \tfrac83$, about 2.67, in between.

EXAMPLE 4 · WHEN AREAS CANCEL OUT

Calculate $\int_{-1}^{2} (x^2 - 1)\,\mathrm{d}x$ and interpret the result.

$\left[\tfrac13 x^3 - x\right]_{-1}^{2} = \tfrac23 - \tfrac23 = 0$

Below the axis on $[-1, 1]$ there is a piece of size $\tfrac43$, above it on $[1, 2]$ also $\tfrac43$. They cancel each other out.

UNIT 32 LESSONS

3Areas between a graph and the axis and between two graphs

The integral of $x^2 - 1$ over $[-1, 2]$ is 0, but the area between the graph and the axis is $\tfrac43 + \tfrac43 = \tfrac83$. If you want to calculate areas, you have to split at the zeros and add the pieces with their absolute values.

Between two graphs you calculate with the difference function $f - g$. Its zeros are the intersection points of the graphs, and they form the limits. If an area is given and a limit is wanted, the fundamental theorem turns into an equation for the limit.

KEY POINT · CALCULATING AREAS

Graph and $x$-axis: find the zeros in the interval, integrate over each piece separately, add the absolute values.

Between two graphs: intersection points $x_1 < x_2$ from $f(x) = g(x)$, then $A = \left|\displaystyle\int_{x_1}^{x_2} \big(f(x) - g(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|$. With more intersection points, piece by piece.

Limit wanted: solve $F(b) - F(a) = A$ for $b$.

Sketch first: A rough graph shows which pieces lie above and which below the axis, and which graph is on top.

On the graphing calculator: integrate the absolute value straight away, $\int |f(x) - g(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x$; then you do not have to split.

INTERACTIVE · SIGNED AREA

Drag the limits $a$ and $b$. Yellow pieces lie above the axis and count as positive, red ones below and count as negative. Compare the integral and the area.

INTERACTIVE · AREA BETWEEN TWO GRAPHS

The widget finds the intersection points and integrates the difference function from one intersection point to the next.

EXAMPLE 5 · BELOW THE AXIS

Calculate the area between the graph of $f(x) = x^2 - 4$ and the $x$-axis.

Zeros $\pm 2$: $\left[\tfrac13 x^3 - 4x\right]_{-2}^{2} = -\tfrac{32}{3}$; the area lies entirely below the axis.

$A = \tfrac{32}{3} \approx 10.67$

EXAMPLE 6 · PARABOLA AND LINE

Calculate the area between $f(x) = -x^2 + 4$ and $g(x) = x + 2$.

$-x^2 + 4 = x + 2$: $x^2 + x - 2 = 0$, so $x_1 = -2$, $x_2 = 1$.

$\int_{-2}^{1} \left(-x^2 - x + 2\right)\mathrm{d}x = \left[-\tfrac13 x^3 - \tfrac12 x^2 + 2x\right]_{-2}^{1} = \tfrac76 + \tfrac{10}{3}$: $A = 4.5$

Calculate the area between $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = 2x$.

Justify: for an increasing function the lower sum with left rectangles is always too small. What holds for a decreasing one?

Increasing: the left edge of each strip is the smallest value in it, so the rectangle stays below the graph. Decreasing: the other way round, the left rectangles are too large.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Archimedes and the parabolic arc

As early as the 3rd century BC, Archimedes calculated the area under a parabolic arc, entirely without an integral. He put a triangle inside it, then two smaller triangles into the two gaps, which together are only a quarter as large, then four even smaller ones, and so on. The sum $1 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac{1}{16} + \ldots$ is $\tfrac43$.

His result: a parabolic segment is $\tfrac43$ the size of the triangle with the same base and height. For the arc $4 - x^2$ above: triangle $\tfrac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 = 8$, segment $\tfrac43 \cdot 8 = \tfrac{32}{3}$. With the fundamental theorem: $\int_{-2}^{2}(4 - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{32}{3}$. Two thousand years lie between the two calculations.

Source: Archimedes, “Quadrature of the Parabola”, Proposition 24. Explanation: MacTutor History of Mathematics, “Archimedes: Quadrature of the parabola”, University of St Andrews.
