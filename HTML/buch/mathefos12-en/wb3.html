UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Exact calculation with the CAS: families from economics

For $f(x) = ax^3 - bx$ the CAS answers Solve(Derivative(f(x)) = 0, x) with $x = \pm\sqrt{\tfrac{b}{3a}}$. The calculation is done, the thinking is not: for $a, b > 0$ there are two extreme points, for $a > 0$ and $b < 0$ there are none. With $a = 1$ and $b = 12$ they are at $\pm 2$, and the minimum is $T(2, -16)$.

A CAS calculates with terms instead of numbers: it differentiates, integrates, factorises and solves equations, even when there are parameters in them. That makes families with two parameters accessible. In economics these are, for example, the selling price and the fixed costs: how does the best number of sales depend on the price?

KEY POINT · WORKING WITH THE CAS

Commands in GeoGebra CAS: f(x) := … defines, Derivative(f(x)), Integral(f(x), a, b), Solve(equation, x), Factor(…)

Costs, revenue, profit: $G(x) = E(x) - K(x)$ with $E(x) = p \cdot x$. Break-even point and profit limit: zeros of $G$. Maximum profit: $G'(x) = 0$, i.e. $K'(x) = p$.

The CAS gives solutions for all parameters. You decide which of them make sense in context.

Exact or numerical: The CAS writes $\sqrt2$, not 1.414. With Numeric(…) or ≈ you get the decimal number.

Marginal cost: In economics $K'(x)$ is called the marginal cost, the cost of one additional item.

INTERACTIVE · COSTS, REVENUE AND PROFIT

The selling price $p$ is the parameter. Watch how the break-even point, the profit limit and the maximum profit shift with the price, and from which price on production no longer pays at all.

EXAMPLE 1 · A FAMILY WITH TWO PARAMETERS

Investigate $f_{a,b}(x) = ax^3 - bx$ with $a > 0$ for extreme points.

CAS: $f'(x) = 3ax^2 - b$, $x = \pm\sqrt{\tfrac{b}{3a}}$. For $b > 0$ one maximum and one minimum, for $b \le 0$ no extreme points.

For $a = 1$, $b = 12$: $H(-2, 16)$, $T(2, -16)$

EXAMPLE 2 · THE MAXIMUM PROFIT

$K(x) = 0.02x^3 - 1.2x^2 + 30x + 200$ (in €), selling price €30 per item. Find the break-even point, the profit limit and the maximum profit.

$G(x) = 30x - K(x) = -0.02x^3 + 1.2x^2 - 200$. Zeros with the CAS: about 14.9 and 56.9 items.

$G'(x) = -0.06x^2 + 2.4x = 0$: 40 items, $G(40) = 440$ €

In Example 2 the price rises to €40. How many items now bring the greatest profit? Use the widget and check with $K'(x) = 40$.

$0.06x^2 - 2.4x + 30 = 40$: $x \approx 43.7$ items, and the profit rises to about €860.

For which prices $p$ is any profit possible at all in Example 2? Try it with the widget and justify.

$f_{a}(x) = x^2 \mathrm{e}^{-ax}$ with $a > 0$. Use the CAS to find the extreme points depending on $a$. On which curve do the maxima lie?

$f_a'(x) = (2x - ax^2)\,\mathrm{e}^{-ax} = 0$: $x = 0$ (minimum) and $x = \tfrac2a$ (maximum) with $y = \tfrac{4}{a^2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2}$. With $a = \tfrac2x$: $y = x^2\,\mathrm{e}^{-2}$, a parabola.

A cost function has the form $K(x) = 0.02x^3 - 1.2x^2 + 30x + F$ with the fixed costs $F$. For which fixed costs is the maximum profit at the price of €30 exactly €0?

The fixed costs only shift $G$ downwards; the maximum stays at 40 items. $G(40) = 640 - F = 0$: $F = 640$ €. With higher fixed costs the company always makes a loss.

UNIT 22.5 LESSONS

2Piecewise defined functions and open optimisation

Up to 50 items each component brings in €20; above that there is a discount: $E(x) = 20x$ for $x \le 50$, $E(x) = 20x - 0.2(x - 50)^2$ for $x > 50$. The costs are $K(x) = 8x + 300$. In the first piece the profit $12x - 300$ rises to €300. In the second, $G'(x) = 12 - 0.4(x - 50) = 0$ at $x = 80$: there the sale brings in €480, more despite the discount.

Many processes follow different laws in different ranges: tariffs, discounts, roads made of straight sections and bends. You investigate each piece on its own and check the transition points separately. And often the question itself is open: greatest profit, greatest turnover or as little material as possible are different goals with different answers.

KEY POINT · PIECEWISE FUNCTIONS

Continuous at the transition point $x_0$: both parts have the same function value there. Without a kink (differentiable): the same gradient as well.

Optimisation problems: solve $f'(x) = 0$ in each piece, keep only solutions inside the piece, then compare the transition points and the end points.

Open questions: first fix the goal, then optimise, and at the end evaluate the result in context.

In GeoGebra: G(x) := If(x <= 50, 12x - 300, 12x - 0.2(x - 50)^2 - 300) draws both parts in one graph.

Watch out for kinks: At a kink there is no derivative. There can still be an extremum there, as with $|x|$ at the origin.

FIGURE · PROFIT WITH A QUANTITY DISCOUNT

To the left of 50 items a straight line, to the right a parabola. The point 50 is not a maximum; that only comes at 80 items.

EXAMPLE 3 · JOINED WITHOUT A KINK

A slide has the profile $f(x) = 0.5x^2$ for $0 \le x \le 2$ and $f(x) = 2x - 2$ for $x > 2$. Is the transition continuous and without a kink?

At $x = 2$: $0.5 \cdot 4 = 2$ and $2 \cdot 2 - 2 = 2$, continuous. Gradients: on the left $f'(2) = 2$, on the right $2$.

Continuous and without a kink: the line is the tangent to the parabola at 2.

EXAMPLE 4 · WHAT IS OPTIMAL?

With the quantity discount: how many items bring the greatest revenue, how many the greatest profit?

Revenue on the right: $E'(x) = 20 - 0.4(x - 50) = 0$ at $x = 100$, $E(100) = 1500$ €. Profit: maximum at 80 items.

The greatest revenue and the greatest profit are at different points. Which goal counts is a decision, not a calculation.

EXERCISES

A phone tariff costs €10 up to 5 GB, and €2 for each further GB above that. Write the costs as a piecewise function. Is it continuous? Is it without a kink?

$T(x) = 10$ for $x \le 5$, $T(x) = 10 + 2(x - 5)$ for $x > 5$. Continuous, but with a kink: gradient 0 on the left, 2 on the right.

For which $c$ is $f(x) = x^2$ for $x \le 2$ and $f(x) = 4x + c$ for $x > 2$ continuous? Is $f$ then also without a kink?

$4 = 8 + c$: $c = -4$. Gradients $2 \cdot 2 = 4$ and $4$: also without a kink.

With the quantity discount the fixed costs rise from €300 to €500. Does the best number of items change? Does the profit change?

The number of items stays at 80, because fixed costs only shift the graph downwards. The profit falls to €280.

The most economical one-litre can has $h = 2r \approx 10.8$ cm (Chapter 2.4). Real cans are often narrower and taller. Name three goals that could explain this.

For example: easy to hold in the hand, space on the shelf and in the fridge, room for the label, stability when stacking. For these goals a different $r$ is optimal.

A taxi costs a €4 basic fare, €2 per km for the first 10 km and €1.50 for every further kilometre. A hire car costs €25 plus €0.80 per km. Write down both costs and use the CAS to find from which distance on the hire car is cheaper.

Taxi: $4 + 2x$ for $x \le 10$, $24 + 1.5(x - 10)$ for $x > 10$. Hire car: $25 + 0.8x$. In the first piece they are equal at $x = 17.5$, which is not in the piece. In the second: $9 + 1.5x = 25 + 0.8x$, $x \approx 22.9$ km.

UNIT 32.5 LESSONS

3Regression: functions from measurements

A candle is 20 cm long at the start and is measured every hour. The points lie almost on a straight line. Linear regression gives $y \approx -1.57x + 20.0$. At $y = 0$ the candle has burnt down: after about 12.7 hours. The coefficient of determination $R^2 \approx 0.9997$ says that the line fits almost perfectly.

In regression the calculator searches among all functions of one type for the one that comes closest to the measured points. You choose the type: it should fit the context, not just the points. And every model only holds in the range in which you measured. Calculating far beyond it is risky.

KEY POINT · REGRESSION

Choosing the model: the same change per step → linear · the same factor per step → exponential · braking distance, throw, area → quadratic

Quality: the coefficient of determination $R^2$ lies between 0 and 1. The closer to 1, the better the model describes the points.

Limits: interpolating within the measured values is usually safe; extrapolating outside them only with care.

In GeoGebra: put the measurements into the spreadsheet, select them, tool “Two Variable Regression Analysis”, choose the regression model. Commands: FitPoly, FitExp.

A good $R^2$ alone is not enough: even a wrong model can fit well within the measured range and be completely off outside it.

INTERACTIVE · REGRESSION

Choose a series of measurements and a model. You can move the points. Which model fits the candle, the braking distance, the cooling?

EXAMPLE 5 · THE BRAKING DISTANCE

Measurements of the braking distance at 30 to 130 km/h are in the widget. Which model fits? Estimate the braking distance at 150 km/h.

Physically, the braking distance grows with the square of the speed: quadratic regression, roughly $y \approx 0.0099x^2 + 0.013x - 0.27$.

At 150 km/h: about 224 m, an extrapolation just beyond the measured range.

EXAMPLE 6 · THE TEA COOLS DOWN

The temperature difference from the room was 70 °C, 55 °C after 5 min, 42 °C after 10 min and so on down to 15 °C after 30 min. Model it.

Roughly the same factor every 5 minutes: exponential, $\Delta T(t) \approx 70.7 \cdot 0.949^t$.

After 45 minutes still about 7 °C above room temperature, as with the coffee in Chapter 4.2.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

Let the CAS calculate $\int_0^2 \left(ax^2 + 1\right)\mathrm{d}x$. For which $a$ is the integral 10?

$\tfrac{8a}{3} + 2 = 10$: $a = 3$

Connecting with Chapter 2.4: For which $k$ does the line $y = kx$ touch the graph of $f(x) = \mathrm{e}^x$? Use the CAS to solve the conditions for the point of contact.

$\mathrm{e}^{x_0} = kx_0$ and $\mathrm{e}^{x_0} = k$: from this $x_0 = 1$ and $k = \mathrm{e}$. You know the tangent $y = \mathrm{e}\,x$ from Chapter 4.2.

Connecting with Chapter 1.3: Let the CAS calculate the cross product of $(a, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 1, a)$. For which $a$ is the area of the parallelogram 3 square units?

$(a, -a^2, a)$ with the magnitude $\sqrt{2a^2 + a^4}$. $a^4 + 2a^2 = 9$: $a^2 = -1 + \sqrt{10}$, so $a \approx \pm 1.47$.

EXCURSIONOUTLOOK ELECTIVE 1 · ART AND ENGINEERING

Central projection: how perspective was invented

Around 1420 the Florentine architect Filippo Brunelleschi painted the Baptistery of Florence in such a way that viewers could compare his picture with the real building by looking through a hole and using a mirror. All the lines running into the depth met in one point. In 1435 Leon Battista Alberti described the method in his book “De pictura”: the picture is a window through which you look at the world.

Mathematically this is a central projection: every point is mapped onto the picture plane along the line to the eye. An object at depth $z$ behind the picture plane appears reduced by the factor $\tfrac{a}{a + z}$ if the eye is at distance $a$ in front of it. Edges running into the depth converge on the vanishing point at eye level, the horizon.

Source: L. B. Alberti, “De pictura” (On Painting), 1435. Brunelleschi's experiment according to his biographer Antonio Manetti; overview: Smarthistory, “Linear Perspective: Brunelleschi’s Experiment”.
