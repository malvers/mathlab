Random variables and path rules

If you throw two dice, you are rarely interested in the pair, mostly in a number: the total score, the winnings, the number of sixes. Such numbers are called random variables. You set up their probability distribution, calculate with tree diagrams and path rules and learn what “exactly”, “at least” and “at most” mean in probability.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Random variables and distributions

Two dice have 36 equally likely outcomes, but only 11 possible totals. The total 7 arises in six ways, (1, 6), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2) and (6, 1), the total 2 in only one. That is why 7 occurs most often in the exploration.

KEY POINT · RANDOM VARIABLE AND DISTRIBUTION

A random variable $X$ assigns a real number to every outcome of a random experiment.

The probability distribution of $X$ gives the probability $P(X = x_i)$ for every value $x_i$. Together they add up to $1$.

It can be shown as a table, as a bar chart (a bar of length $P(X = x_i)$ above $x_i$) or as a histogram (columns of width 1 whose area is the probability).

Notation: $P(X = 7)$ is read “the probability that $X$ takes the value 7”. $P(X \leq 3)$ combines all the values up to 3.

Check: if the probabilities do not add up to exactly 1, a value is missing or there is a calculation error.

EXAMPLE 1 · THREE COINS

Three coins are tossed, and $X$ is the number of heads. Set up the distribution.

8 equally likely outcomes: TTT · HTT, THT, TTH · HHT, HTH, THH · HHH.

$P(X = 0) = \tfrac18$, $P(X = 1) = \tfrac38$, $P(X = 2) = \tfrac38$, $P(X = 3) = \tfrac18$, together $\tfrac88 = 1$.

EXAMPLE 2 · THE WINNINGS AS A RANDOM VARIABLE

A wheel of fortune costs a stake of €1. With $\tfrac12$ you get nothing back, with $\tfrac14$ your stake, with $\tfrac14$ €5. $X$ is the gain.

Gain = payout − stake: $X$ has the values $-1$, $0$ and $4$ with $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac14$.

Winning something: $P(X > 0) = P(X = 4) = \tfrac14$.

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Path rules and counting

At least one six in four throws: that is a great many paths in the tree. Its opposite is a single path, “no six four times”, with the probability $\left(\tfrac56\right)^4 \approx 0.482$. So $P(\text{at least one six}) = 1 - 0.482 \approx 0.518$. From four throws on, the bet is worth it.

KEY POINT · PATH RULES AND COUNTING

Multiplication rule for paths: the probability of a path is the product of the probabilities along the path.

Addition rule for paths: the probability of an event is the sum of the probabilities of all the paths that belong to it.

Counting: if all paths of an event have the same probability, you calculate number times path probability. With $n$ stages of success or failure there are $\binom{n}{k}$ paths with exactly $k$ successes (graphing calculator: nCr).

“at least $k$”: $P(X \geq k)$ · “at most $k$”: $P(X \leq k)$ · “at least one”: $1 - P(\text{none})$, the complementary event.

Translating: “more than 2” means $X \geq 3$, “fewer than 2” means $X \leq 1$, “not more than 2” means $X \leq 2$.

Without replacement the probabilities change from stage to stage; with replacement they stay the same.

EXAMPLE 3 · GUESSING IN A TEST

5 questions, 4 answers each, everything guessed. $X$ is the number of right answers. Calculate $P(X = 3)$ and $P(X \geq 3)$.

One path with 3 successes: $0.25^3 \cdot 0.75^2 \approx 0.00879$. There are $\binom{5}{3} = 10$ such paths: $P(X = 3) \approx 0.0879$.

$P(X \geq 3) = 0.0879 + 5 \cdot 0.25^4 \cdot 0.75 + 0.25^5 \approx$ $0.104$, so a good 10 %.

EXAMPLE 4 · THE COMPLEMENTARY EVENT

How likely is at least one six in ten throws?

Complementary event: no six ten times, $\left(\tfrac56\right)^{10} \approx 0.162$.

$P(X \geq 1) = 1 - \left(\tfrac56\right)^{10} \approx 0.838$. Without the complementary event you would have to add ten terms.

INTERACTIVE · EXACTLY k SUCCESSES

All paths with exactly $k$ successes have the same probability. You only have to count them.

$\left(\tfrac56\right)^{12} \approx 0.112$, $\left(\tfrac56\right)^{13} \approx 0.093$: 13 times.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

A tombola drum contains 10 tickets, 2 of which win. You draw two tickets. How likely is at least one win?

$1 - \tfrac{8}{10} \cdot \tfrac79 = 1 - \tfrac{56}{90} = \tfrac{17}{45} \approx 0.378$

Three people are chosen at random. How likely is it that at least two were born on the same day of the week? (Assume every day of the week is equally likely.)

Complementary event “all different”: $\tfrac77 \cdot \tfrac67 \cdot \tfrac57 = \tfrac{30}{49}$. So $1 - \tfrac{30}{49} = \tfrac{19}{49} \approx 0.388$.

Connecting with chapter 1.1: $P(\text{no six in } n \text{ throws}) = \left(\tfrac56\right)^n$. Which growth model is this? After how many throws is this probability below $\tfrac12$ for the first time?

Exponential decrease with the factor $\tfrac56$. $\left(\tfrac56\right)^3 \approx 0.579$, $\left(\tfrac56\right)^4 \approx 0.482$: after 4 throws, the “half-life” of the run of bad luck.

Three draws with replacement are made from the urn with 3 red and 2 blue balls. $X$ is the number of red ones. Set up the distribution and draw a bar chart.

$P(X = k) = \binom{3}{k} \cdot 0.6^k \cdot 0.4^{3 - k}$: $0.064$; $0.288$; $0.432$; $0.216$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, 3$. Sum 1.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

The problem of the Chevalier de Méré

Antoine Gombaud, Chevalier de Méré, liked to gamble for money in 17th-century Paris. He knew from experience: if you bet on throwing at least one six in four throws of one dice, you win in the long run. He concluded that the bet “at least one double six in 24 throws of two dice” must then be favourable too, because 4 is to 6 as 24 is to 36. But he lost with it.

Blaise Pascal wrote about this to Pierre de Fermat in 1654. With the complementary event it is quickly worked out: $1 - \left(\tfrac56\right)^4 \approx 0.518$, but $1 - \left(\tfrac{35}{36}\right)^{24} \approx 0.491$. The first bet is just favourable, the second just unfavourable. The correspondence between the two is regarded as the beginning of probability theory.

Source: letter from Blaise Pascal to Pierre de Fermat of 29 July 1654, printed in the collected works of Pascal (Œuvres complètes) and of Fermat (Œuvres, volume 2).
