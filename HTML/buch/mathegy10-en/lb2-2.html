Expected value and fair games

Is a raffle ticket worth it? What does an insurance company earn? How do you set the stake and the prizes at the school fair? The expected value tells you what a random variable brings on average in the long run, the standard deviation how strongly it fluctuates. With both you judge games, bets and risks, and see why games of chance are never fair.

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1The expected value

In 600 throws each score comes up about 100 times. The sum is then about $100 \cdot (1 + 2 + \ldots + 6) = 2100$, and the mean $\tfrac{2100}{600} = 3.5$. The same follows directly from the probabilities: $1 \cdot \tfrac16 + 2 \cdot \tfrac16 + \ldots + 6 \cdot \tfrac16 = 3.5$.

KEY POINT · EXPECTED VALUE

If $X$ takes the values $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, then $$E(X) = \mu = x_1 \cdot P(X = x_1) + x_2 \cdot P(X = x_2) + \ldots + x_n \cdot P(X = x_n)$$ is called the expected value of $X$.

It is the mean that the results of many repetitions settle down to. It does not have to be a possible value: nobody throws a 3.5.

If all values are equally likely, $E(X)$ is the arithmetic mean of the values, as in Year 9.

A table helps: the values $x_i$ in one row, the probabilities below, then the products $x_i \cdot P(X = x_i)$ and their sum.

Gain or payout? Always make clear what $X$ is. Gain = payout − stake.

INTERACTIVE · RANDOM VARIABLE AND EXPECTED VALUE

Choose a random variable, read off the expected value and then play a thousand times: the mean approaches it.

EXAMPLE 1 · THE WHEEL OF FORTUNE

Stake €1. With $\tfrac12$ you get nothing back, with $\tfrac14$ your stake, with $\tfrac14$ €5. Calculate the expected value of the gain $X$.

$E(X) = (-1) \cdot \tfrac12 + 0 \cdot \tfrac14 + 4 \cdot \tfrac14 = -0.5 + 1 = 0.5$

On average you win 50 cents per game. For the operator this is a losing business.

EXAMPLE 2 · THE TOMBOLA

1000 tickets at €1 each: one main prize of €500, ten prizes of €20, a hundred of €2, and the rest are blanks. What is a ticket worth?

Expected payout: $500 \cdot \tfrac{1}{1000} + 20 \cdot \tfrac{10}{1000} + 2 \cdot \tfrac{100}{1000} = 0.5 + 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.9$ €.

Expected gain: $0.9 - 1 = -0.10$ € per ticket. If all tickets are sold, the tombola takes €100.

UNIT 22 LESSONS

2Variance and standard deviation

The two games from the exploration have the same expected value €0. With the coin bet you are €1 richer or poorer after every game. With the ticket you almost always lose a euro and very rarely win a great deal. The values spread to different degrees around the expected value.

KEY POINT · VARIANCE AND STANDARD DEVIATION

Variance: $V(X) = (x_1 - \mu)^2 \cdot P(X = x_1) + \ldots + (x_n - \mu)^2 \cdot P(X = x_n)$, the mean squared deviation from the expected value.

Standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{V(X)}$. It has the same unit as $X$.

Small $\sigma$: the values lie close to $\mu$. Large $\sigma$: strong fluctuations, so a large risk.

Why square? Otherwise deviations upwards and downwards would cancel each other out: the sum of $(x_i - \mu) \cdot P(X = x_i)$ is always 0.

Year 9: for data there was the empirical standard deviation. Here there are probabilities instead of frequencies.

INTERACTIVE · THE SAME AVERAGE, A DIFFERENT RISK

Eight people each play a hundred rounds. Choose the game and compare how far apart their balances drift.

EXAMPLE 3 · COIN BET AND TICKET

Calculate $\sigma$ for the two games from the exploration.

Coin bet, $\mu = 0$: $V = 1^2 \cdot \tfrac12 + (-1)^2 \cdot \tfrac12 = 1$, $\sigma = 1$ €.

Ticket, $\mu = 0$: $V = 99^2 \cdot 0.01 + (-1)^2 \cdot 0.99 = 99$, $\sigma \approx 9.95$ €, almost ten times as much.

EXAMPLE 4 · THE DICE

Calculate the variance and the standard deviation of the score of a dice.

$\mu = 3.5$. Deviations $\pm 2.5$, $\pm 1.5$, $\pm 0.5$, each twice: $V = \tfrac{2 \cdot (6.25 + 2.25 + 0.25)}{6} = \tfrac{17.5}{6} \approx 2.92$.

$\sigma \approx 1.71$

EXERCISES

Wheel of fortune from example 1: gains $-1$, $0$, $4$ with $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac14$ and $\mu = 0.5$. Calculate $V(X)$ and $\sigma$.

$V = (-1.5)^2 \cdot \tfrac12 + (-0.5)^2 \cdot \tfrac14 + 3.5^2 \cdot \tfrac14 = 1.125 + 0.0625 + 3.0625 = 4.25$, $\sigma \approx 2.06$ €

Investment A brings a certain 2 % interest. Investment B brings 12 % with probability $\tfrac12$ and a loss of 8 % with $\tfrac12$. Compare the expected value and the standard deviation.

Both have the expected value 2 %. $\sigma_A = 0$, $\sigma_B = 10$ percentage points: B is riskier.

Three coins, $X$ = number of heads, $\mu = 1.5$. Calculate $V(X)$ and $\sigma$.

$V = 2.25 \cdot \tfrac18 + 0.25 \cdot \tfrac38 + 0.25 \cdot \tfrac38 + 2.25 \cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac68 = 0.75$, $\sigma \approx 0.87$

Game 1 pays out the score of a dice in euros. Game 2 pays €21 for a six, otherwise nothing. Show that both have the same expected value, and compare the standard deviations.

Both €3.50. $\sigma_1 \approx 1.71$ €, $\sigma_2 = \sqrt{17.5^2 \cdot \tfrac16 + 3.5^2 \cdot \tfrac56} = \sqrt{61.25} \approx 7.83$ €.

In the coin bet ($\pm$ €1) all amounts are a) increased by €2, b) doubled. How do the expected value and the standard deviation change? Give a general reason.

a) Values 3 and 1: $\mu = 2$, $\sigma = 1$. Shifting changes $\mu$, but not the distances.   b) Values 2 and −2: $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 2$. With the factor, all distances and so $\sigma$ are doubled too.

UNIT 33 LESSONS

3Fair and unfair games

A dice game: stake €1, for a six you get €5 back. Sounds generous, but it is not: with $\tfrac16$ you win €4, with $\tfrac56$ you lose €1. $E(X) = 4 \cdot \tfrac16 - 1 \cdot \tfrac56 = -\tfrac16$, so on average a loss of about 17 cents per game. A payout of €6 would be fair.

KEY POINT · FAIR GAMES

A game is called fair if the expected value of the gain is zero: $E(\text{gain}) = 0$, which is the same as $E(\text{payout}) = \text{stake}$.

$E(\text{gain}) < 0$: unfavourable for the player, the operator earns. $E(\text{gain}) > 0$: favourable for the players.

The fair stake is the expected payout.

Games of chance such as the lottery, roulette or slot machines are never fair. The operator lives on the negative expected value, and over many games it becomes reality very reliably.

“70 % chance of rain” means: on about 7 out of 10 days with this forecast it rains. A single dry day does not make the forecast wrong.

INTERACTIVE · THE SCHOOL FAIR GAME
EXAMPLE 5 · FINDING THE FAIR STAKE

In the school fair game a double brings €6, the total 7 brings €3. Which stake is fair? Which stake does the class need for a profit of 50 cents per game?

A double and the total 7 each have the probability $\tfrac16$. $E(\text{payout}) = 6 \cdot \tfrac16 + 3 \cdot \tfrac16 = 1.50$ €: fair stake €1.50.

For 50 cents per game: stake €2. From 300 games the class earns €150 on average.

EXAMPLE 6 · ROULETTE

In roulette there are 37 pockets (0 to 36). If you put €1 on a number and win, you get your stake back and €35 on top. Calculate the expected value of the gain.

$E(X) = 35 \cdot \tfrac{1}{37} + (-1) \cdot \tfrac{36}{37} = -\tfrac{1}{37} \approx -0.027$ €

The casino keeps 2.7 % on average of all stakes. The zero makes the difference.

EXERCISES

Stake €2. For heads you get €4 back, for tails nothing. Is the game fair?

$E(\text{gain}) = 2 \cdot \tfrac12 + (-2) \cdot \tfrac12 = 0$: fair.

A wheel of fortune has four sectors of the same size with the payouts €0, €0, €1 and €5. Which stake is fair?

$\tfrac{0 + 0 + 1 + 5}{4} = 1.50$ €

On a wheel of fortune you get nothing with $\tfrac12$, your stake of €2 back with $\tfrac13$, and the amount $a$ with $\tfrac16$. For which $a$ is the game fair?

$0 \cdot \tfrac12 + 2 \cdot \tfrac13 + a \cdot \tfrac16 = 2 \Rightarrow \tfrac{a}{6} = \tfrac43 \Rightarrow a = 8$ €

On 10 days with the forecast “70 % rain” it rained on 8 days. Was the forecast bad? What would you have to check to judge a weather forecast?

No. About 7 out of 10 are expected, and 8 is within the range of chance. You can only judge it over many days: if it rains on about 70 % of all days with “70 %”, it is good.

You toss a coin at most three times, until heads comes up. Heads on the 1st toss brings €2, on the 2nd €4, on the 3rd €8, and three tails bring nothing. Which stake is fair?

$2 \cdot \tfrac12 + 4 \cdot \tfrac14 + 8 \cdot \tfrac18 = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$ €. Every further toss allowed would raise the fair stake by €1, see the excursion.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

A lottery sells 500 tickets at €2: one prize of €200, four of €50, twenty of €10. Calculate the expected gain of a ticket and the surplus of the lottery.

Expected payout $\tfrac{200 + 200 + 200}{500} = 1.20$ €, expected gain $-0.80$ €. Surplus: $1000 - 600 = 400$ €.

Connecting with chapter 2.1: in a test with 5 questions and 4 answers each, everything is guessed. A right answer gets one point, and for a wrong one a third of a point is deducted. What score is to be expected?

Per question: $1 \cdot \tfrac14 - \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac34 = 0$. In total 0 points: with this deduction, guessing does not pay on average.

Two fair dice games with a stake of €1: A pays €2 for an even score, B pays €6 only for a six. Calculate $\sigma$ of the gain for each. Which game suits a cautious person?

A: gains $\pm 1$, $\sigma = 1$ €. B: gain €5 with $\tfrac16$, $-1$ € with $\tfrac56$, $\sigma = \sqrt{25 \cdot \tfrac16 + 1 \cdot \tfrac56} = \sqrt5 \approx 2.24$ €. A is less risky.

Design your own dice game for the school fair with two dice and two prize levels, in which the class earns 30 cents per game on average and at least every fourth game wins something. Justify with probabilities and the expected value.

For example: stake €2, a double ($\tfrac16$) brings €6, the total 7 ($\tfrac16$) brings €4.20. Chance of winning $\tfrac13$, expected payout $\tfrac{6 + 4.20}{6} = 1.70$ €, and the class earns 30 cents per game on average. Other solutions are possible.

EXCURSIONA FAMOUS PARADOX

The St Petersburg paradox

Take the coin game from exercise 15 and allow as many tosses as you like: heads on the $k$-th toss brings $2^k$ euros. Every possible outcome then contributes €1 to the expected value, $2 \cdot \tfrac12 + 4 \cdot \tfrac14 + 8 \cdot \tfrac18 + \ldots = 1 + 1 + 1 + \ldots$, and this sum grows beyond every bound. So the fair stake would be infinitely large. Even so, hardly anyone would pay more than a few euros for this game.

Daniel Bernoulli published an explanation in St Petersburg in 1738: one euro more is worth less to someone who already has a lot than to someone who has little. He suggested averaging not the amount of money but its utility, and measured utility by the logarithm of wealth. You will get to know the logarithm in chapter 4.1.

Source: Daniel Bernoulli: “Specimen theoriae novae de mensura sortis”, Commentarii Academiae Scientiarum Imperialis Petropolitanae 5 (1738), pp. 175–192.
