Sine rule, cosine rule and area

You know sine and cosine from the right-angled triangle. But most triangles have no right angle: a plot of land, a gable, the triangle formed by two footpaths. With the sine rule and the cosine rule you calculate any triangle from three suitable pieces of information, and with a new area formula also its area, without measuring the height.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1The sine rule

The height $h_c$ splits every triangle into two right-angled triangles. In the left one $\sin\alpha = \tfrac{h_c}{b}$, in the right one $\sin\beta = \tfrac{h_c}{a}$. So $h_c = b \cdot \sin\alpha = a \cdot \sin\beta$, and after dividing by $\sin\alpha \cdot \sin\beta$ you get: $\tfrac{a}{\sin\alpha} = \tfrac{b}{\sin\beta}$. The third part follows in the same way with the height $h_a$.

KEY POINT · SINE RULE

In every triangle $$\frac{a}{\sin\alpha} = \frac{b}{\sin\beta} = \frac{c}{\sin\gamma}$$ The three quotients are equal to the diameter $2r$ of the circumcircle.

You need it when a side and its opposite angle are known, plus one more quantity: for ASA (after the angle sum) and for SSA.

Careful with SSA: $\sin\beta = 0.8$ has two solutions, $\beta \approx 53.1^\circ$ and $\beta \approx 126.9^\circ$. The triangle is unique if the given angle lies opposite the longer of the two sides.

Labels: side $a$ lies opposite vertex $A$, and angle $\alpha$ is at $A$. Sketch first, then calculate.

Why two angles? On the unit circle $\sin(180^\circ - \beta) = \sin\beta$. The calculator only shows the acute angle.

INTERACTIVE · THE SINE RULE IN THE TRIANGLE

Drag the vertices. The three quotients always stay equal, whatever the triangle looks like. With “Circumcircle” you can see why.

EXAMPLE 1 · ASA

Given: $c = 8$ cm, $\alpha = 50^\circ$, $\beta = 60^\circ$. Calculate the missing sides.

Angle sum: $\gamma = 70^\circ$. Sine rule: $a = \tfrac{c \cdot \sin\alpha}{\sin\gamma} = \tfrac{8 \cdot \sin 50^\circ}{\sin 70^\circ} \approx 6.52$ cm.

$a \approx 6.52$ cm, $b = \tfrac{8 \cdot \sin 60^\circ}{\sin 70^\circ} \approx 7.37$ cm

EXAMPLE 2 · SSA WITH TWO SOLUTIONS

Given: $c = 6$ cm, $\alpha = 40^\circ$, $a = 5$ cm (the exploration).

$\sin\gamma = \tfrac{c \cdot \sin\alpha}{a} = \tfrac{6 \cdot \sin 40^\circ}{5} \approx 0.771$, so $\gamma_1 \approx 50.5^\circ$ or $\gamma_2 \approx 129.5^\circ$. Both are possible, because $\alpha$ lies opposite the shorter side.

$\beta_1 \approx 89.5^\circ$, $b_1 \approx 7.78$ cm · $\beta_2 \approx 10.5^\circ$, $b_2 \approx 1.41$ cm

EXERCISES

Calculate the triangle with $a = 7$ cm, $\alpha = 35^\circ$ and $\beta = 80^\circ$.

$\gamma = 65^\circ$, $b = \tfrac{7 \cdot \sin 80^\circ}{\sin 35^\circ} \approx 12.02$ cm, $c = \tfrac{7 \cdot \sin 65^\circ}{\sin 35^\circ} \approx 11.06$ cm

Calculate the triangle with $b = 10$ cm, $\beta = 120^\circ$ and $a = 6$ cm. Why is there only one solution here?

$\sin\alpha = \tfrac{6 \cdot \sin 120^\circ}{10} \approx 0.520$, $\alpha \approx 31.3^\circ$. The obtuse angle $148.7^\circ$ is impossible, because $\beta$ is already obtuse. $\gamma \approx 28.7^\circ$, $c \approx 5.54$ cm.

A lighthouse $L$ is sighted from two points $A$ and $B$ on the coast that are 2 km apart. The angle between $AB$ and the direction to the lighthouse is $62^\circ$ at $A$ and $71^\circ$ at $B$. How far is the lighthouse from $A$ and from $B$?

Angle at $L$: $47^\circ$. $\overline{AL} = \tfrac{2 \cdot \sin 71^\circ}{\sin 47^\circ} \approx 2.59$ km, $\overline{BL} = \tfrac{2 \cdot \sin 62^\circ}{\sin 47^\circ} \approx 2.41$ km.

How many triangles are there with $b = 6$ cm and $\alpha = 30^\circ$ if a) $a = 4$ cm   b) $a = 3$ cm   c) $a = 2$ cm? Calculate $\beta$ in each case.

Compare $a$ with $b \cdot \sin\alpha = 3$ cm, the distance from $C$ to the other arm of the angle.

a) two: $\sin\beta = 0.75$, $\beta \approx 48.6^\circ$ or $131.4^\circ$   b) one: $\sin\beta = 1$, $\beta = 90^\circ$   c) none: $\sin\beta = 1.5 > 1$

A triangle has $c = 5$ cm and $\gamma = 30^\circ$. How large is its circumcircle? Justify $\tfrac{c}{\sin\gamma} = 2r$ at least for $\gamma = 90^\circ$.

$2r = \tfrac{5}{\sin 30^\circ} = 10$, so $r = 5$ cm. For $\gamma = 90^\circ$, $c$ is a diameter by Thales’ theorem, and because $\sin 90^\circ = 1$, $\tfrac{c}{\sin\gamma} = c = 2r$.

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2The cosine rule

In the exploration, $\gamma = 90^\circ$ gave exactly $c^2 = 25$, $60^\circ$ only $c^2 \approx 13$ and $120^\circ$ about $c^2 \approx 37$. Each time the difference is $\pm 12 = \pm 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot \tfrac12$, that is $2ab \cdot \cos\gamma$. Pythagoras’ theorem gets a correction term.

KEY POINT · COSINE RULE

In every triangle $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\gamma$$ and likewise $a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos\alpha$, $\;b^2 = a^2 + c^2 - 2ac\cos\beta$.

For $\gamma = 90^\circ$, $\cos\gamma = 0$: what remains is Pythagoras’ theorem.

You need it for SAS (the third side) and for SSS (an angle): $\cos\gamma = \dfrac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}$. Here the angle is always unique.

Obtuse or acute? $\cos\gamma < 0$ means $\gamma > 90^\circ$. Quick test: if $c^2 > a^2 + b^2$, the angle opposite $c$ is obtuse.

For SSS first find the largest angle (opposite the longest side) with the cosine rule. The other two are then acute, and there the sine rule is safe as well.

INTERACTIVE · SQUARES ON THE SIDES

Drag the vertices. If $\gamma$ is acute, the square on $c$ is smaller than the other two together; if $\gamma$ is obtuse, it is larger.

EXAMPLE 3 · SAS

Given: $a = 4$ cm, $b = 3$ cm, $\gamma = 120^\circ$. Calculate $c$.

$c^2 = 16 + 9 - 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot \cos 120^\circ = 25 - 24 \cdot (-0.5) = 37$

$c = \sqrt{37} \approx 6.08$ cm, longer than with a right angle.

EXAMPLE 4 · SSS

A triangle has the sides $a = 5$ cm, $b = 6$ cm, $c = 7$ cm. Calculate the angles.

$\cos\gamma = \tfrac{25 + 36 - 49}{60} = 0.2$, $\gamma \approx 78.5^\circ$; $\cos\alpha = \tfrac{36 + 49 - 25}{84} \approx 0.714$, $\alpha \approx 44.4^\circ$

$\alpha \approx 44.4^\circ$, $\beta \approx 57.1^\circ$, $\gamma \approx 78.5^\circ$

UNIT 34 LESSONS

3Area of general triangles

For the area you need a base and the matching height: $A = \tfrac12 \cdot a \cdot h_a$. But the height can be calculated: in the triangle formed by $b$, $h_a$ and a piece of $a$, $h_a = b \cdot \sin\gamma$. So $A = \tfrac12 \cdot a \cdot b \cdot \sin\gamma$, and nobody has to walk onto the plot.

KEY POINT · AREA FORMULA WITH SINE

The area of a triangle is $$A = \tfrac12\, a\, b \sin\gamma = \tfrac12\, b\, c \sin\alpha = \tfrac12\, a\, c \sin\beta$$ always two sides and the angle they enclose.

If three sides are given, you first calculate an angle with the cosine rule.

Polygons are split into triangles by diagonals, and their areas are added.

Same area, two angles: because $\sin(180^\circ - \gamma) = \sin\gamma$, the triangles with $\gamma = 30^\circ$ and $\gamma = 150^\circ$ (and otherwise the same sides) have the same area.

The largest area for fixed sides $a$ and $b$ is at $\gamma = 90^\circ$; then $\sin\gamma = 1$.

INTERACTIVE · TWO SIDES AND AN ANGLE
EXAMPLE 5 · THE PLOT OF LAND

Two sides of 40 m and 55 m enclose an angle of $70^\circ$. How large is the plot?

$A = \tfrac12 \cdot 40 \cdot 55 \cdot \sin 70^\circ = 1100 \cdot 0.9397$

$A \approx 1034$ m²

EXAMPLE 6 · SPLITTING A QUADRILATERAL

A quadrilateral $ABCD$ has the sides $\overline{AB} = 6$ cm, $\overline{BC} = 5$ cm, $\overline{CD} = 4$ cm, $\overline{DA} = 7$ cm and the diagonal $\overline{AC} = 8$ cm. Calculate its area.

Triangle $ABC$: $\cos\beta = \tfrac{36 + 25 - 64}{60} = -0.05$, $\beta \approx 92.9^\circ$, $A_1 = \tfrac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 5 \cdot \sin\beta \approx 14.98$ cm².

Triangle $ACD$: $\cos\delta = \tfrac{16 + 49 - 64}{56} \approx 0.018$, $\delta \approx 89.0^\circ$, $A_2 \approx 14.00$ cm². $A \approx 28.98$ cm²

EXERCISES

Calculate the area of the triangle with $a = 6$ cm, $b = 9$ cm and $\gamma = 40^\circ$.

$A = \tfrac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 9 \cdot \sin 40^\circ \approx 17.36$ cm²

Calculate the area of an equilateral triangle with side length 10 cm.

$A = \tfrac12 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot \sin 60^\circ = 25\sqrt3 \approx 43.30$ cm²

A triangle has the sides 13 cm, 14 cm and 15 cm. Calculate its area.

Opposite 15 cm: $\cos\gamma = \tfrac{169 + 196 - 225}{364} = \tfrac{5}{13}$, so $\sin\gamma = \tfrac{12}{13}$. $A = \tfrac12 \cdot 13 \cdot 14 \cdot \tfrac{12}{13} = 84$ cm², a whole number.

For $a = 6$ cm and $b = 9$ cm: at which angle $\gamma$ is the area largest? For which angles is it exactly half as large?

Largest at $90^\circ$: 27 cm². Half as large, so 13.5 cm², if $\sin\gamma = 0.5$: at $30^\circ$ and at $150^\circ$.

Calculate the area of a) a regular hexagon with side 4 cm   b) a regular decagon with circumradius 5 cm.

Split it into equal triangles from the centre. What angle do they have at the centre?

a) 6 equilateral triangles: $6 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 4^2 \cdot \sin 60^\circ = 24\sqrt3 \approx 41.57$ cm²   b) 10 triangles with two sides of 5 cm and $36^\circ$: $10 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 25 \cdot \sin 36^\circ \approx 73.47$ cm², already close to the circle ($25\pi \approx 78.5$).

Connecting with chapter 1.2: explain with the unit circle why $\sin(180^\circ - \alpha) = \sin\alpha$. What two consequences does this have in this chapter?

The points for $\alpha$ and $180^\circ - \alpha$ are mirror images in the $y$-axis, so they are at the same height. Consequences: SSA can have two solutions, and triangles with $\gamma$ and $180^\circ - \gamma$ have the same area.

The triangle $A(0, 0)$, $B(8, 0)$, $C(2, 5)$: calculate the side lengths, the angle $\alpha$ and the area in two ways.

$a = \sqrt{61} \approx 7.81$, $b = \sqrt{29} \approx 5.39$, $c = 8$. $\cos\alpha = \tfrac{29 + 64 - 61}{2 \cdot 8 \cdot \sqrt{29}} \approx 0.371$, $\alpha \approx 68.2^\circ$. $A = \tfrac12 \cdot 8 \cdot 5 = 20$ and $A = \tfrac12 \cdot 8 \cdot \sqrt{29} \cdot \sin\alpha \approx 20$.

Prove the cosine rule: the height $h_c$ divides $c$ into the pieces $b\cos\alpha$ and $c - b\cos\alpha$. Apply Pythagoras’ theorem in the right-hand part of the triangle (for acute $\alpha$).

$a^2 = h_c^2 + (c - b\cos\alpha)^2 = b^2\sin^2\alpha + c^2 - 2bc\cos\alpha + b^2\cos^2\alpha = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos\alpha$, because $\sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha = 1$.

EXCURSIONSURVEYING

The Earth from triangles

Pacing out long distances with measuring rods is tedious and inaccurate. Angles, on the other hand, can be measured very precisely from tower to tower. The Dutch mathematician Willebrord Snellius made use of this in 1615: he laid a chain of triangles between church towers across the flat country from Alkmaar to Bergen op Zoom. He actually measured only one short baseline; all the other sides he calculated from the measured angles, triangle by triangle, with the sine rule.

From the length of the chain and the difference in latitude of its two end points he determined the circumference of the Earth. This method, triangulation, was the basis of all maps until surveying by satellite.

Source: Willebrord Snellius: “Eratosthenes Batavus, de terrae ambitus vera quantitate”, Leiden 1617.
