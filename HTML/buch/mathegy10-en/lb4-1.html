Inverse functions and logarithms

How many years does it take for savings to double? How often do you have to double 1 to get past a billion? Questions like these turn a function round: you know the function value and are looking for the argument. You learn to invert functions, and with the logarithm the inverse of the exponential function, which turns multiplying into adding.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Inverting functions

Solving $F = 1.8\,C + 32$ gives $C = \tfrac{F - 32}{1.8}$, and 98.6 °F is exactly 37 °C, body temperature. For the square, on the other hand, the way back only works because side lengths are positive: $y = x^2 = 9$ has the two solutions $3$ and $-3$.

KEY POINT · INVERSE FUNCTION

A function $f$ is called invertible if every function value is taken at only one point. This is the case, for example, if $f$ is strictly monotonic.

The inverse function $f^{-1}$ assigns to every $y$ the $x$ with $f(x) = y$. The domain and the range swap roles.

Term: solve $y = f(x)$ for $x$, then swap $x$ and $y$. Graph: reflection in the bisector $y = x$.

Functions that are not invertible become invertible on a smaller domain: $y = x^2$ with $x \geq 0$ has the inverse function $y = \sqrt{x}$.

Horizontal line test: if every horizontal line cuts the graph at most once, the function is invertible.

With the CAS: solve(y=2x-4, x) solves for $x$; then swap $x$ and $y$.

INTERACTIVE · FUNCTION AND INVERSE FUNCTION

Choose a function and drag the point on $f$. Its reflection in $y = x$ lies on $f^{-1}$.

EXAMPLE 1 · A LINEAR FUNCTION

Find the inverse function of $f(x) = 2x - 4$.

$y = 2x - 4 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \tfrac{y + 4}{2} = 0.5y + 2$. Swap: $f^{-1}(x) = 0.5x + 2$.

Check: $f(5) = 6$ and $f^{-1}(6) = 5$.

EXAMPLE 2 · A RESTRICTED DOMAIN

Find the inverse function of $f(x) = (x - 1)^2 + 2$ for $x \geq 1$.

For $x \geq 1$, $f$ is strictly increasing, and the values are $y \geq 2$. $(x - 1)^2 = y - 2 \Rightarrow x = 1 + \sqrt{y - 2}$ (only the plus sign, because $x \geq 1$).

$f^{-1}(x) = 1 + \sqrt{x - 2}$ for $x \geq 2$

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2The logarithm

After 10 doublings you are at 1024, just over 1000. You get exactly 1000 for an exponent between 9 and 10: the solution of $2^x = 1000$. This number has a name: the logarithm of 1000 to base 2, $\log_2 1000 \approx 9.97$.

KEY POINT · LOGARITHM

For $a > 0$, $a \neq 1$ and $b > 0$, $\log_a b$ is the number $x$ with $a^x = b$: $$\log_a b = x \iff a^x = b$$ Said: “the logarithm of $b$ to base $a$”. It is the exponent to which you must raise $a$ to get $b$.

Always $\log_a 1 = 0$, $\log_a a = 1$, $\log_a a^x = x$ and $a^{\log_a b} = b$.

Special bases: $\lg b = \log_{10} b$ (common logarithm) and $\ln b = \log_e b$, the natural logarithm to Euler’s number $e \approx 2.718$.

Only positive numbers: $a^x$ is always positive, which is why $\log_a 0$ and $\log_a(-5)$ do not exist.

Calculator: the LOG and LN keys calculate $\lg$ and $\ln$. Other bases: $\log_a b = \tfrac{\lg b}{\lg a}$ (unit 3).

UNIT 34 LESSONS

3Laws of logarithms

$32 \cdot 1024 = 2^5 \cdot 2^{10} = 2^{15} = 32{,}768$: instead of multiplying, you add the exponents, and the exponents are the logarithms to base 2. In the same way dividing becomes subtracting: $65{,}536 : 256 = 2^{16 - 8} = 256$.

KEY POINT · LAWS OF LOGARITHMS

For $u, v > 0$ and every real number $r$:

$\log_a(u \cdot v) = \log_a u + \log_a v$; $\log_a \tfrac{u}{v} = \log_a u - \log_a v$; $\log_a\left(u^r\right) = r \cdot \log_a u$

Change of base: $\log_a b = \dfrac{\log_c b}{\log_c a}$, for example $\log_a b = \dfrac{\lg b}{\lg a} = \dfrac{\ln b}{\ln a}$.

Common mistake: $\lg(u + v)$ cannot be split up. The laws hold for products, quotients and powers.

Memory aid: they are the laws of powers read from the other side: $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m + n}$ becomes $\log(u v) = \log u + \log v$.

INTERACTIVE · THE SLIDE RULE

Until the 1970s engineers calculated like this. On the scales the distances are logarithms: putting two lengths end to end means multiplying.

EXAMPLE 5 · DERIVING THE PRODUCT LAW

Show that $\log_a(u \cdot v) = \log_a u + \log_a v$.

Set $m = \log_a u$ and $n = \log_a v$, so $u = a^m$ and $v = a^n$. Then $u \cdot v = a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m + n}$.

Taking logarithms: $\log_a(u \cdot v) = m + n = \log_a u + \log_a v$

EXAMPLE 6 · TRANSFORMING TERMS

Split up $\lg(1000x^2)$ and $\log_2 \tfrac{8}{x}$. Combine $2\lg 5 + \lg 4$.

$\lg(1000x^2) = \lg 1000 + 2\lg x = 3 + 2\lg x$; $\log_2 \tfrac8x = 3 - \log_2 x$ (for $x > 0$)

$2\lg 5 + \lg 4 = \lg(25 \cdot 4) = \lg 100 = 2$

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Napier’s wonderful tables

Around 1600 astronomers and navigators calculated with numbers full of digits, and every multiplication cost time and caused errors. The Scottish landowner John Napier worked for twenty years on a remedy. In 1614 he published the “Description of the Wonderful Table of Logarithms”: tables with which multiplying could be replaced by adding. The Swiss clockmaker Jost Bürgi had a similar idea independently.

Henry Briggs travelled to Edinburgh to meet Napier and suggested base 10. In 1624 his tables of common logarithms appeared. Johannes Kepler used logarithms to calculate the Rudolphine Tables of the planetary orbits (1627). And until the pocket calculator, logarithms were hidden in every slide rule.

Sources: John Napier: “Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio”, Edinburgh 1614 · Henry Briggs: “Arithmetica Logarithmica”, London 1624.
