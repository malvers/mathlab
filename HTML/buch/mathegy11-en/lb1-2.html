Rates of change and the derivative

How fast is a falling stone exactly two seconds after it is let go? How quickly is the temperature rising at 2 pm? With a stopwatch you can only ever measure averages over a span of time. If you let the span of time tend to zero, the average becomes the instantaneous rate of change and the secant becomes the tangent. This idea of Newton and Leibniz is called the derivative.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1From the secant to the tangent

Between $t = 2$ and $t = 3$ the distance fallen goes from 20 m to 45 m, so on average $\dfrac{45 - 20}{1} = 25$ m/s. Between 2 and 2.1 it is $\dfrac{22.05 - 20}{0.1} = 20.5$ m/s, between 2 and 2.01 already $20.05$ m/s. In general, with the span of time $h$: $\dfrac{5(2 + h)^2 - 20}{h} = \dfrac{20h + 5h^2}{h} = 20 + 5h$. For $h \to 0$ the instantaneous speed of 20 m/s remains.

Geometrically, every average is the gradient of a secant through two points of the graph. If the second point moves closer and closer, the secant turns into the tangent. Its gradient is the local rate of change.

KEY POINT · DIFFERENCE QUOTIENT AND DERIVATIVE

Difference quotient (average rate of change, gradient of the secant): $\dfrac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}$, on $[a, b]$ also $\dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$.

Differential quotient (local rate of change, gradient of the tangent): $$f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}$$

If this limit exists, $f$ is called differentiable at the point $x_0$, and $f'(x_0)$ is the derivative of $f$ at the point $x_0$.

The tangent at the point $P(x_0, f(x_0))$ has the equation $t(x) = f'(x_0)\,(x - x_0) + f(x_0)$.

Units: the rate of change has the unit “$y$-unit per $x$-unit”: m/s, °C/h, € per item.

Notations: $f'(x_0)$ after Lagrange, $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ after Leibniz, $\dot{s}$ after Newton, above all in physics for derivatives with respect to time.

INTERACTIVE · THE SECANT BECOMES THE TANGENT

Push the point $Q$ towards $P$. The difference quotient approaches the gradient of the tangent.

EXAMPLE 1 · WITH THE h-METHOD

Find the derivative of $f(x) = x^3$ at the point $x_0 = 1$.

$(1 + h)^3 = 1 + 3h + 3h^2 + h^3$, so $\dfrac{(1 + h)^3 - 1}{h} = 3 + 3h + h^2$.

For $h \to 0$: $f'(1) = 3$.

EXAMPLE 2 · EQUATION OF THE TANGENT

Find the tangent to $f(x) = x^2$ at the point $P(1, 1)$.

$\dfrac{(1 + h)^2 - 1}{h} = 2 + h \to 2$, so $f'(1) = 2$.

$t(x) = 2(x - 1) + 1$: $t(x) = 2x - 1$.

At 6 am the thermometer shows 12 °C, at 10 am 20 °C. Find the average rate of change with its unit. What does it tell you about the temperature at 8 am?

2 °C per hour. It tells you nothing certain about 8 am; only with a steady rise would it be 16 °C.

Use the h-method to find the local rate of change of $f(x) = x^2$ at $x_0 = -2$ and of $g(x) = 3x + 1$ at $x_0 = 5$.

$\dfrac{(-2 + h)^2 - 4}{h} = -4 + h \to -4$. For $g$ the difference quotient is always 3, so $g'(5) = 3$.

A ball flies according to $h(t) = 20t - 5t^2$ ($h$ in m, $t$ in s). How fast is it rising at $t = 1$? When is it highest?

At the highest point the instantaneous speed is zero.

$\dfrac{h(1 + k) - h(1)}{k} = 10 - 5k \to 10$ m/s. In general the derivative is $20 - 10t$, zero at $t = 2$.

Show: for the parabola $f(x) = x^2$, the secant through the points $a$ and $b$ is parallel to the tangent at the point $\dfrac{a + b}{2}$.

Gradient of the secant $\dfrac{b^2 - a^2}{b - a} = a + b$. Gradient of the tangent at the midpoint: $2 \cdot \dfrac{a + b}{2} = a + b$. Equal, so parallel.

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2The derivative function

The tangents to the standard parabola at $x = -2$, $-1$, $0$, $1$, $2$ have the gradients $-4$, $-2$, $0$, $2$, $4$. The pattern: gradient $= 2x$. You can prove it for every point $x$ at once: $\dfrac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \dfrac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h \to 2x$.

If you assign to every point $x$ the gradient $f'(x)$, you get a new function, the derivative function $f'$. Its graph shows at a glance where $f$ increases ($f' > 0$), where $f$ decreases ($f' < 0$) and where the tangent is horizontal ($f' = 0$).

KEY POINT · DERIVATIVE FUNCTION

The function $f': x \mapsto f'(x)$ is called the derivative function (in short: the derivative) of $f$. Finding the derivative is called differentiating.

From the definition: $(x^2)' = 2x$ · $(x^3)' = 3x^2$ · $(mx + n)' = m$ · $(c)' = 0$ · $\left(\tfrac{1}{x}\right)' = -\tfrac{1}{x^2}$

Sketching the graph of $f'$: maxima and minima of $f$ → zeros of $f'$. Where $f$ increases, $f'$ lies above the $x$-axis; where $f$ decreases, below it.

At a kink (e.g. $|x|$ at 0) $f$ is not differentiable.

Shifting changes nothing: $x^2$ and $x^2 + 5$ have the same derivative $2x$, because shifting upwards changes no gradient.

Thinking backwards: which function has the derivative $4x^3$? For example $x^4$. This reversal of differentiating comes up again in chapter 1.3 and in Year 12.

INTERACTIVE · THE GRAPH OF THE DERIVATIVE APPEARS

Drag the point along the graph of $f$. Its gradient is plotted point by point as the graph of $f'$.

EXAMPLE 3 · FROM THE DEFINITION

Find the derivative function of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$.

$\dfrac{\frac{1}{x + h} - \frac{1}{x}}{h} = \dfrac{x - (x + h)}{h \cdot x(x + h)} = -\dfrac{1}{x(x + h)}$.

For $h \to 0$: $f'(x) = -\dfrac{1}{x^2}$, negative on both branches.

EXAMPLE 4 · SKETCHING THE GRAPH OF f′

$f$ is a cubic function with a maximum at $x = -1$ and a minimum at $x = 1$. Sketch $f'$.

Zeros of $f'$ at $-1$ and $1$. In between, $f$ decreases: $f' < 0$. Outside, $f$ increases: $f' > 0$.

$f'$ is an upward-opening parabola with the zeros $-1$ and $1$, for example $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3$ for $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$.

EXERCISES

Use $f'(x) = 2x$ to find: a) the gradient of the standard parabola at $x = 3$   b) the point with gradient 4   c) the point at which the tangent is parallel to $y = -6x + 1$.

a) 6   b) $x = 2$, point $(2, 4)$   c) $2x = -6$, so $x = -3$

Find the tangent to $f(x) = x^2$ at $x_0 = -1$.

$f(-1) = 1$, $f'(-1) = -2$: $t(x) = -2(x + 1) + 1 = -2x - 1$

Differentiate $f(x) = x^3$ from the definition. The difference quotient is $3x^2 + 3xh + h^2$.

For $h \to 0$, $3xh$ and $h^2$ drop out: $f'(x) = 3x^2$.

The graph of $f'$ is the horizontal line $y = 3$. What do you know about $f$? And what if $f'$ is an increasing line through $(2, 0)$?

$f$ is a straight line with gradient 3. In the second case $f$ is an upward-opening parabola with its vertex at $x = 2$.

Use the one-sided limits of the difference quotient to explain why $f(x) = |x|$ is not differentiable at the point 0.

$\dfrac{|h|}{h} = 1$ for $h > 0$ and $-1$ for $h < 0$. The one-sided limits are different; there is no tangent.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

The costs of a production are $K(x) = 0.5x^2 + 10$ (in €). How fast do they rise on average between 10 and 20 items? How fast exactly at 10 items?

$\dfrac{210 - 60}{10} = 15$ € per item. Locally: $K'(x) = x$, so €10 per item.

Connecting with chapter 1.1: explain why you cannot calculate the derivative simply by substituting $h = 0$.

For $h = 0$ you get $\dfrac{0}{0}$. You first have to simplify (cancel) and then take the limit, exactly as with the holes in chapter 1.1.

A car drives for an hour at an average speed of 80 km/h. What is certain, what is not?

Certain: 80 km in one hour. Not certain: the speed at any particular moment. But at some point the speedometer shows exactly 80 km/h, as long as the car does not jump.

The average rate of change of $f$ on $[a, b]$ is 0. Does it follow that $f$ is constant there? Give a counterexample.

No. $f(x) = x^2$ on $[-1, 1]$: $f(-1) = f(1)$, but $f$ decreases and increases in between.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Newton versus Leibniz

Isaac Newton developed his “method of fluxions” as early as 1665 and 1666 in Cambridge and wrote it out in detail around 1671, but did not publish it. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz found his own way and published it in October 1684 in the Leipzig journal “Acta Eruditorum” under the title “Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis”, a new method for maxima and minima.

This led to one of the most bitter priority disputes in the history of science. Today there is agreement: Newton was there first, Leibniz found his method independently and published first. It is above all Leibniz’s notation that has prevailed: $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ for the derivative and the sign $\int$ for the integral.

Sources: G. W. Leibniz, “Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis”, Acta Eruditorum, October 1684, pp. 467–473. Newton’s treatise on fluxions of 1671 only appeared after his death, in English in 1736 in the translation by John Colson.
