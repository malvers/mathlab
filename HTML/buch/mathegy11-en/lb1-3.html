Rules of differentiation

Working out every derivative from the limit of the difference quotient would be laborious. Fortunately a few rules are enough: with the power, constant multiple and sum rules you differentiate every polynomial function in seconds, with the product rule and the chain rule composite functions as well. On top of that come three special functions: $e^x$, which is equal to its own derivative, $\ln x$ and $\sin x$.

UNIT 1WITHOUT AIDS · 4 LESSONS

1Power, constant multiple and sum rules

The pattern is striking: $(x^2)' = 2x$, $(x^3)' = 3x^2$, $(x^4)' = 4x^3$. The exponent moves to the front as a factor and becomes 1 smaller. $\tfrac{1}{x} = x^{-1}$ fits too: $-1 \cdot x^{-2} = -\tfrac{1}{x^2}$. This power rule even holds for fractional exponents such as $\sqrt{x} = x^{\frac{1}{2}}$.

Two further rules follow directly from the limit theorems: a constant factor stays, and a sum is differentiated term by term. Deriving the sum rule: $\dfrac{(u + v)(x + h) - (u + v)(x)}{h} = \dfrac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} + \dfrac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} \to u'(x) + v'(x)$.

KEY POINT · THE FIRST RULES OF DIFFERENTIATION

Power rule: $(x^r)' = r \cdot x^{r - 1}$ for every rational number $r$ · constant: $(c)' = 0$

Constant multiple rule: $(c \cdot f)' = c \cdot f'$ · sum rule: $(u + v)' = u' + v'$

Frequently used: $\left(\sqrt{x}\right)' = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ · $\left(\dfrac{1}{x}\right)' = -\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ · $\left(\dfrac{1}{x^2}\right)' = -\dfrac{2}{x^3}$

Higher derivatives: $f''$ is the derivative of $f'$, $f'''$ that of $f''$.

Rewrite first: $\dfrac{x^2 + 4}{x} = x + 4x^{-1}$ and $x^2(x + 1) = x^3 + x^2$ can then be differentiated term by term.

Backwards: which function has the derivative $3x^2$? $x^3$, but also $x^3 + 5$. In physics: from $v(t) = 2t$ you get the distance $s(t) = t^2$ if you start at $s(0) = 0$.

EXAMPLE 1 · TERM BY TERM

Differentiate: $f(x) = 2x^3 - 4x^2 + x - 9$ and $g(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 4}{x}$.

$f'(x) = 6x^2 - 8x + 1$; the constant drops out.

$g(x) = x + 4x^{-1}$: $g'(x) = 1 - \dfrac{4}{x^2}$, without any quotient rule.

EXAMPLE 2 · TANGENT

Find the tangent to $f(x) = x^3$ at the point $x_0 = 2$.

$f(2) = 8$, $f'(x) = 3x^2$, $f'(2) = 12$.

$t(x) = 12(x - 2) + 8$: $t(x) = 12x - 16$.

2$e^x$, $\ln x$, $\sin x$ and the product rule

At $x = 0$, $2^x$ has the gradient $0.69$, $3^x$ the gradient $1.10$. In between lies a base with gradient exactly 1: Euler’s number $e \approx 2.718$. For $e^x$ the gradient at every point is as large as the function value: $(e^x)' = e^x$. Its inverse function $\ln x$ has the derivative $\tfrac{1}{x}$.

For the sine curve (in radians) you can see from the graph: where $\sin x$ rises most steeply, at $x = 0$, the gradient is 1; at the maxima it is 0. That is exactly how $\cos x$ runs. For products it is not enough to differentiate the factors separately: $(x \cdot x)' = 2x$, but $1 \cdot 1 = 1$.

KEY POINT · SPECIAL FUNCTIONS, PRODUCT RULE

$(e^x)' = e^x$ · $(\ln x)' = \dfrac{1}{x}$ for $x > 0$ · $(\sin x)' = \cos x$ · $(\cos x)' = -\sin x$

Product rule: $(u \cdot v)' = u' \cdot v + u \cdot v'$

Example: $(x \cdot e^x)' = 1 \cdot e^x + x \cdot e^x = (1 + x)\,e^x$

Without aids, the basic course does without the quotient rule. You write fractions as a product with a negative exponent or differentiate them with the CAS.

Radians: the rule $(\sin x)' = \cos x$ only holds in radians. Set the CAS to RAD.

Trap: $(e^x)' = x \cdot e^{x-1}$ is wrong. The power rule only holds when $x$ is in the base, not in the exponent.

INTERACTIVE · TANGENT AND NORMAL

Choose $e^x$ and drag the point of contact. Observe: the gradient of the tangent and the function value are always equal.

EXAMPLE 3 · PRODUCT RULE

Differentiate: $f(x) = x^2 \sin x$ and $g(x) = x \ln x$.

$f'(x) = 2x \sin x + x^2 \cos x$.

$g'(x) = 1 \cdot \ln x + x \cdot \dfrac{1}{x}$, so $g'(x) = \ln x + 1$.

EXAMPLE 4 · HORIZONTAL TANGENT

Where does $f(x) = (x - 2)\,e^x$ have a horizontal tangent?

$f'(x) = 1 \cdot e^x + (x - 2)\,e^x = (x - 1)\,e^x$.

$e^x$ is never zero, so $x = 1$, at the point $(1, -e)$.

A classmate calculates $(x \cdot e^x)' = 1 \cdot e^x$. Explain the mistake and correct it.

They differentiated the factors separately. Correct with the product rule: $e^x + x e^x$.

Use the product rule to show: for $f(x) = x^n \cdot x$ you get the power rule for $x^{n+1}$ again if it holds for $x^n$.

$f'(x) = n x^{n-1} \cdot x + x^n \cdot 1 = (n + 1)x^n$. This is how you prove the power rule step by step for all natural numbers $n$.

3The chain rule

If A turns twice as fast as B and B three times as fast as C, then A turns six times as fast as C. For composite functions, rates of change are multiplied. For $f(x) = (3x + 1)^4$ you first calculate $3x + 1$ (inner function), then take the fourth power (outer function). The outer function changes with $4z^3$, the inner one with 3, together $f'(x) = 4(3x + 1)^3 \cdot 3 = 12(3x + 1)^3$.

KEY POINT · CHAIN RULE

If $f(x) = u\bigl(v(x)\bigr)$ with inner function $v$ and outer function $u$, then $$f'(x) = u'\bigl(v(x)\bigr) \cdot v'(x)$$

In short: outer derivative times inner derivative.

$\left(e^{kx}\right)' = k\,e^{kx}$ · $\bigl(\sin(ax + b)\bigr)' = a\cos(ax + b)$ · $\bigl(\ln(ax + b)\bigr)' = \dfrac{a}{ax + b}$

Without aids: simple compositions and combinations such as $3x^2 \cdot e^{2x - 1}$ or $\ln(3x - 5)$; with aids, any functions.

Find the inside first: what does the calculator work out first? That is the inner function. For $e^{x^2}$ it is $x^2$, for $\sqrt{4x + 1}$ it is $4x + 1$.

Check: $(x^2 + 1)^2$ can also be multiplied out: $x^4 + 2x^2 + 1$ differentiated is $4x^3 + 4x = 4x(x^2 + 1)$, as with the chain rule.

INTERACTIVE · CHAIN RULE KIT

Choose an outer and an inner function. The widget assembles $f(x) = u(v(x))$ and its derivative. Drag the point: the chain rule and the difference quotient give the same gradient.

EXAMPLE 5 · FROM THE CURRICULUM

Differentiate: $f(x) = 3x^2 \cdot e^{2x - 1}$.

Product rule with $u = 3x^2$, $u' = 6x$ and $v = e^{2x - 1}$, $v' = 2e^{2x - 1}$ (chain rule).

$f'(x) = 6x\,e^{2x - 1} + 6x^2 e^{2x - 1}$ = $6x(1 + x)\,e^{2x - 1}$.

EXAMPLE 6 · BREAKDOWN

A drug is broken down according to $A(t) = 100\,e^{-0.2t}$ ($A$ in mg, $t$ in h). How fast does the amount change at the start and after 5 hours?

$A'(t) = 100 \cdot (-0.2)\,e^{-0.2t} = -20\,e^{-0.2t}$.

$A'(0) = -20$ mg/h, $A'(5) = -20e^{-1} \approx -7.4$ mg/h: the breakdown slows down.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

The first textbook of analysis

A few years after Leibniz’s publication the French Marquis Guillaume de L’Hospital wanted to learn the new calculus. He had himself taught by the young mathematician Johann Bernoulli from Basel and even paid him to tell him new results. In 1696 the “Analyse des infiniment petits” appeared in Paris, at first without the author’s name: the first textbook of differential calculus.

The book explains tangents, maxima and minima, points of inflection and curvature, exactly what you learn in this chapter and the next. How much of it came from Bernoulli only became clear in the 20th century, when a copy of Bernoulli’s lectures was rediscovered in Basel. A rule for limits still bears L’Hospital’s name today, although Bernoulli claimed it for himself.

Source: R. E. Bradley, S. J. Petrilli, C. E. Sandifer: “L’Hôpital’s Analyse des infiniments petits. An Annotated Translation with Source Material by Johann Bernoulli”, Birkhäuser 2015.
