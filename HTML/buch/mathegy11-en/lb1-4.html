Investigating functions

When is the concentration of a drug highest? When does a disease spread fastest? At what number of items is the profit largest? With the derivative you find maxima and minima, with the second derivative points of inflection. Add to that zeros, symmetry, poles and asymptotes. What matters is not working through a routine, but the question: what is of interest in the context?

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Monotonicity and extrema

For $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$, $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3$ is positive to the left of $-1$, negative between $-1$ and $1$ and positive again to the right of $1$. So $f$ increases, decreases, increases. Where $f'$ changes sign from plus to minus there is a maximum $H(-1, 2)$, where it changes from minus to plus a minimum $T(1, -2)$. For $g(x) = x^3$, $g'(0) = 0$, but $g'$ does not change sign: no extremum, but a saddle point.

KEY POINT · MONOTONICITY AND EXTREMA

$f'(x) > 0$ on the interval: $f$ strictly increasing · $f'(x) < 0$: strictly decreasing.

Necessary condition for a local extremum at the point $x_E$: $f'(x_E) = 0$.

Sufficient condition: $f'(x_E) = 0$ and $f'$ changes sign at $x_E$, or $f'(x_E) = 0$ and $f''(x_E) \neq 0$. $f''(x_E) < 0$: maximum · $f''(x_E) > 0$: minimum.

Global extrema on an interval: compare the local extrema and the values at the ends.

Notations: $[a, b]$ with the ends, $(a, b)$ without the ends, $[a, \infty)$ open to the right, $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$: all real numbers except 0.

Necessary ≠ sufficient: without $f'(x_E) = 0$ there is no extremum (for differentiable functions), but $f'(x_E) = 0$ alone guarantees none.

EXAMPLE 1 · TURNING POINTS

Find the turning points of $f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$.

$f'(x) = 4x^3 - 4x = 4x(x^2 - 1) = 0 \Rightarrow x \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$; $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 4$.

$f''(0) = -4$: $H(0, 0)$; $f''(\pm 1) = 8$: $T_{1,2}(\pm 1, -1)$.

EXAMPLE 2 · GLOBAL AT THE END

Where is $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$ largest on $[-3, 3]$?

Local: $f(-1) = 2$ (maximum). Ends: $f(-3) = -18$, $f(3) = 18$.

Global maximum 18 at the end $x = 3$, not at the local maximum.

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Points of inflection, zeros and symmetry

The flu wave $f(t) = -t^3 + 6t^2$ has its maximum where $f'(t) = -3t^2 + 12t = 0$, so after 4 weeks. But the new cases increase most strongly earlier: where $f'$ itself is at its maximum. That is the zero of $f''(t) = -6t + 12$, so after 2 weeks. At this point the graph changes from a left bend to a right bend: a point of inflection.

KEY POINT · CURVATURE, POINTS OF INFLECTION, ZEROS, SYMMETRY

$f''(x) > 0$: convex (left bend) · $f''(x) < 0$: concave (right bend). A point of inflection is a point at which the curvature changes.

Necessary: $f''(x_W) = 0$. Sufficient: in addition a change of sign of $f''$ or $f'''(x_W) \neq 0$. A point of inflection with $f'(x_W) = 0$ is called a saddle point.

Zeros: factor out, zero product rule, substitution $z = x^2$, otherwise CAS. Double zero: the graph touches the $x$-axis.

Symmetry: $f(-x) = f(x)$: symmetric about the $y$-axis (polynomial: only even exponents) · $f(-x) = -f(x)$: point-symmetric about the origin (only odd exponents).

Meaning in context: the point of inflection marks the point of strongest increase or decrease. For epidemics, sales figures and growth it is often more interesting than the maximum.

Careful: $f(x) = x^4$ has $f''(0) = 0$, but $f''(x) = 12x^2$ does not change sign: no point of inflection, but a minimum.

EXAMPLE 3 · INFLECTIONAL TANGENT

Find the point of inflection and the inflectional tangent of $f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2$.

$f''(x) = 6x - 6 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 1$, $f'''(x) = 6 \neq 0$. $f(1) = -2$: $W(1, -2)$.

$f'(1) = 3 - 6 = -3$: $t(x) = -3x + 1$.

EXAMPLE 4 · SUBSTITUTION

Find the zeros and the symmetry of $f(x) = x^4 - 5x^2 + 4$.

Only even exponents: symmetric about the $y$-axis.

$z = x^2$: $z^2 - 5z + 4 = 0$, $z = 1$ or $z = 4$: $x \in \{-2, -1, 1, 2\}$.

UNIT 3WITH CAS · 4 LESSONS

3Poles, asymptotes and investigations in context

The concentration $c(t) = 10t\,e^{-0.5t}$ rises first and then falls. A doctor wants to know: when is the concentration highest, how high is it, when is the drug broken down fastest, what happens in the long run? Symmetry or negative times are of no interest. That is exactly how you work in context: investigate only the relevant properties, on the sensible domain $t \geq 0$, and interpret the result.

KEY POINT · POLES, ASYMPTOTES, PROCEDURE

Pole $x_P$ of a rational function: denominator zero, numerator not. There $x = x_P$ is a vertical asymptote. If the factor cancels out, there is a hole.

Pole with a change of sign (single factor in the denominator) or without (e.g. $\tfrac{1}{x^2}$).

Horizontal asymptote $y = g$ if $f(x) \to g$ for $x \to \pm\infty$ (chapter 1.1).

In context: fix a sensible domain, find the required properties with the CAS, check the sufficient condition, interpret the result with its unit.

The CAS is no proof: if the CAS gives the solution $1.37$ for $f'(x) = 0$, you still have to show that there really is an extremum there (change of sign or $f''$).

Curious: $f(x) = x + \tfrac{1}{x}$ has a maximum $(-1, -2)$ that lies lower than the minimum $(1, 2)$. Local simply means local.

EXAMPLE 5 · A DRUG

Investigate $c(t) = 10t\,e^{-0.5t}$ for $t \geq 0$ for the highest concentration and the fastest breakdown.

$c'(t) = 10e^{-0.5t}(1 - 0.5t) = 0 \Rightarrow t = 2$, change of sign plus/minus: maximum $c(2) = \tfrac{20}{e} \approx 7.36$.

$c''(t) = 10e^{-0.5t}(0.25t - 1) = 0 \Rightarrow t = 4$: fastest breakdown after 4 hours; in the long run $c(t) \to 0$.

EXAMPLE 6 · UNIT COSTS

The unit costs are $k(x) = \dfrac{200 + 5x}{x}$ (in €, $x$ in items). Interpret the pole and the asymptote.

$k(x) = \dfrac{200}{x} + 5$. Pole at $x = 0$: with few items the fixed costs per item are huge.

Asymptote $y = 5$: for large numbers of items the unit costs approach the variable costs of €5 per item.

What does $f(x) = \dfrac{x + 1}{x^2 - 1}$ have at $x = 1$ and at $x = -1$?

At 1 a pole, at $-1$ a hole, because $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x - 1}$ for $x \neq -1$.

Find the turning points of $f(x) = x + \dfrac{1}{x}$ and give its asymptotes.

$f'(x) = 1 - \dfrac{1}{x^2} = 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm 1$: $T(1, 2)$, $H(-1, -2)$. Asymptotes $x = 0$ and the oblique line $y = x$.

A population grows logistically according to $N(t) = \dfrac{1000}{1 + 9e^{-t}}$. Use the CAS to find the time of fastest growth and the population then.

Point of inflection at $t = \ln 9 \approx 2.2$, where $N = 500$: half the carrying capacity.

Give a rational function with a pole without a change of sign at $x = 2$, horizontal asymptote $y = 1$ and $f(0) = 0$.

For example $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 4x}{(x - 2)^2} = 1 - \dfrac{4}{(x - 2)^2}$: $f(0) = 0$, pole without a change of sign at 2, asymptote $y = 1$.

A profit is described by $G(x) = -x^2 + 40x - 300$ ($x$ in items, $G$ in €). Find the largest profit and the break-even point.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Fermat’s trick for maxima

Half a century before Newton and Leibniz, around 1636, the French lawyer and mathematician Pierre de Fermat found a method of determining maxima and minima. His idea: near a maximum the function value hardly changes. If you replace $x$ by $x + e$ with a tiny $e$, then $f(x)$ and $f(x + e)$ are “almost equal”.

Example: a rectangle with perimeter 20 has the area $A(x) = x(10 - x)$. Fermat sets $x(10 - x) \approx (x + e)(10 - x - e)$, cancels equal terms, divides by $e$ and gets $10 - 2x - e \approx 0$. Then he leaves out $e$: $x = 5$. At its core that is our $A'(x) = 0$, just without the word limit.

Source: Fermat’s treatise “Methodus ad disquirendam maximam et minimam” was written around 1636 and only printed in 1679 in his “Varia opera mathematica”. French translation: P. Fermat, Œuvres, volume III, ed. P. Tannery, 1896, pp. 121–156.
