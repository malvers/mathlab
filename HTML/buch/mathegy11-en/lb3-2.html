Lines in space

An aeroplane climbs on a straight course after take-off, a laser beam runs right across a room, two bars of a climbing frame cross without touching. With a point and a direction vector you describe every line in space. Then you check whether a point lies on it, where it pierces a coordinate plane, and how two lines lie relative to each other: in space there is one more case than in the plane.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Lines in parametric form

After $t$ minutes the aeroplane is at the point with the position vector $\vec{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + t \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix}$. After 2 minutes at $(12, 16, 2)$, after 6 minutes at an altitude of 4 km. If you let $t$ run through all real numbers, negative ones too, you get all the points of a line: the start, the past and the future of the course.

KEY POINT · LINES IN SPACE

Parametric form: $g\colon \vec{x} = \vec{p} + t \cdot \vec{u}$ with the position vector $\vec{p}$ (of a point on the line) and the direction vector $\vec{u} \neq \vec{o}$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$.

Line through $A$ and $B$: $\vec{x} = \vec{a} + t \cdot \overrightarrow{AB}$. The line segment $AB$ belongs to $0 \leq t \leq 1$, the midpoint to $t = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

Point test: substitute the point; if all three coordinate equations give the same $t$, it lies on the line.

Trace points: points of intersection with the coordinate planes, e.g. with the $xy$ plane via $z = 0$.

Many equations, one line: any point of the line will do as the base point, and any non-zero multiple of the direction vector as the direction.

Motion: If $t$ is time, then $|\vec{u}|$ is the speed. For the climb: $|\vec{u}| = \sqrt{36 + 64 + 0.25} \approx 10.01$ km per minute.

INTERACTIVE · A LINE IN SPACE

Set the base point and the direction vector, let the point run and do the point test. The widget also shows the trace points.

EXAMPLE 1 · POINT TEST

$g\colon \vec{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} + t \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Do $P(5, 2, 1)$ and $Q(3, 1, 2)$ lie on $g$?

$P$: $1 + 2t = 5$, $2 = 2$, $3 - t = 1$: always $t = 2$, so yes.

$Q$: the $y$-coordinate on $g$ is always 2: $Q$ does not lie on $g$.

EXAMPLE 2 · TRACE POINT

Where does the same line $g$ pierce the $xy$ plane?

$z = 3 - t = 0 \Rightarrow t = 3$.

$S_{xy}(7, 2, 0)$.

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Relative positions of two lines

Two pens on the table intersect, are parallel or lie on top of each other. If you hold them in the air, there is a fourth case: they are not parallel and still do not touch, like the bars of a climbing frame, or an edge of the base and a diagonally opposite edge of the top face of a cube. Such lines are called skew.

KEY POINT · POSITION OF TWO LINES

1. Direction vectors collinear? Yes: point test with the base point of one in the other. If it lies on it: identical, otherwise parallel (and distinct).

2. Not collinear: set the equations of the lines equal. The system has 3 equations for 2 parameters: find $t$ and $s$ with two of them, check with the third.

If the third fits: intersecting; find the point of intersection by substituting. If it does not fit: skew.

Collision? In motion the parameter is time. Two aeroplanes only collide if they are at the point of intersection at the same time: $t = s$.

Outlook: The widget already shows how far apart skew lines are; you will learn to calculate distances in Year 12.

INTERACTIVE · HOW DO TWO LINES LIE?

Set up two lines and rotate the space. The widget decides: identical, parallel, intersecting (with point of intersection) or skew.

EXAMPLE 3 · INTERSECTING

$g\colon \vec{x} = (0, 0, 0) + t(1, 2, 3)$ and $h\colon \vec{x} = (2, 0, 0) + s(-1, 2, 3)$.

Not collinear. $t = 2 - s$, $2t = 2s$, $3t = 3s$: from the second $t = s$, then $t = s = 1$; the third fits.

Point of intersection $S(1, 2, 3)$.

EXAMPLE 4 · SKEW

$g\colon \vec{x} = (1, 2, 0) + t(1, 0, 1)$ and $h\colon \vec{x} = (3, 0, 4) + s(0, 1, 1)$.

Not collinear. $1 + t = 3$, $2 = s$, $t = 4 + s$: from the first two $t = 2$, $s = 2$; third: $2 = 6$, false.

Skew.

Two aeroplanes fly along $\vec{x} = (0, 0, 8) + t(6, 8, 0)$ and $\vec{x} = (60, 0, 8) + s(-6, 8, 0)$ ($t$, $s$ in minutes, lengths in km, both start at the same time). Do the paths intersect? Is there a collision?

$6t = 60 - 6s$, $8t = 8s$: $t = s = 5$, point of intersection $(30, 40, 8)$. Both are there after 5 minutes: collision course! Air traffic control must intervene.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

On a cube $ABCDEFGH$ (base $ABCD$, above it $EFGH$): how do the edges $AB$ and $GH$, $AB$ and $BF$, $AB$ and $CG$ lie relative to each other?

$AB$ and $GH$ parallel; $AB$ and $BF$ intersect at $B$; $AB$ and $CG$ skew.

Connecting with Chapter 2: Write the system of equations from Example 4 as an augmented matrix for $t$ and $s$ and show with Gaussian elimination that it has no solution.

$\left(\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 1 & -1 & 4 \end{array}\right)$; $R_3 - R_1 - R_2$ gives $(0 \; 0 \mid 4)$: contradiction.

A balloon rises from $(2, 3, 0)$ in a straight line along $\vec{x} = (2, 3, 0) + t(1, 0, 2)$ ($t$ in min, lengths in units of 100 m). When does it reach 1000 m, and where is it then?

$2t = 10 \Rightarrow t = 5$ min, point $(7, 3, 10)$.

Three lines pass through the same point $P$ and have the directions $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 1, 0)$, $(1, 1, 0)$. Do they lie in one plane? Justify with Chapter 3.1.

Yes: the three direction vectors are linearly dependent (coplanar), so all three lines lie in the plane through $P$ parallel to the $xy$ plane.

EXCURSIONAVIATION

Why aeroplanes fly on skew lines

Two aeroplanes on the same airway, one heading east, one heading west, would come towards each other on the same line. To prevent this, there is the semicircular rule: anyone flying a course between 0° and 179°, i.e. roughly east, chooses an odd flight level (e.g. flight level 350, i.e. 35,000 feet); anyone flying between 180° and 359° chooses an even one (e.g. flight level 360).

Geometrically this means: the paths of aeroplanes coming towards each other lie in different horizontal planes, at least 1000 feet (about 300 m) apart. If they run parallel, they are parallel lines; if they cross when seen from above, they are skew. A point of intersection is ruled out even before air traffic control does any calculation.

Source: ICAO, Annex 2 “Rules of the Air”, Appendix 3 (table of cruising levels). The reference is the magnetic track; in some airspaces the airways set their own levels, which take precedence.
