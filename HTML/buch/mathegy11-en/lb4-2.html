The binomial distribution

How many of 100 light bulbs are faulty? How many correct answers can you get by guessing? How many of 20 seeds germinate? Each time, a trial with two outcomes is repeated several times independently: a Bernoulli chain. The number of successes is binomially distributed. You learn to calculate its probabilities, to deal with “at most” and “at least” and to use the expected value and the standard deviation to say what is typical.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Bernoulli chains and the binomial coefficient

In the guessing test, each question is a trial with two outcomes: correct (success, $p = \tfrac{1}{4}$) or wrong. A path with exactly 2 successes, for example “correct, correct, wrong, wrong, wrong”, has the probability $\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right)^2 \cdot \left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^3$. Every path with 2 successes has the same probability, so all that matters is the number of paths: you choose the 2 success questions out of 5, which can be done in $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ ways. So $P = 10 \cdot \tfrac{1}{16} \cdot \tfrac{27}{64} \approx 0.264$.

KEY POINT · BERNOULLI CHAIN AND BINOMIAL COEFFICIENT

A Bernoulli experiment has exactly two outcomes, success with probability $p$ and failure with $1 - p$. A Bernoulli chain of length $n$ is the $n$-fold independent repetition with the same $p$.

Factorial: $n! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot \ldots \cdot n$, $0! = 1$. Binomial coefficient: $\dbinom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k! \cdot (n - k)!}$ counts the paths with exactly $k$ successes.

Bernoulli's formula: For the number $X$ of successes, $$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} \cdot p^k \cdot (1 - p)^{n - k}$$

$X$ is called binomially distributed with the parameters $n$ and $p$; you also write $B_{n,\,p}(k)$.

Symmetry: $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n - k}$, because choosing 2 successes is the same as choosing 3 failures.

Not a Bernoulli chain: drawing without replacement from a small urn, because $p$ changes. For large populations (deliveries, the population of a country) you still calculate binomially as an approximation.

INTERACTIVE · THE TREE OF A BERNOULLI CHAIN

Choose $n$ and $k$: all paths with exactly $k$ successes light up. Their number is the binomial coefficient.

EXAMPLE 1 · TWO SIXES

A dice is rolled three times. How likely are exactly two sixes?

$n = 3$, $p = \tfrac{1}{6}$, $k = 2$: $\binom{3}{2} \cdot \left(\tfrac{1}{6}\right)^2 \cdot \tfrac{5}{6} = 3 \cdot \tfrac{5}{216}$.

$\tfrac{15}{216} = \tfrac{5}{72} \approx 0.069$.

EXAMPLE 2 · BINOMIAL COEFFICIENTS

Calculate $\binom{10}{3}$ and $\binom{20}{18}$.

$\binom{10}{3} = \dfrac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8}{3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = 120$.

$\binom{20}{18} = \binom{20}{2} = \dfrac{20 \cdot 19}{2} = 190$.

INTERACTIVE · PASCAL'S TRIANGLE

Each number is the sum of the two above it. This is how all the binomial coefficients arise.

2Single and cumulative probabilities

Of 10 seeds with germination probability 0.9, all germinate with probability $0.9^{10} \approx 0.349$, exactly 9 with $10 \cdot 0.9^9 \cdot 0.1 \approx 0.387$. But a gardener is more likely to ask: how likely is it that at least 8 germinate? For this you add single probabilities: $P(X \geq 8) = P(X = 8) + P(X = 9) + P(X = 10) \approx 0.194 + 0.387 + 0.349 = 0.930$.

KEY POINT · CUMULATIVE PROBABILITIES

Cumulative: $P(X \leq k) = \displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k} \binom{n}{i} p^i (1 - p)^{n - i}$

“at most $k$”: $X \leq k$ · “fewer than $k$”: $X \leq k - 1$ · “at least $k$”: $X \geq k$, calculated as $1 - P(X \leq k - 1)$ · “more than $k$”: $X \geq k + 1$

“between $a$ and $b$” (inclusive): $P(a \leq X \leq b) = P(X \leq b) - P(X \leq a - 1)$

The histogram shows a bar of height $P(X = k)$ above each $k$; for $p = 0.5$ it is symmetric, for small $p$ it is skewed to the right.

Most common mistake: $P(X \geq 3) = 1 - P(X \leq 3)$ is wrong, because 3 belongs to “at least 3”. Correct: $1 - P(X \leq 2)$.

With the CAS: There are separate commands for $P(X = k)$ (binomPdf) and $P(X \leq k)$ (binomCdf). Translate first, then type it in.

INTERACTIVE · BINOMIAL DISTRIBUTION

Set $n$ and $p$ and mark ranges: $P(X = k)$, $P(X \leq k)$, $P(X \geq k)$ or $P(a \leq X \leq b)$. $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are shown too.

EXAMPLE 3 · AT LEAST THREE CORRECT

Guessing test with 5 questions ($p = \tfrac{1}{4}$): how likely is it to pass with at least 3 correct answers?

$P(X \geq 3) = \binom{5}{3}\tfrac{9}{1024} + \binom{5}{4}\tfrac{3}{1024} + \tfrac{1}{1024} = \tfrac{90 + 15 + 1}{1024}$.

$\tfrac{106}{1024} \approx 0.104$: guessing does not pay.

EXAMPLE 4 · AT MOST ONE

20 % of people are left-handed. How likely is it that in a group of 10 at most one is left-handed?

$P(X \leq 1) = 0.8^{10} + 10 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 0.8^9 \approx 0.107 + 0.268$.

$\approx 0.376$.

3Expected value, variance and standard deviation

With 60 rolls you expect $60 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} = 10$ sixes. But in the class, 7, 12 or 14 occur too. How far the results typically deviate from the expected value is measured by the standard deviation. For binomially distributed random variables there are simple formulae, here $\sigma = \sqrt{60 \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} \cdot \tfrac{5}{6}} \approx 2.9$: results between about 7 and 13 are perfectly normal.

KEY POINT · PARAMETERS OF THE BINOMIAL DISTRIBUTION

Expected value: $E(X) = \mu = n \cdot p$ · Variance: $V(X) = n \cdot p \cdot (1 - p)$ · Standard deviation: $\sigma = \sqrt{n \cdot p \cdot (1 - p)}$

$E(X)$ is the mean in the long run, not the certain result. $\sigma$ measures the spread about $E(X)$ and has the same unit as $X$.

In the histogram the highest bar is close to $\mu$; the larger $\sigma$, the wider the distribution. For a fixed $n$ the spread is largest at $p = 0.5$.

Growing n: If you multiply $n$ by four, $\mu$ is multiplied by four, but $\sigma$ only doubles. Relative to the expected value the spread becomes smaller: that is the law of large numbers.

Backwards: From $E(X) = 6$ and $V(X) = 4.2$ it follows that $1 - p = 0.7$, so $p = 0.3$ and $n = 20$.

INTERACTIVE · EXPECTED VALUE AND SPREAD

A random variable with bar chart, $E(X)$, $V(X)$ and $\sigma$. Let it run a thousand times: the mean of the results approaches $E(X)$.

EXAMPLE 5 · QUALITY CONTROL

2 % of the components are faulty. How many faulty ones do you expect among 1000? What is $\sigma$?

$\mu = 1000 \cdot 0.02 = 20$.

$\sigma = \sqrt{1000 \cdot 0.02 \cdot 0.98} = \sqrt{19.6} \approx 4.43$.

EXAMPLE 6 · σ INTERVAL

A coin is tossed 100 times. In which range does the number of heads typically lie?

$\mu = 50$, $\sigma = \sqrt{25} = 5$.

$[\mu - \sigma,\,\mu + \sigma] = [45,\,55]$; about two thirds of all results lie there.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Bernoulli's golden theorem

The Basel mathematician Jacob Bernoulli worked for about twenty years on the question of how to infer probabilities from observations. His main work, “Ars Conjectandi”, the art of conjecturing, only appeared in 1713, eight years after his death, edited by his nephew Nicolaus. It contains not only the formula for Bernoulli chains but also his “golden theorem”: the first law of large numbers. The more often you repeat an experiment, the more certainly the relative frequency lies close to the probability.

You can see this on the Galton board. Francis Galton had it built in 1873: balls bounce randomly to the left or right at each row of pins, a Bernoulli chain with $p = 0.5$. The number of routes to a slot is a binomial coefficient, and the balls form a bell shape. Galton described the device in 1889 in his book “Natural Inheritance”.

Sources: J. Bernoulli, “Ars Conjectandi”, Basel 1713, Part IV. F. Galton, “Natural Inheritance”, London: Macmillan 1889, pp. 63–70.
