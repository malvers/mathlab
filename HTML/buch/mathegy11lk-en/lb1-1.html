Limits and continuity

A cup of tea cools down, but never below room temperature. A function has a hole, and still you know exactly which value belongs in it. A taxi fare changes its price per kilometre at 5 km without the bill jumping. In the advanced course “comes arbitrarily close” becomes an exact concept: you learn the ε definition of the limit, calculate with the limit theorems and investigate continuity, also for functions that are defined piecewise.

UNIT 15 LESSONS

1Behaviour at infinity

After 10 minutes the tea is at about 45.8 °C, after an hour at 20.2 °C, after two hours at 20.0004 °C. The distance from 20 is $70 \cdot e^{-0.1t}$. It becomes smaller than 0.1 as soon as $t > 10 \ln 700 \approx 65.5$, and smaller than 0.001 from $t \approx 111.6$ on. For every bound, however small, there is such a point in time. Exactly that is what makes a limit.

KEY POINT · LIMIT FOR x → ∞

$\lim\limits_{x \to \infty} f(x) = g$ means: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a point $x_\varepsilon$ such that $|f(x) - g| < \varepsilon$ for all $x > x_\varepsilon$. The line $y = g$ is then a horizontal asymptote. Likewise for $x \to -\infty$.

Limit theorems: if $\lim f = a$ and $\lim h = b$ exist, then $\lim (f \pm h) = a \pm b$, $\lim (f \cdot h) = a \cdot b$ and $\lim \tfrac{f}{h} = \tfrac{a}{b}$ for $b \neq 0$.

Who wins? Polynomial: the term with the highest power. Rational: compare the degrees of numerator and denominator. $e^x$ grows faster than every power $x^n$, and every power $x^r$ with $r > 0$ faster than $\ln x$: $\;x^n e^{-x} \to 0$, $\;\tfrac{\ln x}{x^r} \to 0$.

Oblique asymptote: if $f(x) - (mx + n) \to 0$, then $y = mx + n$ is an asymptote. For rational functions with degree of the numerator = degree of the denominator + 1, polynomial division gives it.

Calculation trick: divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of the denominator, then apply the limit theorems: $\tfrac{3x^2 - x}{x^2 + 4} = \tfrac{3 - \frac1x}{1 + \frac{4}{x^2}} \to 3$.

“→ ∞” is not a limit: if $f(x)$ grows beyond every bound, you write $f(x) \to \infty$, but the function then diverges to infinity. $\sin x$ has no limit at all for $x \to \infty$.

Polynomial division with the CAS: the command propFrac or expand, depending on the calculator.

EXAMPLE 1 · PROOF WITH ε

Show $\lim\limits_{x \to \infty} \tfrac{2x + 1}{x} = 2$ with the definition. From which $x$ on is the distance smaller than 0.001?

$\left|\tfrac{2x + 1}{x} - 2\right| = \tfrac{1}{x}$ for $x > 0$. For any $\varepsilon > 0$ choose $x_\varepsilon = \tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}$: for $x > x_\varepsilon$ we have $\tfrac1x < \varepsilon$.

For $\varepsilon = 0.001$: from $x > 1000$ on.

EXAMPLE 2 · OBLIQUE ASYMPTOTE

Find the behaviour of $f(x) = \tfrac{x^2 + 1}{x}$ for $x \to \pm\infty$.

$f(x) = x + \tfrac1x$. The term $\tfrac1x$ tends to 0, so $f(x) - x \to 0$.

Oblique asymptote $y = x$; $f(x) \to \infty$ for $x \to \infty$ and $f(x) \to -\infty$ for $x \to -\infty$.

“The exponential function beats every power” – even $1.1^x$ against $x^{10}$? Use the CAS to find the whole number $x \geq 2$ from which on $1.1^x > x^{10}$. Justify the limit $\lim\limits_{x \to \infty} \tfrac{x^{10}}{1.1^x}$.

Compare $\ln(1.1^x) = x \ln 1.1$ with $\ln(x^{10}) = 10 \ln x$.

From $x = 686$ on. Reason: $x \ln 1.1$ grows linearly, $10 \ln x$ only logarithmically, their difference tends to $\infty$. So $\tfrac{x^{10}}{1.1^x} = e^{10\ln x - x \ln 1.1} \to 0$. The exponential function takes a long time here, but it wins for sure.

UNIT 25 LESSONS

2Limit at a point

For $x \neq 2$, $\tfrac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = x + 2$. The graph is the line $y = x + 2$ with a hole at $(2, 4)$. For $f(x)$ to differ from 4 by less than 0.01, we need $|x + 2 - 4| = |x - 2| < 0.01$. So here every $\varepsilon$ comes with $\delta = \varepsilon$. The function value at the point itself does not matter, only the values nearby.

KEY POINT · LIMIT AT A POINT

$\lim\limits_{x \to x_0} f(x) = g$ means: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a $\delta > 0$ such that $|f(x) - g| < \varepsilon$ for all $x$ with $0 < |x - x_0| < \delta$.

One-sided limits: $\lim\limits_{x \to x_0^-} f(x)$ (from the left) and $\lim\limits_{x \to x_0^+} f(x)$ (from the right). The limit exists if and only if both exist and are equal.

h-method: calculate $\lim\limits_{h \to 0^+} f(x_0 - h)$ and $\lim\limits_{h \to 0^+} f(x_0 + h)$. The limit theorems hold at a point just as at infinity.

Tools for “$\tfrac00$”: factorise and cancel, multiply by the conjugate root term, use known limits such as $\lim\limits_{x \to 0} \tfrac{\sin x}{x} = 1$.

Piecewise-defined functions: to the left and to the right of the joining point different terms apply. Then you have to calculate one-sidedly, each time with the matching term.

No limit: $\tfrac{|x|}{x}$ has the limit −1 on the left of 0 and 1 on the right. $\sin\tfrac1x$ oscillates infinitely often between −1 and 1 near 0.

EXAMPLE 3 · h-METHOD

Find $\lim\limits_{x \to 3} \tfrac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$ with the h-method.

$f(3 + h) = \tfrac{(3 + h)^2 - 9}{h} = \tfrac{6h + h^2}{h} = 6 + h \to 6$ for $h \to 0$, from the left with $3 - h$ likewise: $6 - h \to 6$.

Limit 6, although $f(3)$ is not defined.

EXAMPLE 4 · MULTIPLYING BY THE ROOT

Find $\lim\limits_{x \to 0} \tfrac{\sqrt{x + 4} - 2}{x}$.

Multiply numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{x + 4} + 2$: $\tfrac{(x + 4) - 4}{x(\sqrt{x + 4} + 2)} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{x + 4} + 2}$.

Now substitute (limit theorems): $\tfrac{1}{2 + 2} = \tfrac14$

Find $\lim\limits_{x \to 9} \tfrac{\sqrt{x} - 3}{x - 9}$.

$x - 9 = (\sqrt{x} - 3)(\sqrt{x} + 3)$, so $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{x} + 3} \to \tfrac16$.

For $f(x) = x^3$, calculate the limit $\lim\limits_{h \to 0} \tfrac{f(2 + h) - f(2)}{h}$. What does it mean for the graph? (A look ahead to chapter 1.2)

$\tfrac{(2 + h)^3 - 8}{h} = \tfrac{12h + 6h^2 + h^3}{h} = 12 + 6h + h^2 \to 12$. That is the gradient of the tangent to the graph at the point $(2, 8)$.

Prove with the ε-δ definition: $\lim\limits_{x \to 2} (3x + 1) = 7$. Give a suitable $\delta$ for $\varepsilon$.

$|3x + 1 - 7| = 3|x - 2|$. This number is smaller than $\varepsilon$ as soon as $|x - 2| < \tfrac{\varepsilon}{3}$. So $\delta = \tfrac{\varepsilon}{3}$ does what is required, and it does so for every $\varepsilon > 0$.

UNIT 35 LESSONS

3Continuity

For the taxi the price up to 5 km is $4 + 2.2x$, after that $15 + 1.8(x - 5)$. At $x = 5$ both terms give €15: the price does not jump, it only changes its gradient. The car park, on the other hand, jumps by €2 at every full hour. The difference has a name: the taxi function is continuous, the car park function is not.

KEY POINT · CONTINUITY

$f$ is called continuous at the point $x_0 \in D_f$ if $\lim\limits_{x \to x_0} f(x)$ exists and is equal to $f(x_0)$. Otherwise $x_0$ is a point of discontinuity.

$f$ is called continuous on an interval if it is continuous at every point of the interval (one-sidedly at closed end points), and continuous if it is continuous at every point of its domain.

Sums, products, quotients (where the denominator is not zero) and compositions of continuous functions are continuous. Hence all polynomial functions, $e^x$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $\sqrt{x}$ on $[0, \infty)$, $\ln x$ on $(0, \infty)$ and rational functions on their domain are continuous.

Intervals: $[a, b]$ with, $(a, b)$ without end points, $[a, \infty)$, and $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ for “all real numbers except 0”.

Careful: $\tfrac1x$ is continuous, because it is continuous at every point of its domain. The point 0 does not belong to it at all.

Intermediate value theorem: if $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have different signs, then $f$ has a zero in between. The bisection method in the elective area builds on this.

EXAMPLE 5 · FINDING THE PARAMETER

For which $a$ is $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ for $x \leq 1$ and $f(x) = ax - 1$ for $x > 1$ continuous?

Left: $\lim\limits_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = f(1) = 2$. Right: $\lim\limits_{x \to 1^+} (ax - 1) = a - 1$.

Continuous if and only if $a - 1 = 2$: $a = 3$. At all other points $f$ is continuous anyway.

EXAMPLE 6 · KINDS OF DISCONTINUITY

Investigate $g(x) = \tfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$ for $x \neq 1$ and $g(1) = 3$ at the point 1.

$\lim\limits_{x \to 1} g(x) = \lim\limits_{x \to 1} (x + 1) = 2 \neq 3 = g(1)$.

Removable discontinuity: with $g(1) = 2$, $g$ would be continuous. Other kinds: jump (one-sided limits differ) and pole.

Is $f(x) = x \cdot \sin\tfrac1x$ for $x \neq 0$ and $f(0) = 0$ continuous at the point 0? Justify without a calculator.

A population grows according to $N(t) = \tfrac{500}{1 + 9e^{-0.5t}}$. Give $N(0)$, the limit for $t \to \infty$ and the asymptote.

$N(0) = 50$. $e^{-0.5t} \to 0$, so $N(t) \to 500$: asymptote $N = 500$, the carrying capacity.

Connecting with Year 10: calculate $\left(1 + \tfrac1n\right)^n$ for $n = 10$, 100, 1000 and $10^6$. Which number does the sequence approach?

2.594; 2.705; 2.717; $2.718\,28$. The limit is Euler’s number $e \approx 2.718\,28$.

Electricity costs €0.40 per kWh up to 1000 kWh a year. Above that a tariff with a standing charge $G$ and €0.30 per kWh for the whole consumption applies. For which $G$ is the annual price as a function of consumption continuous?

At 1000 kWh: $0.40 \cdot 1000 = G + 0.30 \cdot 1000$, so $G = €100$.

Find $\lim\limits_{x \to \infty} \left(\sqrt{x^2 + ax} - x\right)$ in terms of $a$.

Multiply numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{x^2 + ax} + x$ and then divide by $x$.

$\tfrac{x^2 + ax - x^2}{\sqrt{x^2 + ax} + x} = \tfrac{a}{\sqrt{1 + \frac{a}{x}} + 1} \to \tfrac{a}{2}$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

How ε came into analysis

At the end of the 17th century Newton and Leibniz calculated with “infinitely small” quantities that were sometimes zero and sometimes not. This worked astonishingly well, but it was logically open to attack. In 1821, in his textbook for the École polytechnique in Paris, Augustin-Louis Cauchy explained limit and continuity in words: a quantity has a limit if it comes “as close to it as one wishes”.

Today’s version with $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ goes back to Karl Weierstrass. In his Berlin lectures around 1861, which are known only from his students’ notes, he defined continuity like this: for every number $\varepsilon$, however small, there is a bound $\delta$ for the change in the argument. Eduard Heine published the ideas in 1872. Since then analysis no longer needs infinitely small quantities, only inequalities.

Sources: Augustin-Louis Cauchy: “Cours d'analyse de l'École royale polytechnique”, Paris 1821 · Eduard Heine: “Die Elemente der Functionenlehre”, Journal für die reine und angewandte Mathematik 74 (1872).
