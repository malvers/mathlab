Space and vectors

An aeroplane at a height of 10 km, the tip of a roof, the position of Dresden on the globe: you describe points in space with three numbers. Usually these are Cartesian coordinates, but for solids of revolution and for the Earth cylindrical and spherical coordinates are much more convenient. You describe displacements, forces and velocities with vectors. You calculate with them almost as with numbers and justify the laws of arithmetic. They are the tool for lines and planes in the next two chapters.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1The three-dimensional coordinate system

The ceiling light hangs 4 m from one wall, 3 m from the other and 3 m high: $L(4, 3, 3)$. Its distance from the origin corner: The point below it on the floor is $\sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5$ m away; together with the height, $\sqrt{5^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{34} \approx 5.83$ m. So Pythagoras' theorem also holds in space, with three squares under the root.

KEY POINT · POINTS IN SPACE

A point has three coordinates $P(x, y, z)$. The axes are pairwise perpendicular. The three coordinate planes are the $xy$ plane ($z = 0$), the $xz$ plane ($y = 0$) and the $yz$ plane ($x = 0$). They divide space into eight octants.

Distance between two points: $\overline{AB} = \sqrt{(x_B - x_A)^2 + (y_B - y_A)^2 + (z_B - z_A)^2}$

Midpoint: $M\!\left(\tfrac{x_A + x_B}{2}, \tfrac{y_A + y_B}{2}, \tfrac{z_A + z_B}{2}\right)$

Sphere with centre $M(m_1, m_2, m_3)$ and radius $r$: all points with $(x - m_1)^2 + (y - m_2)^2 + (z - m_3)^2 = r^2$.

Oblique drawing: the $x$-axis is usually drawn slanting forwards and shortened, the $y$-axis to the right and the $z$-axis upwards.

Reflecting: In the $xy$ plane only $z$ changes sign, in the $z$-axis $x$ and $y$ do, in the origin all three coordinates.

Software: In 3D programs and in the widget you rotate the coordinate system. An oblique drawing shows only one view, in which different points sometimes look the same.

EXAMPLE 1 · DISTANCE AND MIDPOINT

Given $A(1, 2, 3)$ and $B(4, 6, 3)$. Does $C(4, 2, 7)$ lie on the sphere with centre $A$ through $B$?

$\overline{AB} = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 0^2} = 5$ and $M(2.5, 4, 3)$.

$\overline{AC} = \sqrt{3^2 + 0^2 + 4^2} = 5$: $C$ lies on the sphere with centre $A$ and radius 5.

EXAMPLE 2 · PYRAMID

A right pyramid has the square base with the corners $(\pm 2, \pm 2, 0)$ and the height 6. How long is a lateral edge?

UNIT 22 LESSONS

2Other coordinate systems

Airport radar does not report an aeroplane with $x$, $y$ and $z$ but with three other numbers: how far away it is along the ground, in which direction it lies and how high it is flying. A globe names a place with two angles, longitude and latitude; the distance to the centre of the Earth is the Earth’s radius anyway. Such coordinates suit solids that can be rotated about an axis or about a point.

KEY POINT · CYLINDRICAL AND SPHERICAL COORDINATES

Polar coordinates in the plane: distance $r$ from the origin and angle $\varphi$ to the positive $x$-axis. $x = r\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\varphi$ and back $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$.

Cylindrical coordinates $(r,\,\varphi,\,z)$: polar coordinates in the $xy$ plane, plus the height $z$. Then $x = r\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\varphi$, $z = z$.

Spherical coordinates $(r,\,\varphi,\,\vartheta)$: distance $r$ from the origin, longitude $\varphi$ and latitude $\vartheta$ (angle to the $xy$ plane). Then $x = r\cos\vartheta\cos\varphi$, $y = r\cos\vartheta\sin\varphi$, $z = r\sin\vartheta$.

When converting back, mind the quadrant: $(-1, -1)$ has the angle $225^\circ$, not $45^\circ$, although $\tfrac{y}{x} = 1$ both times.

Which system when? Cylindrical coordinates for rotational symmetry about an axis (can, pipe, radar, spiral staircase), spherical coordinates for symmetry about a point (Earth, sound, light from a lamp).

Another convention: Many university textbooks measure the angle to the $z$-axis instead of the latitude. Then sine and cosine of $\vartheta$ swap places in the formulas.

Navigation: Headings in air traffic are counted clockwise from north, the angle $\varphi$ anticlockwise from the $x$-axis.

Dresden lies at 51.05° north latitude and 13.74° east longitude; the radius of the Earth is about 6371 km. How far is Dresden from the Earth’s axis and from the equatorial plane? How long is the circle of latitude through Dresden?

From the axis: $r\cos\vartheta = 6371 \cdot \cos 51.05^\circ \approx 4005$ km. From the equatorial plane: $z = 6371 \cdot \sin 51.05^\circ \approx 4955$ km. Circle of latitude: $2\pi \cdot 4005 \approx 25{,}165$ km.

Show: for every point in spherical coordinates, $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2$. Why, then, are they called spherical coordinates?

Factor out $r^2\cos^2\vartheta$ and use $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$ twice.

$x^2 + y^2 = r^2\cos^2\vartheta\,(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi) = r^2\cos^2\vartheta$, then $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2(\cos^2\vartheta + \sin^2\vartheta) = r^2$. For fixed $r$, all points lie on the sphere about the origin with radius $r$.

3Calculating with vectors

Pulling on the ring are $\vec{F}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ N and $\vec{F}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ N. If you attach the arrows head to tail, the sum $\vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ N with magnitude $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$ N shows what both do together. The ring only stays at rest if the third rope pulls exactly against it: $\vec{F}_3 = \begin{pmatrix} -3 \\ -4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ N, so that $\vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \vec{F}_3 = \vec{o}$.

KEY POINT · VECTORS

A vector is the set of all arrows of the same length, parallel and pointing the same way. In space you write $\vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \\ a_3 \end{pmatrix}$.

Position vector of $P$: $\vec{p} = \overrightarrow{OP}$. Connecting vector “head minus tail”: $\overrightarrow{AB} = \vec{b} - \vec{a}$. Magnitude: $|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}$.

Calculate component by component: $\vec{a} + \vec{b}$, $\vec{a} - \vec{b}$ and $r \cdot \vec{a}$. Opposite vector $-\vec{a}$, zero vector $\vec{o}$, unit vector $\tfrac{1}{|\vec{a}|}\,\vec{a}$ with length 1.

Laws of arithmetic for all vectors $\vec{a}$, $\vec{b}$, $\vec{c}$ and numbers $r$, $s$: $\vec{a} + \vec{b} = \vec{b} + \vec{a}$, $(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) + \vec{c} = \vec{a} + (\vec{b} + \vec{c})$, $r(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) = r\vec{a} + r\vec{b}$, $(r + s)\vec{a} = r\vec{a} + s\vec{a}$, $r(s\vec{a}) = (rs)\vec{a}$.

Why do the laws hold? Component by component you calculate with numbers, and the laws already hold for numbers. Example: $a_1 + b_1 = b_1 + a_1$ in every row.

Vector quantities: displacement, velocity, force, field strength (physics advanced course 11). Scalar quantities such as mass, time and temperature have no direction.

Triangle inequality: $|\vec{a} + \vec{b}| \leq |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}|$. Equality holds only if both arrows point in the same direction.

EXAMPLE 5 · CALCULATING AND MAGNITUDE

$\vec{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $\vec{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Calculate $2\vec{a} - \vec{b}$ and the unit vector in the direction of $\vec{b}$.

$2\vec{a} - \vec{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 3 \\ 0 - 1 \\ 4 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}$, $|\vec{b}| = \sqrt{9 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt{11}$.

Unit vector $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{11}}\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$

EXAMPLE 6 · EQUILIBRIUM OF FORCES

The forces $\vec{F}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$ N and $\vec{F}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} -4 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ N act on a body. Which third force keeps it in equilibrium, and how large is it?

$\vec{F}_3 = -(\vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2) = -\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix}$ N.

$|\vec{F}_3| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 16} = \sqrt{21} \approx 4.58$ N

Justify the distributive law $r(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) = r\vec{a} + r\vec{b}$ component by component. How can you see it in the picture of two arrows attached head to tail that are stretched by the factor $r$?

In every row $r(a_i + b_i) = ra_i + rb_i$, the distributive law for numbers. Picture: the triangle of $\vec{a}$, $\vec{b}$ and $\vec{a} + \vec{b}$ is enlarged from a centre; the enlarged sum is the sum of the enlarged sides (intercept theorem).

A boat travels at 4 m/s relative to the water straight across a river 60 m wide; the current flows at 3 m/s. How fast is the boat over the ground, how long does it take, and how far is it carried downstream?

$\vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$ m/s, $|\vec{v}| = 5$ m/s. The crossing takes $60 \div 4 = 15$ s; in this time it drifts $3 \cdot 15 = 45$ m.

For which $a$ does $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ have magnitude 3? For which $t$ does $t \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ have magnitude 6? Interpret both results geometrically.

$a^2 + 8 = 9$, so $a = \pm 1$: two arrows, mirror images in the $yz$ plane. $|t| \cdot 3 = 6$, so $t = \pm 2$: two opposite arrows of length 6.

Connecting with chapter 2: The point with cylindrical coordinates $(3,\,30^\circ,\,2)$ is rotated by $90^\circ$ about the $z$-axis with the rotation matrix. Calculate in Cartesian coordinates and show: in cylindrical coordinates the rotation only changes the angle.

$P \approx (2.60, 1.5, 2)$. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $P' \approx (-1.5, 2.60, 2)$, and that is $(3,\,120^\circ,\,2)$: $r$ and $z$ stay the same, $\varphi$ increases by $90^\circ$.

A drone takes off at $(0, 0, 0)$ (in m) and flies $\begin{pmatrix} 30 \\ 40 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 20 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} -10 \\ 0 \\ 10 \end{pmatrix}$ one after the other. Where is it, and how far is it from the start? How long was its path?

$(20, 40, 30)$, distance $\sqrt{2900} \approx 53.9$ m. Path: $50 + 20 + \sqrt{200} \approx 84.1$ m, longer than the distance (triangle inequality).

Show with vectors: the four space diagonals of a cuboid intersect in one point and bisect each other there.

Corner at the origin, edges $a$, $b$, $c$ on the axes: every space diagonal joins two corners whose position vectors add up to $\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{pmatrix}$. So all four midpoints are $\left(\tfrac{a}{2}, \tfrac{b}{2}, \tfrac{c}{2}\right)$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Where longitude is zero

For latitude, the Earth itself provides the zero: the equator, which is equally far from both poles. For longitude there is no such thing. Every semicircle from pole to pole could be the prime meridian, and in the 19th century nautical charts and timetables counted from different observatories. Anyone comparing a chart from Paris with one from Greenwich had to convert.

In October 1884, at the invitation of US President Chester A. Arthur, delegates from more than twenty states met in Washington for the International Meridian Conference. On 13 October 22 states voted for the meridian through the observatory at Greenwich; San Domingo voted against, France and Brazil abstained. The conference also fixed a universal day beginning at midnight in Greenwich. Since then the longitude $\varphi$ in the spherical coordinates of the Earth has measured the angle to the half-plane through Greenwich.

Source: International Conference held at Washington for the purpose of fixing a Prime Meridian and a Universal Day, October 1884. Protocols of the Proceedings, Washington 1884. Accounts give the number of states represented as either 25 or 26; the resolutions were adopted on 22 October 1884.
