Linear dependence and lines

When do three arrows point in “genuinely different” directions, and when do they lie in one plane? You answer this question of linear dependence with a system of equations. With the same idea you prove theorems of geometry, for instance that the midpoints of the sides of any quadrilateral form a parallelogram, even in space. Then you describe lines by a point and a direction and decide how two lines lie relative to each other. In space there is one more case than in the plane.

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Linear dependence and independence

With $\vec{a} = (1, 2)$ and $\vec{b} = (1, -1)$ you can reach every point of the plane, for example $(5, 1) = 2\vec{a} + 3\vec{b}$. With $\vec{a}$ and $2\vec{a}$, however, only the points of a line, because the second arrow brings no new direction: the two are linearly dependent. In space you need three vectors that do not lie in one plane to reach every point. A fourth adds nothing new.

KEY POINT · LINEAR DEPENDENCE

A linear combination of $\vec{a}$, $\vec{b}$, $\vec{c}$ is a vector $r\vec{a} + s\vec{b} + t\vec{c}$ with real numbers $r$, $s$, $t$.

The vectors are called linearly independent if $r\vec{a} + s\vec{b} + t\vec{c} = \vec{o}$ holds only for $r = s = t = 0$. Otherwise they are called linearly dependent; then at least one of them can be written as a linear combination of the others.

Testing: $r\vec{a} + s\vec{b} + t\vec{c} = \vec{o}$ is a homogeneous system of equations. If Gaussian elimination leads to the identity matrix, the vectors are independent.

Geometrically: two vectors dependent ⟺ parallel (collinear); three vectors dependent ⟺ they lie in one plane (coplanar). Four vectors in space are always dependent.

Three independent vectors form a basis of space: every vector can be written as their linear combination in exactly one way.

Quick test for two vectors: Is every coordinate of one the same multiple of the corresponding coordinate of the other? $(2, 4, -6) = 2 \cdot (1, 2, -3)$: parallel.

Three points on a line? Check whether $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{AC}$ are collinear.

Why unique? If there were two representations, their difference would be a linear combination with numbers not all zero that gives $\vec{o}$. That rules out independence.

EXAMPLE 1 · THREE VECTORS WITH GAUSS

Are $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ linearly independent?

$r + t = 0$, $r + s = 0$, $s + t = 0$. From the first two $t = -r$ and $s = -r$, then $-2r = 0$.

Only $r = s = t = 0$: linearly independent, a basis of space.

EXAMPLE 2 · WITH A PARAMETER

For which $t$ are $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ t \end{pmatrix}$ linearly dependent?

The first two are not parallel. Dependent if the third is their linear combination: $r = 1$ from the first row, $s = 1$ from the second, then $t = r + s = 2$.

$t = 2$; for all other $t$ the three are independent.

Do $A(1, 2, 3)$, $B(2, 4, 5)$ and $C(4, 8, 9)$ lie on a line? For which $r$ is $(3, r, 6)$ parallel to $(1, 2, 2)$?

$\overrightarrow{AB} = (1, 2, 2)$, $\overrightarrow{AC} = (3, 6, 6) = 3\overrightarrow{AB}$: yes. $r = 6$.

Write $(4, 5, 6)$ as a linear combination of the basis from Example 1.

$r + t = 4$, $r + s = 5$, $s + t = 6$; adding: $r + s + t = 7.5$. So $s = 3.5$, $t = 2.5$, $r = 1.5$.

Are $(1, 2, 0)$, $(0, 1, 3)$ and $(2, 5, 3)$ linearly dependent? If so, give a linear combination that gives $\vec{o}$ without all the numbers being zero.

Yes: $2 \cdot (1, 2, 0) + (0, 1, 3) = (2, 5, 3)$, so $2\vec{a} + \vec{b} - \vec{c} = \vec{o}$. No two of the vectors are parallel, yet all three lie in one plane.

Justify: four vectors in space are always linearly dependent. Hint: write $r\vec{a} + s\vec{b} + t\vec{c} + u\vec{d} = \vec{o}$ as a system of equations.

There are three equations for four unknowns. Gaussian elimination leaves at least one unknown free; choosing it non-zero gives a solution in which not all the numbers are zero.

UNIT 22 LESSONS

2Division ratios and proofs with vectors

The midpoints of the sides of a quadrilateral $ABCD$ always form a parallelogram. With vectors this is quickly shown: from the midpoint of $AB$ to the midpoint of $BC$ leads $\tfrac{1}{2}(\vec{b} + \vec{c}) - \tfrac{1}{2}(\vec{a} + \vec{b})$, i.e. $\tfrac{1}{2}(\vec{c} - \vec{a})$, and exactly the same vector leads from the midpoint of $DA$ to the midpoint of $CD$. Nowhere did we use that the four corners lie in one plane. So the theorem also holds for quadrilaterals in space.

KEY POINT · POINTS OF DIVISION AND PROOFS

Point of division: $T$ divides $AB$ in the ratio $m : n$ if $\vec{t} = \vec{a} + \tfrac{m}{m + n}\overrightarrow{AB}$. Midpoint: $\vec{m} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\vec{a} + \vec{b})$.

Centroid of a triangle: $\vec{s} = \tfrac{1}{3}(\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c})$. It divides each median in the ratio $2 : 1$, measured from the vertex.

Closed vector chain: Express the same vector in two ways in terms of two linearly independent vectors $\vec{a}$, $\vec{b}$. Because the representation is unique, the coefficients agree (comparing coefficients). This gives a system of equations for the unknown ratios.

Varignon’s theorem: Named after Pierre Varignon (1654–1722); his proof appeared in 1731 in the “Élémens de mathématique”, after his death (source: MacTutor History of Mathematics, St Andrews).

Proving instead of measuring: A picture shows one case; a vector proof holds for all quadrilaterals at once.

EXAMPLE 3 · POINT OF DIVISION AND CENTROID

$A(1, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 3, 0)$, $C(2, 0, 6)$. Find the centroid and the point $T$ that divides $AB$ in the ratio $1 : 2$, starting from $A$.

$\vec{s} = \tfrac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$, so $S(1, 1, 2)$.

$\vec{t} = \vec{a} + \tfrac{1}{3}\overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \tfrac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $T\!\left(\tfrac{2}{3}, 1, 0\right)$

EXAMPLE 4 · THE CENTROID DIVIDES 2 : 1

Prove with a closed vector chain: the medians from $A$ and from $B$ intersect in a point $S$ that divides both in the ratio $2 : 1$.

With $\vec{a} = \overrightarrow{AB}$, $\vec{b} = \overrightarrow{AC}$: $\overrightarrow{AS} = r \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}(\vec{a} + \vec{b})$ and also $\overrightarrow{AS} = \vec{a} + s\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\vec{b} - \vec{a}\right)$.

Comparing at $\vec{a}$: $\tfrac{r}{2} = 1 - s$; at $\vec{b}$: $\tfrac{r}{2} = \tfrac{s}{2}$. So $r = s$ and $\tfrac{3r}{2} = 1$: $r = s = \tfrac{2}{3}$.

EXERCISES

$M(1, 1, 1)$ is the midpoint of $A(3, 0, -1)$ and $B$. Find $B$. Which point divides $AB$ in the ratio $3 : 1$, starting from $A$?

$\vec{b} = 2\vec{m} - \vec{a} = (-1, 2, 3)$. $\vec{t} = \vec{a} + \tfrac{3}{4}\overrightarrow{AB} = (3, 0, -1) + \tfrac{3}{4}(-4, 2, 4) = (0, 1.5, 2)$.

The corners of a quadrilateral in space are $A(0, 0, 0)$, $B(4, 0, 0)$, $C(4, 4, 2)$, $D(0, 4, -2)$. Find the four midpoints of the sides and show that they form a parallelogram.

$(2, 0, 0)$, $(4, 2, 1)$, $(2, 4, 0)$, $(0, 2, -1)$. From the first to the second and from the fourth to the third it is $(2, 2, 1)$ each time. Both sides have length 3, and so do the others: it is even a rhombus, because the diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ are equally long.

Prove with vectors: the line segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half as long.

Midpoints of $AC$ and $BC$: $\tfrac{1}{2}(\vec{b} + \vec{c}) - \tfrac{1}{2}(\vec{a} + \vec{c}) = \tfrac{1}{2}(\vec{b} - \vec{a}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\overrightarrow{AB}$.

Prove with a closed vector chain: the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other.

Set $\vec{a} = \overrightarrow{AB}$, $\vec{b} = \overrightarrow{AD}$ and go from $A$ to the point of intersection $S$ in two ways.

$\overrightarrow{AS} = r(\vec{a} + \vec{b})$ and $\overrightarrow{AS} = \vec{a} + s(\vec{b} - \vec{a})$. Comparing: $r = 1 - s$ and $r = s$, so $r = s = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

In triangle $ABC$, $P$ divides the side $BC$ in the ratio $1 : 1$ and $Q$ divides the side $AC$ in the ratio $2 : 1$ (starting from $A$). In what ratio do $AP$ and $BQ$ divide each other?

$\overrightarrow{AX} = r \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) = \vec{a} + s\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\vec{b} - \vec{a}\right)$. Comparing: $\tfrac{r}{2} = 1 - s$, $\tfrac{r}{2} = \tfrac{2s}{3}$. Hence $s = \tfrac{3}{5}$, $r = \tfrac{4}{5}$: $AP$ is divided in the ratio $4 : 1$, $BQ$ in the ratio $3 : 2$.

UNIT 32 LESSONS

3Lines in parametric form

At time $t = 0$ an aeroplane is at $(0, 0, 1)$ (in km) and covers $(6, 8, 0.5)$ every minute. After $t$ minutes it is at the point with the position vector $\vec{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + t \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix}$, after 2 minutes at $(12, 16, 2)$, after 6 minutes at a height of 4 km. If $t$ runs through all real numbers, you get all the points of a line.

KEY POINT · LINES

Parametric form: $g\colon \vec{x} = \vec{p} + t \cdot \vec{u}$ with the position vector $\vec{p}$ (of a point on the line) and the direction vector $\vec{u} \neq \vec{o}$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$. Through $A$ and $B$: $\vec{x} = \vec{a} + t \cdot \overrightarrow{AB}$; the line segment $AB$ corresponds to $0 \leq t \leq 1$.

Point test: If all three coordinate equations give the same $t$, the point lies on the line. Trace points: points of intersection with the coordinate planes, e.g. with the $xy$ plane via $z = 0$.

Parameter-free form in the plane: eliminate $t$ from the two coordinate equations; what remains is $ax + by = c$. In space such an equation with $z$ describes a plane; a line there needs two equations (chapter 3.3).

Many equations, one line: any point of the line will do as the base point, and any non-zero multiple of the direction vector as the direction.

Motion: If $t$ is time, then $|\vec{u}|$ is the speed. In the climb $\sqrt{36 + 64 + 0.25} \approx 10.01$ km per minute, i.e. about 601 km/h.

EXAMPLE 6 · PARAMETER-FREE IN THE PLANE

Give $g\colon \vec{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + t \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ in the plane in parameter-free form.

UNIT 43 LESSONS

4Relative positions of two lines

Two pens on the table intersect, are parallel or lie on top of each other. If you hold them in the air, there is a fourth case: they are not parallel and still do not touch, like an edge of the base and a diagonally opposite edge of the top face of a cube. Such lines are called skew. In terms of linear dependence: two lines lie in a common plane exactly when their direction vectors and the vector between the base points are coplanar.

KEY POINT · POSITION OF TWO LINES

1. Direction vectors collinear? Yes: point test with the base point of one in the other. If it lies on it: identical, otherwise parallel and distinct.

2. Not collinear: set the equations of the lines equal. That gives three equations for two parameters: find $t$ and $s$ with two of them, check with the third.

If the third fits: intersecting; find the point of intersection by substituting. If it does not fit: skew.

Collision? In motion the parameter is time. Two aeroplanes only collide if they are at the point of intersection at the same time, i.e. at $t = s$.

Outlook: The widget already shows how far apart skew lines are. You will calculate distances in Year 12.

Prove: if in a tetrahedron $ABCD$ you join the midpoints of two opposite edges ($AB$ and $CD$), and likewise for $AC$ and $BD$ and for $AD$ and $BC$, the three connecting segments intersect in one point and bisect each other there.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

The teacher who invented dimension

Hermann Günther Grassmann (1809–1877) was a grammar school teacher in Stettin for almost his entire working life. In 1844 he published “Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre” (“The theory of linear extension”). In it he calculates with directed line segments in any number of dimensions and uses concepts that are now called linear independence, basis and dimension: a space has dimension $n$ if there are $n$ independent vectors from which every other one can be built. The number three plays no special role.

Experts found the book too abstract, and Grassmann never obtained a post at a university. He turned to linguistics; his dictionary of the Rigveda is still regarded as useful today. Only in 1888 did Giuseppe Peano, explicitly building on Grassmann, formulate the rules for calculating with vectors as axioms. The laws of arithmetic from chapter 3.1 are exactly these rules.

Sources: H. Grassmann, “Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre”, Leipzig 1844. G. Peano, “Calcolo geometrico secondo l'Ausdehnungslehre di H. Grassmann”, Turin 1888. Biography: MacTutor History of Mathematics, St Andrews. Sources differ on when his teaching in Stettin began and how it went.
