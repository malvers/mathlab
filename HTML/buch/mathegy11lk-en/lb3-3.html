Planes and relative positions

Roof surfaces, glass façades, ramps, a coal seam deep underground: planes are everywhere. You describe them with a base point and two directions (parametric form) or with a single equation $ax + by + cz = d$ (coordinate form), which you obtain by eliminating the parameters. Then you clarify all the positions: where does a line pass through a plane, and in which line of intersection do two planes meet, such as the two surfaces of a roof at the ridge?

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Planes in parametric form

Three stool legs that do not stand on one line fix exactly one plane. From the first foot $A$ you reach every point of the floor by going some way in the direction $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and some way in the direction $\overrightarrow{AC}$: $\vec{x} = \vec{a} + r \cdot \overrightarrow{AB} + s \cdot \overrightarrow{AC}$. Two parameters, because a plane has two independent directions.

KEY POINT · PARAMETRIC FORM OF A PLANE

$E\colon \vec{x} = \vec{p} + r \cdot \vec{u} + s \cdot \vec{v}$ with position vector $\vec{p}$ and two linearly independent direction vectors $\vec{u}$, $\vec{v}$; $r, s \in \mathbb{R}$.

A plane is fixed by three points that do not lie on one line, by a line and a point outside it, by two intersecting lines or by two distinct parallel lines.

Point test: $\vec{p} + r\vec{u} + s\vec{v} = \vec{q}$ is a system of three equations for $r$ and $s$. Find them with two and check with the third.

Skew does not work: Two skew lines never lie in a common plane.

Flat quadrilateral? Plane through three corners, point test with the fourth. This is how you check whether a glass roof can consist of a single pane.

With linear dependence: $Q$ lies in $E$ exactly when $\vec{u}$, $\vec{v}$ and $\overrightarrow{PQ}$ are coplanar.

EXAMPLE 1 · FROM THREE POINTS

Set up the plane through $A(1, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 1, 0)$, $C(0, 0, 1)$. Does $Q(1, 1, 1)$ lie in it?

$E\colon \vec{x} = (1, 0, 0) + r(-1, 1, 0) + s(-1, 0, 1)$.

$Q$: $r = 1$, $s = 1$ from $y$ and $z$, then $x = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1 \neq 1$: $Q$ does not lie in $E$.

EXAMPLE 2 · A FLAT ROOF?

Do $A(0, 0, 3)$, $B(4, 0, 3)$, $C(4, 2, 5)$ and $D(0, 2, 5)$ lie in one plane?

$\vec{x} = (0, 0, 3) + r(4, 0, 0) + s(4, 2, 2)$; for $D$: $2s = 2$, so $s = 1$; $3 + 2 = 5$ fits; $4r + 4 = 0$, so $r = -1$.

Yes, $ABCD$ is flat, it is even a rectangle.

UNIT 23 LESSONS

2Planes in parameter-free form

For the plane through $(2, 0, 0)$, $(0, 2, 0)$, $(0, 0, 2)$, all the points checked satisfy $x + y + z = 2$. As a calculation: $\vec{x} = (0, 0, 2) + r(1, 0, -1) + s(0, 1, -1)$ means $x = r$, $y = s$, $z = 2 - r - s$. If you substitute the first two into the third, the parameters disappear: $z = 2 - x - y$, so $x + y + z = 2$. A single equation, with no parameters at all.

KEY POINT · COORDINATE FORM

Every plane can be written as $E\colon ax + by + cz = d$ (not all of $a$, $b$, $c$ zero). A point lies in $E$ if its coordinates satisfy the equation.

Eliminating the parameters: write out the parametric form coordinate by coordinate, find $r$ and $s$ from two equations and substitute them into the third. Equivalently: multiply the three rows by numbers and add them so that $r$ and $s$ drop out (Gaussian elimination).

Back to the parametric form: solve for one variable and choose the other two freely, $y = r$, $z = s$.

Intercepts (trace points): set two coordinates to zero. Intercept form: $\dfrac{x}{p} + \dfrac{y}{q} + \dfrac{z}{r} = 1$ with the trace points $(p, 0, 0)$, $(0, q, 0)$, $(0, 0, r)$.

Special positions: if a variable is missing, the plane is parallel to that axis ($3x + 2y = 6$ is parallel to the $z$-axis); $d = 0$: through the origin; $y = 4$: parallel to the $xz$ plane.

Many equations, one plane: $2x - y + z = 3$ and $4x - 2y + 2z = 6$ describe the same plane. $4x - 2y + 2z = 10$ is parallel to it.

Check: The base point must satisfy the equation, and the direction vectors must not change the left-hand side: for $\vec{u} = (u_1, u_2, u_3)$, $au_1 + bu_2 + cu_3 = 0$.

Outlook: The numbers $a$, $b$, $c$ form a vector that is perpendicular to the plane. You will work with this normal vector in Year 12.

EXAMPLE 3 · ELIMINATING

Find the coordinate form of $E\colon \vec{x} = (1, 0, 2) + r(1, 1, 0) + s(0, 1, -1)$.

$x = 1 + r$, $y = r + s$, $z = 2 - s$. From the first $r = x - 1$, from the third $s = 2 - z$, into the second: $y = (x - 1) + (2 - z)$.

$x - y - z = -1$. Check with $(1, 0, 2)$: $1 - 0 - 2 = -1$; direction vectors: $1 - 1 - 0 = 0$ and $0 - 1 + 1 = 0$.

EXAMPLE 4 · INTERCEPTS

Find the trace points of $E\colon 2x + 3y + 6z = 12$ and a parametric form.

$y = z = 0$: $x = 6$; likewise $y = 4$ and $z = 2$. Intercept form $\tfrac{x}{6} + \tfrac{y}{4} + \tfrac{z}{2} = 1$.

With the trace points: $\vec{x} = (6, 0, 0) + r(-6, 4, 0) + s(-6, 0, 2)$

UNIT 33 LESSONS

3Position of a line and a plane

The ray of sunlight through the top of the flagpole is the line $\vec{x} = (0, 0, 6) + t(2, 1, -3)$. On the ground $z = 0$: $6 - 3t = 0$, so $t = 2$, shadow point $(4, 2, 0)$. If there is a ramp in the plane $x - 2z = 0$ in this direction, you substitute the points of the line into its equation: $2t - 2(6 - 3t) = 0$, so $t = 1.5$. That comes before the ground: the shadow falls on the ramp, at the point $(3, 1.5, 1.5)$.

KEY POINT · LINE AND PLANE

A line can intersect a plane (exactly one point of intersection), be parallel to it (no common point) or lie in it.

With the coordinate form: substitute the coordinates of the line into $ax + by + cz = d$. One solution $t$: point of intersection. A contradiction such as $0 = 5$: parallel. Always true, such as $2 = 2$: the line lies in the plane.

With the parametric form: set equal; three equations for $t$, $r$, $s$, solved with Gaussian elimination.

Tip: If the plane is already in coordinate form, substituting is almost always quicker than setting equal.

Parallel or intersecting? After substituting, the factor in front of $t$ is $aw_1 + bw_2 + cw_3$ for the direction vector $\vec{w}$. If it is zero, the line is parallel to the plane or lies in it.

EXAMPLE 6 · PARALLEL OR INSIDE?

$g\colon \vec{x} = (0, 1, 2) + t(1, 0, -1)$ with $E\colon x + z = 2$ and with $F\colon x + z = 5$.

UNIT 43 LESSONS

4Position of two planes, line of intersection

The two surfaces of a gable roof lie in $x + z = 4$ and $-x + z = 4$. A point lies on both surfaces if both equations hold. Adding them gives $2z = 8$; subtracting them, $2x = 0$. So $x = 0$ and $z = 4$, while $y$ remains arbitrary: this is the ridge, the line $\vec{x} = (0, 0, 4) + t(0, 1, 0)$. Two equations in three unknowns leave one unknown free, and it becomes the parameter of the line of intersection.

KEY POINT · TWO PLANES

Two planes are parallel and distinct, identical or intersect in a line. There is never a single common point.

In coordinate form: if the left-hand sides are multiples of each other, the right-hand side decides: in the same ratio identical, otherwise parallel. If not, the planes intersect.

Line of intersection: set one variable equal to $t$ and calculate the other two from the two equations (Gaussian elimination). If one plane is given in parametric form, substitute it into the coordinate form of the other, solve for one parameter and substitute back.

Line without parameters: a line in space is the intersection of two planes, so it is given by two equations, for example $x + z = 4$ and $-x + z = 4$ for the ridge.

Trace lines: the intersection of a plane with a coordinate plane. For $x + 2y + 2z = 4$ and $z = 0$: $x + 2y = 4$ in the $xy$ plane.

Three planes can have exactly one point in common, like $x = 1$, $y = 2$, $z = 3$: a uniquely solvable system of equations in three unknowns (chapter 2).

Find the line of intersection of $E\colon x + y + z = 6$ and $F\colon x - y = 0$.

$g\colon \vec{x} = (0, 0, 6) + t(1, 1, -2)$. With $z = t$ as the free variable you get a different equation of the same line.

EXAMPLE 8 · PARAMETRIC FORM MEETS COORDINATE FORM

Substituting into $F$: $r + s + r = 2$, so $s = 2 - 2r$. Back into $E$: $\vec{x} = r(1, 0, 1) + (2 - 2r)(0, 1, 0)$.

EXCURSIONGEOLOGY

How geologists survey a layer

A coal seam lies deep underground and cannot be seen. Three boreholes hit its lower boundary: at $A$ 100 m below sea level, at $B$, 400 m further east, 140 m below, and at $C$, 300 m north of $A$, only 70 m below. With $x$ to the east, $y$ to the north and $z$ as the height, these are $A(0, 0, -100)$, $B(400, 0, -140)$, $C(0, 300, -70)$. If the seam is flat, it lies in the plane through these three points, and eliminating gives $x - y + 10z = -1000$.

Geologists ask about the strike, the direction of a horizontal line in the layer. That is a line of intersection: with the horizontal plane $z = -100$ what remains is $x - y = 0$, the direction $(1, 1, 0)$, i.e. towards the north-east. At right angles to it the layer dips towards the south-east, by about 14 m per 100 m. This three-point problem is part of every geologist’s toolkit.

Sources: “Three-point problem”: West Virginia Geological and Economic Survey, Geology Handbook, chapter 5; SERC, Carleton College, “The Three-Point Problem, Vector Analysis and Extension to the N-Point Problem”. The numbers in the example are made up.
