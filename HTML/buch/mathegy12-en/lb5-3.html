Areas

The integral of $x^2 - 1$ over $[-1, 2]$ is $0$, and yet the graph clearly encloses areas with the axis. You learn to tell the integral and the area apart, to split areas at the zeros and to calculate areas between two graphs, from the cross-section of a canal to a gilded logo.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Areas between a graph and the $x$-axis

$\int_{-1}^{2} (x^2 - 1)\,\mathrm{d}x = \left[\tfrac13 x^3 - x\right]_{-1}^{2} = \tfrac23 - \tfrac23 = 0$. Separately: $\int_{-1}^{1} = -\tfrac43$ below the axis and $\int_{1}^{2} = \tfrac43$ above it. The integral offsets the two pieces against each other, the area adds them: $\tfrac43 + \tfrac43 = \tfrac83$.

An area is never negative. To calculate it, you first need to know where the graph crosses the axis: between two zeros $f$ keeps its sign, and there the integral gives exactly the area, apart from the sign. If an area is given and a limit is wanted, the fundamental theorem turns this into an equation for the limit.

KEY POINT · AREA BETWEEN A GRAPH AND THE X-AXIS

1. Find the zeros of $f$ in the interval $[a, b]$: $x_1 < x_2 < \ldots$

2. Integrate separately over each subinterval.

3. Add the absolute values: $A = \left|\int_a^{x_1} f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right| + \left|\int_{x_1}^{x_2} f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right| + \ldots$

Sketch first: a rough graph shows at once which pieces lie above and which below the axis.

Symmetry helps: if the graph is symmetric about the $y$-axis, you calculate one half and double it. On the CAS you can also enter $\int |f(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x$ straight away.

EXAMPLE 1 · THREE ZEROS

Calculate the area that $f(x) = x^3 - 4x$ encloses with the $x$-axis.

$x(x^2 - 4) = 0$: zeros $-2$, $0$, $2$. $F(x) = \tfrac14 x^4 - 2x^2$.

$\int_{-2}^{0} f = 0 - (4 - 8) = 4$, $\int_0^2 f = (4 - 8) - 0 = -4$: $A = 4 + 4 = 8$, while the integral over $[-2, 2]$ would be $0$.

EXAMPLE 2 · A ZERO IN THE INTERVAL

Calculate the area between $f(x) = 4x - x^2$ and the $x$-axis over $[0, 5]$.

Zeros $0$ and $4$, so split at $4$. $F(x) = 2x^2 - \tfrac13 x^3$: $\int_0^4 f = \tfrac{32}{3}$, $\int_4^5 f = \tfrac{25}{3} - \tfrac{32}{3} = -\tfrac73$.

$A = \tfrac{32}{3} + \tfrac73 = 13$, while the integral over $[0, 5]$ is only $\tfrac{25}{3}$.

EXERCISES

Calculate the area under $f(x) = x^2 + 1$ over $[0, 3]$.

$f > 0$: $\left[\tfrac13 x^3 + x\right]_0^3 = 9 + 3 = 12$

Calculate the area between $f(x) = 3x - x^2$ and the $x$-axis.

Zeros $0$ and $3$: $\left[1.5x^2 - \tfrac13 x^3\right]_0^3 = 13.5 - 9 = 4.5$

For $f(x) = x^3 - x$ on $[-1, 1]$, calculate the integral and the area between the graph and the axis.

Integral $0$ (point symmetry). Split at $0$: $\int_0^1 (x^3 - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = -\tfrac14$, on the left $+\tfrac14$: area $\tfrac12$.

Calculate $\int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\,\mathrm{d}x$ and the area that the sine curve encloses with the axis over $[0, 2\pi]$.

Integral $[-\cos x]_0^{2\pi} = -1 + 1 = 0$. Area: twice $\int_0^\pi \sin x\,\mathrm{d}x = 2$, so $4$.

The vertical line $x = k$ is to halve the area under $f(x) = x^2$ over $[0, 3]$. Find $k$ and explain why $k$ is greater than $1.5$.

Total area $9$, so $\tfrac13 k^3 = 4.5$: $k = \sqrt[3]{13.5} \approx 2.38$. The curve is much higher on the right, so a narrow strip on the right carries as much as a wide one on the left.

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Areas between two graphs

The lens between $y = x$ and $y = x^2$: under the line the area is $\tfrac12$, under the parabola $\tfrac13$. The lens is the difference, $\tfrac12 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac16$. Shorter: $\int_0^1 (x - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac16$. You integrate the difference “top minus bottom” straight away.

It does not matter where the graphs lie: if you shift both up or down by the same amount, the difference does not change. The limits are the points of intersection, that is the zeros of the difference function $d = f - g$. If the graphs intersect several times, “top” and “bottom” swap: then you calculate piece by piece, as with the area with the axis.

KEY POINT · AREA BETWEEN TWO GRAPHS

1. Find the points of intersection from $f(x) = g(x)$: $x_1 < x_2 < \ldots$

2. Integrate the difference function $d(x) = f(x) - g(x)$ between neighbouring points of intersection.

3. Add the absolute values: $A = \left|\int_{x_1}^{x_2} \big(f(x) - g(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\right| + \left|\int_{x_2}^{x_3} \big(f(x) - g(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\right| + \ldots$

The axis does not matter: even if both graphs run wholly or partly below the $x$-axis, “top minus bottom” still holds.

Applied problems: cross-sections of canals, dykes or profiles are areas between graphs; times the length gives the volume.

EXAMPLE 3 · PARABOLA AND LINE

Calculate the area between $f(x) = -x^2 + 2x + 3$ and $g(x) = x + 1$.

$f(x) - g(x) = -x^2 + x + 2 = 0$: points of intersection at $-1$ and $2$.

$\int_{-1}^{2} (-x^2 + x + 2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \left[-\tfrac13 x^3 + \tfrac12 x^2 + 2x\right]_{-1}^{2} = \tfrac{10}{3} + \tfrac76$: $A = 4.5$

EXAMPLE 4 · THREE POINTS OF INTERSECTION

Calculate the area between $f(x) = x^3$ and $g(x) = x$.

$x^3 - x = x(x^2 - 1) = 0$: $-1$, $0$, $1$. $\int_{-1}^{0} (x^3 - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac14$, $\int_0^1 (x^3 - x)\,\mathrm{d}x = -\tfrac14$.

$A = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$. In one go over $[-1, 1]$ you would wrongly get $0$.

EXERCISES

Calculate the area between $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = 2x$.

Points of intersection at $0$ and $2$: $\int_0^2 (2x - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = 4 - \tfrac83 = \tfrac43$

Calculate the area between $f(x) = 4 - x^2$ and $g(x) = x^2 - 4$.

Points of intersection at $\pm 2$: $\int_{-2}^{2} (8 - 2x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \left[8x - \tfrac23 x^3\right]_{-2}^{2} = \tfrac{64}{3} \approx 21.33$

Calculate the area between $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = x^3$. Which graph is on top between the points of intersection?

Points of intersection at $0$ and $1$; in between $x^2 > x^3$: $\int_0^1 (x^2 - x^3)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac13 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac{1}{12}$

A canal has the cross-section between the water surface $y = 0$ and the bottom $y = 0.25x^2 - 4$ (in m). How large is the cross-section? How much water is in the canal over a length of $1$ km?

Points of intersection at $\pm 4$: $\int_{-4}^{4} (4 - 0.25x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \left[4x - \tfrac{1}{12} x^3\right]_{-4}^{4} = \tfrac{64}{3} \approx 21.3$ m². Over $1000$ m: about $21{,}300$ m³.

For which $a > 0$ do $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = ax$ enclose an area of $36$?

The points of intersection depend on $a$.

Points of intersection at $0$ and $a$: $\int_0^a (ax - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{a^3}{2} - \tfrac{a^3}{3} = \tfrac{a^3}{6} = 36$, so $a^3 = 216$: $a = 6$.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

Calculate the area between $f(x) = x^2 - 2x$ and the $x$-axis.

Zeros $0$ and $2$: $\left[\tfrac13 x^3 - x^2\right]_0^2 = \tfrac83 - 4 = -\tfrac43$, so $A = \tfrac43$.

Connecting with chapter 5.2: explain why the area is never smaller than the absolute value of the integral. When are they equal? Check with $f(x) = x^3 - 2x$ on $[0, 2]$.

In the integral, pieces with different signs partly cancel out; in the area they do not. Both are equal if $f$ does not change sign in the interval. Example: zero at $\sqrt2$, partial integrals $-1$ and $1$; integral $0$, area $2$.

The tangent to $f(x) = x^2$ at the point $(2, 4)$, the graph of $f$ and the $x$-axis bound an area. Calculate it.

Tangent $y = 4x - 4$, zero at $x = 1$. Area under the parabola over $[0, 2]$ minus the triangle under the tangent over $[1, 2]$: $\tfrac83 - 2 = \tfrac23$.

A logo consists of the area between $y = 4 - x^2$ and $y = 0.5x^2 - 2$ (in cm). It is covered with gold leaf costing €0.80 per cm². How much does the gold cost?

$4 - x^2 = 0.5x^2 - 2$ gives $x = \pm 2$. $\int_{-2}^{2} (6 - 1.5x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \left[6x - 0.5x^3\right]_{-2}^{2} = 16$ cm²: €12.80.

EXCURSIONHISTORY AND A PARADOX

Infinitely long and yet finitely large

How large is the area under $y = \tfrac{1}{x^2}$ to the right of $x = 1$, that is all the way to infinity? Up to the limit $b$ we have $\int_1^b \tfrac{1}{x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x = \left[-\tfrac1x\right]_1^b = 1 - \tfrac1b$. However far you move $b$, the area stays below $1$ and approaches $1$. So an infinitely long region has the finite area $1$.

With $y = \tfrac1x$ it is different: $\int_1^b \tfrac1x\,\mathrm{d}x = \ln b$ grows without bound, if very slowly. For the area $10$ you already need $b = \mathrm{e}^{10} \approx 22{,}026$. With calculations like these the Italian Evangelista Torricelli (1608–1647) startled his contemporaries: in 1644 he showed in his Opera geometrica that an infinitely long solid, formed by rotating the region under a hyperbola, has a finite volume. This completely contradicted the intuition of mathematicians and caused a great sensation.

Source: MacTutor History of Mathematics, University of St Andrews: biography “Evangelista Torricelli”.
