Scalar product, vector product and normal form

Two ways of multiplying vectors, and both have a clear meaning: the scalar product measures how much two directions agree, and so it gives you every angle. The vector product builds a vector that is perpendicular to both, and its length is an area. With this normal vector you describe planes briefly and elegantly in normal and coordinate form.

1Scalar product and vector product

If you pull a sledge with the force $\vec F$ along the path $\vec s$, only the part of the force in the direction of the path does work: $W = |\vec F| \cdot |\vec s| \cdot \cos\varphi$. Surprisingly, the simple calculation $F_1 s_1 + F_2 s_2 + F_3 s_3$ gives the same. This sum is called the scalar product, and its result is a number.

Because $\vec a \circ \vec b = |\vec a| \cdot |\vec b| \cdot \cos\varphi$, the scalar product gives you the angle between two vectors. If it is $0$, they are perpendicular. Dividing by $|\vec a|$ gives the length of the projection of $\vec b$ onto the direction of $\vec a$.

The vector product $\vec a \times \vec b$, on the other hand, is a vector. It is perpendicular to $\vec a$ and to $\vec b$, and its length is the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\vec a$ and $\vec b$. Swapping the factors reverses its direction. Parallel vectors have the vector product $\vec 0$.

KEY POINT · SCALAR PRODUCT AND VECTOR PRODUCT

Scalar product: $\vec a \circ \vec b = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3 = |\vec a| \cdot |\vec b| \cdot \cos\varphi$, so $\cos\varphi = \dfrac{\vec a \circ \vec b}{|\vec a| \cdot |\vec b|}$

Vector product: $\vec a \times \vec b = \begin{pmatrix} a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2 \\ a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3 \\ a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \end{pmatrix}$ is perpendicular to $\vec a$ and $\vec b$

$|\vec a \times \vec b| = |\vec a| \cdot |\vec b| \cdot \sin\varphi$ = area of the parallelogram; the triangle has half of it.

Notation: In this book $\vec a \circ \vec b$ stands for the scalar product. Many books and the online training write $\vec a \cdot \vec b$; it means the same.

Memory aid for the vector product: The first row uses components 2 and 3, the second 3 and 1, the third 1 and 2, always “crosswise”. Check: the result times $\vec a$ must give $0$.

EXAMPLE 1 · ANGLE AND PROJECTION

Calculate the angle between $\vec a = (1, 2, 2)$ and $\vec b = (2, -1, 2)$ and the length of the projection of $\vec b$ onto $\vec a$.

$\vec a \circ \vec b = 2 - 2 + 4 = 4$, $|\vec a| = |\vec b| = 3$: $\cos\varphi = \tfrac49$, $\varphi \approx 63.6^\circ$.

Projection: $\dfrac{\vec a \circ \vec b}{|\vec a|} = \tfrac43$

EXAMPLE 2 · TRIANGLE IN SPACE

Calculate the area of the triangle $A(1, 0, 0)$, $B(0, 2, 0)$, $C(0, 0, 3)$.

$\vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} = (-1, 2, 0) \times (-1, 0, 3) = (6, 3, 2)$, length $\sqrt{36 + 9 + 4} = 7$.

$A = \tfrac72 = 3.5$. Check: $(6, 3, 2) \circ (-1, 2, 0) = 0$ ✔

2Orthogonality, normal vector and coordinate form

A plane is fixed by a point $P$ and a direction $\vec n$ perpendicular to it, just as a table top is fixed by a point and the table leg. A point $X$ lies in the plane exactly when the connecting vector $\vec{PX}$ is perpendicular to $\vec n$: $(\vec x - \vec p) \circ \vec n = 0$.

Multiplying out the normal form gives the coordinate form $ax + by + cz = d$. Its coefficients form a normal vector $(a, b, c)$, and the right-hand side is $d = \vec n \circ \vec p$. If you know three points, the vector product of two direction vectors gives the normal vector. The trace points are the points of intersection with the axes: set two coordinates to zero and work out the third.

KEY POINT · NORMAL AND COORDINATE FORM

Orthogonality condition: $\vec a \perp \vec b \iff \vec a \circ \vec b = 0$ (for $\vec a, \vec b \neq \vec 0$)

Normal form: $E\colon (\vec x - \vec p) \circ \vec n = 0$ · Coordinate form: $E\colon n_1 x + n_2 y + n_3 z = d$ with $d = \vec n \circ \vec p$

Normal vector from the parametric form: $\vec n = \vec u \times \vec v$ for the direction vectors $\vec u$, $\vec v$

Intercept form: If $E$ has the trace points $(a, 0, 0)$, $(0, b, 0)$, $(0, 0, c)$, then $\tfrac xa + \tfrac yb + \tfrac zc = 1$.

Relative position of planes: If the normal vectors are multiples of each other, the planes are parallel or identical. If their scalar product is $0$, the planes are perpendicular.

All sides have length $2$: yes. $\cos\alpha = \tfrac{\vec{AB} \circ \vec{AC}}{4} = \tfrac24$, $\alpha = 60^\circ$ ✔

Connecting with Year 11: How does $g\colon \vec x = (1, 0, 0) + t(1, 1, 0)$ lie relative to $E\colon x - y + z = 1$? Use the scalar product of the direction vector and the normal vector.

$(1, 1, 0) \circ (1, -1, 1) = 0$: $g$ is parallel to $E$ or lies in it. Point test with $(1, 0, 0)$: $1 = 1$ ✔, so $g$ lies in $E$.

What angle does the space diagonal of a cube make with an edge that starts at the same corner?

$(1, 1, 1)$ and $(1, 0, 0)$: $\cos\varphi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$, $\varphi \approx 54.7^\circ$.

Prove with the scalar product: the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular to each other.

The rhombus is spanned by $\vec a$ and $\vec b$ with $|\vec a| = |\vec b|$; its diagonals are $\vec a + \vec b$ and $\vec a - \vec b$.

$(\vec a + \vec b) \circ (\vec a - \vec b) = \vec a \circ \vec a - \vec b \circ \vec b = |\vec a|^2 - |\vec b|^2 = 0$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

A formula in stone

The Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton (1805–1865) searched for years for a way to calculate with points in space just as with complex numbers in the plane. On Monday, 16 October 1843, he was walking with his wife along the Royal Canal in Dublin to a meeting of the Royal Irish Academy. Then the solution came to him. He carved it into the stone of Broome Bridge: $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$.

Hamilton called the new numbers quaternions. If you multiply two quaternions that consist only of the parts with $i$, $j$ and $k$, the result contains both things you have learnt in this chapter: the number part is the negative scalar product, and the rest is the vector product of the two vectors. Today quaternions control rotations in computer games and in space travel.

Source: MacTutor History of Mathematics, University of St Andrews: biography “William Rowan Hamilton”.
