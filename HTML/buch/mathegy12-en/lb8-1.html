UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Minimal distances in the plane and in space

Is the vertex of the parabola $y = x^2$ nearest to the point $P(0, 2)$? It is $2$ away. But the point $(1, 1)$ on the curve is only at distance $\sqrt{1 + 1} \approx 1.41$. To find the point that really is nearest, you write the distance as a function of the position $x$ and look for its minimum.

For a point $Q(x, f(x))$ on the curve, $d(x) = \sqrt{(x - x_P)^2 + (f(x) - y_P)^2}$. Because the square root is strictly increasing, $d(x)^2$ has the same minimum point, and without the root it is much easier to differentiate. It works the same for a line in space: every point of the line belongs to a parameter $t$, and the distance becomes a function of $t$.

At the minimum the connecting segment is perpendicular to the line or to the tangent of the curve: the nearest point is the foot of the perpendicular.

KEY POINT · EXTREMAL DISTANCES

1. Set up the distance function: $d(t)^2 = |\vec{PQ_t}|^2$ for the point $Q_t$ of the line or curve.

2. Differentiate $d^2$, solve $\big(d^2\big)' = 0$, check for a minimum (at the end points too).

3. $d_{\min} = \sqrt{d(t_0)^2}$. Check: $\vec{PQ}_{t_0}$ is perpendicular to the direction vector or to the tangent.

Quadratic distance function: For lines, $d(t)^2$ is a parabola in $t$ that opens upwards. Its vertex is the minimum; you do not even need the derivative.

Moving objects: If both points depend on time, $d(t)$ is the distance at time $t$. The minimum tells you how close they come and when.

EXAMPLE 1 · POINT AND LINE IN SPACE

How far is $P(1, 2, 3)$ from the line $g\colon \vec x = t \cdot (1, 1, 1)$?

$d(t)^2 = (t - 1)^2 + (t - 2)^2 + (t - 3)^2 = 3t^2 - 12t + 14$, vertex at $t = 2$: $d^2 = 2$.

$d = \sqrt2$, foot of the perpendicular $F(2, 2, 2)$. Check: $\vec{PF} = (1, 0, -1)$, $\circ\,(1, 1, 1) = 0$ ✔

EXAMPLE 2 · POINT AND PARABOLA

Which points of the parabola $y = x^2$ are nearest to $P(0, 2)$?

$d(x)^2 = x^2 + (x^2 - 2)^2 = x^4 - 3x^2 + 4$, derivative $4x^3 - 6x = 0$: $x = 0$ or $x^2 = 1.5$.

$d(0)^2 = 4$, $d(\pm\sqrt{1.5})^2 = 1.75$: $(\pm\sqrt{1.5}, 1.5)$ with $d \approx 1.32$. The vertex is a local maximum of $d$.

Two points are moving: $A_t(t, 0, 0)$ and $B_t(0, 6 - t, 0)$. When and how close do they come to each other?

A ship starts at $(0, 0)$ and moves on by $(3, 4)$ km every hour. When is it closest to the lighthouse $L(10, 0)$, and how close?

Which point of the graph of $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ is nearest to $(2.5, 0)$? Show that the connecting segment is perpendicular to the tangent there.

2Reflecting points in planes

Your mirror image stands just as far behind the mirror as you stand in front of it, and the line connecting you and your image is perpendicular to the mirror. This is exactly how you reflect a point $P$ in a plane $E$: the foot of the perpendicular $F$ is the midpoint between $P$ and its image $P'$.

So, as in Chapter 7.3, you find the foot of the perpendicular with the perpendicular line and go the way from $P$ to $F$ twice. In terms of the parameter of the perpendicular line this means: $F$ belongs to $t_0$, $P'$ to $2t_0$. A ray of light is redirected by a mirror in such a way that the part of its direction perpendicular to the mirror changes sign. And anyone looking for the shortest path from $A$ via a plane to $B$ reflects $A$: the straight segment from $A'$ to $B$ shows the way.

KEY POINT · REFLECTING IN A PLANE

1. Intersect the perpendicular line $\vec x = \vec p + t \cdot \vec n$ with $E$: parameter $t_0$, foot of the perpendicular $F$.

2. Mirror point: $\vec{OP'} = \vec{OP} + 2\,\vec{PF} = \vec p + 2t_0 \cdot \vec n$. Check: $F$ is the midpoint of $PP'$.

In the point $Z$: $\vec{OP'} = 2\,\vec{OZ} - \vec{OP}$ · Plane of reflection for $A$ and $B$: normal vector $\vec{AB}$, through the midpoint of $AB$.

Coordinate planes: In $z = 0$ only $z$ changes sign: $(x, y, z) \to (x, y, -z)$. In $x = y$ the coordinates $x$ and $y$ are swapped.

Reflecting twice in the same plane leads back to the starting point. Points of the plane stay where they are.

INTERACTIVE · PERPENDICULAR AND MIRROR POINT

Switch on the mirror point $P'$. Change the point and the plane: $F$ always stays the midpoint between $P$ and $P'$.

EXAMPLE 3 · MIRROR POINT

Reflect $P(3, 4, 5)$ in the plane $E\colon x + y + z = 3$.

Perpendicular line $(3 + t, 4 + t, 5 + t)$: $12 + 3t = 3$, $t_0 = -3$, so $F(0, 1, 2)$.

$P' = P + 2t_0 \cdot \vec n$: $P'(-3, -2, -1)$. Check: the midpoint of $P$ and $P'$ is $(0, 1, 2) = F$ ✔

EXAMPLE 4 · SHORTEST PATH VIA A PLANE

A ray of light is to travel from $A(0, 0, 1)$ via a mirror in the plane $z = 0$ to $B(4, 0, 3)$. Where does it hit the mirror, and how long is its path?

Mirror point $A'(0, 0, -1)$. Every path via the mirror is as long as the path from $A'$ to $B$; the shortest is the straight segment.

$A'B\colon (0, 0, -1) + s(4, 0, 4)$ meets $z = 0$ at $s = \tfrac14$: $(1, 0, 0)$, path $|\vec{A'B}| = 4\sqrt2 \approx 5.66$

In which plane must you reflect $A(1, 2, 3)$ to get $B(3, 4, 1)$?

Normal vector $\vec{AB} = (2, 2, -2)$, shorter $(1, 1, -1)$, midpoint $(2, 3, 2)$: $x + y - z = 3$

A ray of light in the direction $(1, 0, 0)$ hits a mirror in the plane $x + y = 2$. In which direction does it continue?

Subtract twice the part of the direction vector $\vec v$ in the direction of the unit normal vector $\vec n_0$: $\vec v' = \vec v - 2(\vec v \circ \vec n_0)\,\vec n_0$.

$\vec n_0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1, 0)$, $\vec v \circ \vec n_0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$: $\vec v' = (1, 0, 0) - (1, 1, 0) = (0, -1, 0)$. The ray is turned through $90^\circ$.

A billiard ball lies at $A(1, 2)$ and is to hit the ball at $B(5, 3)$ off the cushion $y = 0$. Where must it hit the cushion, and how long is its path?

$A'(1, -2)$; the segment $A'B$ meets $y = 0$ at $(2.6, 0)$. Path $|\vec{A'B}| = \sqrt{16 + 25} = \sqrt{41} \approx 6.40$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Light takes the shortest path

Why does “angle of incidence equals angle of reflection” hold for a mirror? Heron of Alexandria gave an answer as early as the 1st century, one you have recalculated in Example 4: in his Catoptrics, a book about mirrors, he showed that the law of reflection follows from a single assumption. The path of light from the object via the mirror to the eye is as short as possible. The reflection trick makes this visible at once: the shortest path from $A'$ to $B$ is the straight line, and there the two angles are equal.

More than 1500 years later Pierre de Fermat generalised the idea. In a letter of 1 January 1662 he assumed that light chooses the path of least time. In this way he also explained the refraction of light when it passes from air into water. This principle of Fermat's is still one of the foundations of optics today.

Source: Wikipedia (English), article “Fermat's principle”; on Heron's lifetime and Catoptrics: MacTutor History of Mathematics, biography “Heron of Alexandria”.
