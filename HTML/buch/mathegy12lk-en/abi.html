ABITUR PROBLEM ANALYSIS · THE DRUG

After it has been taken, the concentration of an active substance in the blood is described by $c(t) = 8t \cdot \mathrm{e}^{-0.5t}$ ($t$ in hours, $c$ in mg/l). The substance is effective as long as $c(t) \ge 4$.

a) Find $c(0)$ and justify that $c(t) > 0$ for all $t > 0$.

$c(0) = 0$. For $t > 0$, $8t$ and $\mathrm{e}^{-0.5t}$ are positive, so their product is too.

b) When is the concentration highest, and how high is it then?

Product rule: $c'(t) = 8\mathrm{e}^{-0.5t}(1 - 0.5t) = 0$ for $t = 2$; change of sign from $+$ to $-$. $c(2) = 16\,\mathrm{e}^{-1} \approx 5.89$ mg/l after $2$ hours.

c) At what time does the concentration decrease fastest? Give the rate of decrease.

$c''(t) = 8\mathrm{e}^{-0.5t}(0.25t - 1) = 0$ for $t = 4$. $c'(4) = -8\mathrm{e}^{-2} \approx -1.08$: after $4$ hours the concentration falls by about $1.08$ mg/l per hour.

d) How long is the substance effective? Find the times with the CAS.

$c(t) = 4$ for $t_1 \approx 0.71$ and $t_2 \approx 4.31$. The substance is effective for about $3.6$ hours, roughly $3$ h $36$ min.

e) Show that $C(t) = -16(t + 2)\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.5t}$ is an antiderivative of $c$, and calculate the mean concentration in the first $6$ hours, $\tfrac16 \int_0^6 c(t)\,\mathrm{d}t$.

$C'(t) = -16\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.5t} + (-16)(t + 2)(-0.5)\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.5t} = 8t\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.5t}$ ✔ $\int_0^6 c = C(6) - C(0) = 32 - 128\,\mathrm{e}^{-3} \approx 25.63$, mean $\approx 4.27$ mg/l.

ABITUR PROBLEM GEOMETRY · THE SUN SAIL

A triangular sun sail is fixed to three masts at $A(0, 0, 3)$, $B(6, 0, 2)$ and $C(0, 4, 2)$ (lengths in m, the $x$-$y$ plane is the ground).

a) Find a coordinate equation of the plane $E$ in which the sail lies.

$\vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} = (6, 0, -1) \times (0, 4, -1) = (4, 6, 24)$, simplified $(2, 3, 12)$, $d = 36$: $E\colon 2x + 3y + 12z = 36$. Check with $B$: $12 + 24 = 36$ ✔

b) How much fabric is needed for the sail?

$\tfrac12 |(4, 6, 24)| = \tfrac12 \sqrt{628} \approx 12.5$ m²

c) At what angle is the sail inclined to the ground?

$\cos\varphi = \tfrac{12}{\sqrt{157}} \approx 0.958$: $\varphi \approx 16.7^\circ$

d) A lamp hangs below the sail at $L(2, 1, 1)$. How far is it from the sail?

$\dfrac{|4 + 3 + 12 - 36|}{\sqrt{157}} = \dfrac{17}{\sqrt{157}} \approx 1.36$ m. The negative sign of $-17$ confirms that the lamp is on the side of the ground, i.e. below the sail.

e) An additional vertical pole is to stand at the point $(3, 1, 0)$ and support the sail. How long must it be? Justify that it meets the sail inside the triangle.

$6 + 3 + 12z = 36$: $z = 2.25$, so $2.25$ m. With $(3, 1, 2.25) = \vec a + 0.5\,\vec{AB} + 0.25\,\vec{AC}$ both factors are positive and their sum is $0.75 \le 1$: the point lies inside the triangle.

ABITUR PROBLEM PROBABILITY · THE BOTTLING PLANT

A plant fills juice into bottles. Experience shows that $4\,\%$ of the bottles are sealed defectively, independently of one another.

a) What is the probability that none of $20$ bottles is defective, and that at least two are?

$0.96^{20} \approx 0.442$. $P(X \ge 2) = 1 - 0.96^{20} - 20 \cdot 0.04 \cdot 0.96^{19} \approx 1 - 0.442 - 0.368 = 0.190$

b) How many defective bottles do you expect among $200$? What is the standard deviation?

$E(X) = 8$, $\sigma = \sqrt{200 \cdot 0.04 \cdot 0.96} \approx 2.77$

c) After a repair, the management fears that the proportion has risen. It checks $200$ bottles. Formulate a suitable test with $\alpha = 5\,\%$ and find the rejection region. $P(X \ge 13) \approx 0.060$ and $P(X \ge 14) \approx 0.031$.

$H_0\colon p = 0.04$, $H_1\colon p > 0.04$, right-tailed, $X$ binomially distributed with $n = 200$. $K = \{14, \ldots, 200\}$: from $14$ defective bottles on, $H_0$ is rejected.

d) In truth, $8\,\%$ are defective after the repair. What is the probability that the test does not notice this? $P(X \le 13) \approx 0.264$ for $p = 0.08$.

Type II error: $P(X \le 13) \approx 26\,\%$. In roughly one check in four the deterioration would go undetected.

e) In a sample of $200$ bottles, $13$ are defective. Estimate the proportion, decide with the test from c) and formulate both results so that the management understands them.

$h = 0.065$. $13 \notin K$: at the $5\,\%$ level no increase has been shown. The estimate of $6.5\,\%$ is above $4\,\%$, but the difference may still be chance; a larger sample would give more certainty.

f) Advanced course: A camera reports $95\,\%$ of the defective bottles, but also $2\,\%$ of the good ones. How likely is it that a reported bottle really is defective? Set up a two-way table or a tree.

$P(F \cap M) = 0.04 \cdot 0.95 = 0.038$, $P(\bar F \cap M) = 0.96 \cdot 0.02 = 0.0192$. Bayes: $P_M(F) = \tfrac{0.038}{0.0572} \approx 0.66$. Every third report is a false alarm, because there are so many more good bottles.

g) Advanced course: The filling volume is normally distributed with $\mu = 1000$ ml and $\sigma = 6$ ml. What proportion is below $990$ ml? A day's production comprises $2000$ bottles. Use de Moivre–Laplace to approximate the probability that at most $100$ are sealed defectively ($p = 0.04$), and justify that the approximation is admissible.

$\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{990 - 1000}{6}\right) = \Phi(-1.67) \approx 0.048$. Seals: $\mu = 80$, $\sigma = \sqrt{76.8} \approx 8.76 > 3$, admissible. $P(X \le 100) \approx \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{100.5 - 80}{8.76}\right) = \Phi(2.34) \approx 0.990$ (exactly $0.988$).

TIPS FOR THE EXAMAND FOR THE ORAL

TIME

First skim all the problems and start with the one you are surest of. Do not get stuck on one part: leave a gap, carry on, come back later.

It pays to carry on cleanly with a wrong intermediate result; follow-on errors are usually not penalised twice.

WITHOUT AIDS

Derivatives, antiderivatives, scalar products and probabilities with small numbers, done reliably in your head and on paper.

The typical traps are in the training “Konsultation”: chain rule, rules for $\ln$, area versus integral, sine for the angle with a plane.

WITH AIDS

Always write down your approach, then let the CAS calculate. A result without an approach is worth only part of the marks.

Check beforehand: battery, degree mode for angles, variables cleared.

CHECKING

Check by differentiating, point test in the plane, plausibility: an area is never negative, an angle of intersection at most $90^\circ$, a probability never greater than $1$.

Does the result fit the sketch and the situation?

EXCURSIONTO CATCH YOUR BREATH

Three doors, two goats and a thousand doctors

Behind one of three doors there is a car, behind each of the other two a goat. You choose a door. The host, who knows where the car is, opens another door with a goat and offers to let you switch. Is it worth it? In September 1990 Marilyn vos Savant answered this reader's question from Craig F. Whitaker in her column “Ask Marilyn” in the magazine Parade: yes, whoever switches wins with probability $\tfrac23$.

About $10{,}000$ letters followed, almost $1000$ of them from people with doctorates, and most of them thought the answer was wrong. Even the great mathematician Paul Erdős was only convinced by a computer simulation. The reason in one sentence: your first choice hits the car with probability only $\tfrac13$; in the other $\tfrac23$ of cases the host shows you where it is. Try it out yourself.
