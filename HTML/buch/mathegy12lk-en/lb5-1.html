Antiderivatives and integration

Last school year you found the derivative of every function. Now it goes backwards: which function has this derivative? That is how speed turns into position and a rate of inflow into an amount of water. You learn the basic integrals and the rules for integrating sums, multiples and linear substitutions, by hand and with the CAS.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Antiderivative and indefinite integral

Which function has the derivative $2x$? $x^2$, but also $x^2 + 1$ and $x^2 - 7$: when you differentiate, every constant drops out, and when you work backwards you do not know it. All solutions have the form $x^2 + C$. Their graphs are shifted up and down against each other, and at every point they have the same gradient.

A function $F$ with $F'(x) = f(x)$ is called an antiderivative of $f$. So integration is the reverse of differentiation. Once you have found one antiderivative, you know them all: two antiderivatives of the same function differ only by a constant. The set of all antiderivatives is called the indefinite integral.

You always check an antiderivative the same way: differentiate it. If you get $f$, it is correct.

KEY POINT · ANTIDERIVATIVE AND INDEFINITE INTEGRAL

$F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ if $F'(x) = f(x)$ for all $x$.

If $F$ is one, every function $F + C$ is an antiderivative too, and on an interval there are no others. $C$ is called the constant of integration.

Indefinite integral: $\displaystyle\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = F(x) + C$   Say: “the integral of $f(x)$ with respect to $x$”.

The notation goes back to Leibniz: the $\int$ is a long S for “summa”, the sum. You will see why in chapter 5.2.

Reading off gradients: where $f$ is positive, every antiderivative $F$ increases; where $f$ changes sign, $F$ has a turning point.

INTERACTIVE · F AND ITS ANTIDERIVATIVES

At the top an antiderivative $F$ with a constant $C$ you can move and its tangent, at the bottom $f$. The gradient of $F$ at every point is the value of $f$ there, however large $C$ is.

EXAMPLE 1 · CHECKING BY DIFFERENTIATING

Is $F(x) = x^3 - 2x + 5$ an antiderivative of $f(x) = 3x^2 - 2$? And $G(x) = x^3 - x^2$?

$F'(x) = 3x^2 - 2 = f(x)$ ✔ The $5$ drops out when you differentiate.

$G'(x) = 3x^2 - 2x \neq f(x)$: $F$ yes, $G$ no.

EXAMPLE 2 · THROUGH A POINT

Find the antiderivative of $f(x) = 2x + 3$ whose graph passes through $P(1, 6)$.

All antiderivatives: $F(x) = x^2 + 3x + C$. Point test: $F(1) = 1 + 3 + C = 6$, so $C = 2$.

$F(x) = x^2 + 3x + 2$

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Basic integrals and rules of integration

When you differentiate $x^n$, the exponent goes down by $1$ and moves to the front as a factor. Backwards that means: raise the exponent by $1$ and divide by the new exponent. Check for $x^4$: $\left(\tfrac{x^5}{5}\right)' = \tfrac{5x^4}{5} = x^4$ ✔

The rule holds for every exponent except $-1$, so also for roots and fractions if you first write them as powers: $\sqrt{x} = x^{\frac12}$, $\tfrac{1}{x^2} = x^{-2}$. For $r = -1$ there would be a zero in the denominator. The logarithm fills this gap: $(\ln x)' = \tfrac1x$.

As with differentiating, you may integrate term by term, and a constant factor simply stays.

KEY POINT · BASIC INTEGRALS AND RULES

Power rule: $\displaystyle\int x^r\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{x^{r+1}}{r+1} + C$ for every rational number $r \neq -1$

$\displaystyle\int \frac1x\,\mathrm{d}x = \ln|x| + C$ · $\displaystyle\int \mathrm{e}^x\,\mathrm{d}x = \mathrm{e}^x + C$ · $\displaystyle\int \sin x\,\mathrm{d}x = -\cos x + C$ · $\displaystyle\int \cos x\,\mathrm{d}x = \sin x + C$

Constant multiple rule: $\int k \cdot f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = k \cdot \int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ · Sum rule: $\int \big(f(x) + g(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + \int g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$

Why $|x|$? For $x < 0$, $\ln x$ is not defined, but $\left(\ln(-x)\right)' = \tfrac{-1}{-x} = \tfrac1x$. The modulus covers both sides.

Careful: there is no such simple rule for products. $\int x \cdot x\,\mathrm{d}x$ is not $\tfrac{x^2}{2} \cdot \tfrac{x^2}{2}$: multiply out first, $\int x^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{x^3}{3} + C$.

INTERACTIVE · INTEGRATION TRAINER

Type in an antiderivative, for example x^4/4 - 3x, 2/3 x^(3/2) or -cos(x). The book checks whether it differs from a correct solution only by a constant; you may add a “+ C”.

3Linear substitutions and integrating with the CAS

First attempt for $\int \mathrm{e}^{2x}\,\mathrm{d}x$: $\mathrm{e}^{2x}$. With the chain rule the check gives $2\mathrm{e}^{2x}$, twice as much as wanted. So halve it: $\left(\tfrac12 \mathrm{e}^{2x}\right)' = \mathrm{e}^{2x}$ ✔ The factor $\tfrac12$ cancels the inner derivative $2$ again.

This always works when the inner function is linear, that is $ax + b$: you integrate the outer function and divide by $a$. With other inner functions the trick fails, because then the inner derivative is not a constant.

A CAS integrates difficult terms too. But it usually leaves out the $+ C$ and sometimes rearranges differently from you. Two different results can still both be correct if they differ only by a constant. You check this with the derivative or the difference.

KEY POINT · LINEAR SUBSTITUTION

If $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ and $a \neq 0$, then $$\int f(ax + b)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac1a\,F(ax + b) + C$$

$\displaystyle\int \mathrm{e}^{ax+b}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac1a\,\mathrm{e}^{ax+b} + C$ · $\displaystyle\int \sin(ax + b)\,\mathrm{d}x = -\frac1a \cos(ax + b) + C$ · $\displaystyle\int (ax + b)^n\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{(ax + b)^{n+1}}{a\,(n + 1)} + C$ for $n \neq -1$

On the CAS: call up the command for the indefinite integral, enter the term and the variable. Always confirm the result by differentiating; the CAS can do that too.

Limits: for $\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}$ there is no antiderivative made of the known functions. A CAS does not find one either; it can only calculate numerically, see Elective area E.

INTERACTIVE · LINEAR SUBSTITUTIONS

Here all inner functions have the form $ax + b$. Write, for example, e^(3x-1)/3, -cos(2x+1)/2 or (2x+1)^4/8.

4Composite functions: the chain rule backwards and the CAS

$\int 2x\,\mathrm{e}^{x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x$: the inner function $x^2$ is not linear, so the trick from unit 3 fails. But the factor $2x$ is exactly its derivative. Guess $\mathrm{e}^{x^2}$ and differentiate: $\left(\mathrm{e}^{x^2}\right)' = \mathrm{e}^{x^2} \cdot 2x$ ✔ The integrand was a result of the chain rule.

If the integrand contains a composition $f(g(x))$ times the inner derivative $g'(x)$, then $F(g(x))$ is an antiderivative. Often only a constant factor is missing, which you balance as with linear substitutions. An important special case is a fraction whose numerator is the derivative of the denominator: then a logarithm appears.

For all other composite and combined functions you use the CAS and confirm the result by differentiating. Some functions have no antiderivative at all that can be written with the known functions, for example $\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}$, the bell curve from chapter 6. Their integrals are calculated numerically.

KEY POINT · CHAIN RULE BACKWARDS

If $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$, then $$\int f\big(g(x)\big) \cdot g'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = F\big(g(x)\big) + C$$

Special case: $\displaystyle\int \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x = \ln|g(x)| + C$ · Every result, including that of the CAS, is checked by differentiating.

How do you recognise it? You look in the integrand for an inner function whose derivative, apart from a factor, appears once more as a factor.

Products such as $x\,\mathrm{e}^x$ are not chain rule cases. The CAS gives $(x - 1)\,\mathrm{e}^x$, and the product rule confirms it.

FIGURE · FUNCTION AND ANTIDERIVATIVE

$f(x) = 2x\,\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}$ and its antiderivative $F(x) = -\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}$: where $f$ is positive, $F$ increases; at the zero of $f$, $F$ has its minimum.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Newton, Leibniz and the dispute over who was first

Differential and integral calculus were invented by two men independently of each other. Isaac Newton in England called his method “fluxions”. He wrote it down in 1671, but it was not printed until 1736, long after his death. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, born in Leipzig in 1646, first wrote $\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ in a note on 21 November 1675. In 1684 he published the differential calculus in the Leipzig journal Acta Eruditorum, and in 1686 his integral sign appeared there too.

Later, Newton's followers accused Leibniz of plagiarism. The Royal Society in London set up a committee whose report, early in 1713, found in Newton's favour. It had been written, without his name, by Newton himself: he had been President of the Society since 1703. Today both are regarded as inventors. But it was Leibniz's notation that won: the $\int$ and the $\mathrm{d}x$ you use in this chapter.

Source: MacTutor History of Mathematics, University of St Andrews: biographies “Gottfried Wilhelm von Leibniz” and “Isaac Newton”.

$\tfrac{1}{2 \cdot 5}$

By which constant do $\sin^2 x$ and $-\tfrac12 \cos(2x)$ differ?

$\cos(2x) = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$

$v(t) = 4t$, starting at a height of $10$ m: how high is the lift after $2$ s?

$s(t) = 2t^2 + 10$

$\int \tfrac{x}{x^2 + 4}\,\mathrm{d}x = k \cdot \ln(x^2 + 4) + C$. What is $k$?

The numerator is half the derivative of the denominator.
