Areas, density, families and loci

At the end the different areas of mathematics are linked. An area can be calculated with formulae from lower secondary school, with the vector product or with the integral; you learn when which way is shortest. Then a parameter comes into play: families of functions and families of lines describe whole families of graphs. You find common points, loci and the parameter that meets a condition, as in many Abitur problems.

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Areas in three ways

The triangle $(0, 0)$, $(4, 0)$, $(4, 2)$: base times height divided by two gives $4$. The vector product $(4, 0, 0) \times (4, 2, 0) = (0, 0, 8)$ gives $4$ when halved. And because the triangle lies below $y = \tfrac x2$, also $\int_0^4 \tfrac x2\,\mathrm{d}x = 4$. Three ways, one result.

Which way is best depends on the area. Straight edges in a coordinate plane: often the formula from lower secondary school. Straight edges slanted in space: the vector product, because it only needs the corners. Curved boundaries: the integral. Many areas are mixed; then you split them into pieces and calculate each piece in the best way.

KEY POINT · AREAS IN THREE WAYS

Elementary geometry: triangle $\tfrac12 g h$, trapezium $\tfrac{a + c}{2} h$, split polygons into triangles.

With the vector product: parallelogram $|\vec a \times \vec b|$, triangle $ABC$: $\tfrac12 |\vec{AB} \times \vec{AC}|$, slanted in space too.

With the integral: curved boundaries, $A = \left|\int_a^b \big(f(x) - g(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|$ between points of intersection.

In the plane you calculate with the vector product by adding $z = 0$: for $(a_1, a_2)$ and $(b_1, b_2)$ only $|a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1|$ remains.

Projection: If a flat area is projected perpendicularly onto the $x$-$y$ plane, it shrinks by the factor $\cos\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is its angle of inclination.

INTERACTIVE · AREAS WITH THE VECTOR PRODUCT

Parallelogram and triangle from two vectors. Try the roof surface and vectors in the $x$-$y$ plane: then the vector product points straight up.

EXAMPLE 1 · SPLITTING A QUADRILATERAL

Calculate the area of the quadrilateral $(0, 0)$, $(4, 0)$, $(3, 3)$, $(0, 2)$.

Diagonal from $(0, 0)$ to $(3, 3)$. Triangle 1: $\tfrac12 |4 \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot 3| = 6$, triangle 2: $\tfrac12 |3 \cdot 2 - 3 \cdot 0| = 3$.

$A = 9$

EXAMPLE 2 · ARCHIMEDES WITH THE INTEGRAL

Compare the parabolic segment below $4 - x^2$ with the triangle $(-2, 0)$, $(2, 0)$, $(0, 4)$.

Segment: $\int_{-2}^{2} (4 - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{32}{3}$. Triangle: $\tfrac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 = 8$.

The segment is $\tfrac43$ the size of the triangle, just as Archimedes found without an integral.

UNIT 22 LESSONS

2Unbounded areas, density and probability

In the excursion of Chapter 5.3, the area under $y = \tfrac{1}{x^2}$ to the right of $x = 1$ had the size $1$, although it reaches to infinity: $\int_1^b \tfrac{1}{x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x = 1 - \tfrac1b \to 1$. For the bell curve from Chapter 6 the total area was also $1$, over the whole number line. Such areas are calculated as the limit of ordinary integrals.

You integrate up to a movable limit $b$ and then let $b$ tend to infinity. If the limit exists, the unbounded area has a finite size. You proceed in the same way if the graph has a pole at one of the limits, for example $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt x}$ at $0$. This matters for probabilities: a density may live on an unbounded interval, but its total area must be $1$. The density $\lambda\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}$, for example, describes the lifetime of components.

KEY POINT · UNBOUNDED AREAS

$\displaystyle\int_a^{\infty} f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$, if the limit exists. With a pole at the limit $a$: $\displaystyle\lim_{c \to a} \int_c^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$.

$\int_1^\infty \tfrac{1}{x^r}\,\mathrm{d}x$ is finite for $r > 1$ and infinite for $r \le 1$. $\int_0^1 \tfrac{1}{x^r}\,\mathrm{d}x$ is finite for $r < 1$.

Density: $f \ge 0$ and total area $1$; $P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ is the area over $[a, b]$.

Calculate with a bound first: Never “substitute” $\infty$ into the antiderivative. First calculate with $b$, then take the limit.

Exponential distribution: For $t \ge 0$, $f(t) = \lambda\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}$ is a density, because $\int_0^b \lambda\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\,\mathrm{d}t = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\lambda b} \to 1$.

INTERACTIVE · OUT TO INFINITY

Push the limit further and further. For $\tfrac{1}{x^2}$ and $\mathrm{e}^{-x}$ the area hardly grows any more; for $\tfrac1x$ it grows without end. For $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt x}$ the limit moves towards the pole.

EXAMPLE 3 · FINITE OR NOT?

Calculate $\int_1^\infty \tfrac{1}{x^3}\,\mathrm{d}x$ and investigate $\int_1^\infty \tfrac{1}{\sqrt x}\,\mathrm{d}x$.

$\int_1^b x^{-3}\,\mathrm{d}x = \left[-\tfrac{1}{2x^2}\right]_1^b = \tfrac12 - \tfrac{1}{2b^2} \to \tfrac12$. By contrast, $\int_1^b x^{-\frac12}\,\mathrm{d}x = 2\sqrt b - 2 \to \infty$.

$\tfrac12$; the second area is unboundedly large.

EXAMPLE 4 · LIFETIME

The lifetime $T$ (in h) of an LED lamp has the density $f(t) = 0.0001\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.0001 t}$. How likely is it to last longer than $10{,}000$ h? After what time have half of them failed?

$P(T > 10{,}000) = \lim_{b \to \infty} \left[-\mathrm{e}^{-0.0001 t}\right]_{10{,}000}^{b} = \mathrm{e}^{-1} \approx 0.368$.

$\mathrm{e}^{-0.0001 t} = 0.5$: $t = \tfrac{\ln 2}{0.0001} \approx 6931$ h

UNIT 33 LESSONS

3Families of functions and loci

For every value of $a$, $f_a(x) = -x^2 + ax$ is a different parabola. All of them pass through the origin, because $f_a(0) = 0$. Their vertices are at $\left(\tfrac a2, \tfrac{a^2}{4}\right)$. If you set $x = \tfrac a2$, i.e. $a = 2x$, then $y = \tfrac{a^2}{4} = x^2$: all the vertices lie on the standard parabola.

Such a family is called a family of functions, and $a$ is its parameter. You investigate it like a single function, except that $a$ comes along as a fixed but unknown number. You often need to distinguish cases: for some $a$ there are extreme points, for others there are not. The curve on which all the extreme points (or points of inflection) lie is called the locus. A CAS with a slider helps with discovering; the justification comes from the calculation.

KEY POINT · FAMILIES OF FUNCTIONS

Common points: solve $f_a(x) = f_b(x)$ for $a \neq b$; the solutions do not depend on the parameter.

Special points: differentiate with respect to $x$, treat $a$ like a number; distinguish cases for $a$.

Locus: point $\big(x(a), y(a)\big)$; solve the equation $x = x(a)$ for $a$ and substitute into $y(a)$.

Notation: $f_a$ is a function of $x$; the index $a$ says which member of the family is meant. $f_a'$ is the derivative with respect to $x$.

Finding the parameter: write down the condition (point, gradient, area) and solve for $a$, as when finding a function from its properties in Year 11.

INTERACTIVE · A FAMILY AND ITS LOCUS

Move the parameter $a$. The other members of the family are grey, the locus of all the extreme points is violet.

EXAMPLE 5 · A CUBIC FAMILY

Investigate $f_a(x) = x^3 - 3ax^2$ for $a > 0$ for extreme points and points of inflection. On which curve do the points of inflection lie?

$f_a'(x) = 3x(x - 2a)$: maximum point $(0, 0)$, minimum point $(2a, -4a^3)$. $f_a''(x) = 6x - 6a = 0$: point of inflection $(a, -2a^3)$.

Substituting $x = a$: locus of the points of inflection $y = -2x^3$

EXAMPLE 6 · PARAMETER FROM AN AREA

The graph of $f_t(x) = tx - x^2$ with $t > 0$ encloses an area with the $x$-axis. For which $t$ is it $36$?

Zeros $0$ and $t$: $\int_0^t (tx - x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac{t^3}{2} - \tfrac{t^3}{3} = \tfrac{t^3}{6}$.

$\tfrac{t^3}{6} = 36$: $t = 6$

EXERCISES

Which points do all the graphs of $f_a(x) = a\,x\,(x - 2)$ with $a \neq 0$ have in common?

$(a - b)\,x\,(x - 2) = 0$: $(0, 0)$ and $(2, 0)$

How many zeros does $f_a(x) = x^3 - ax$ have, depending on $a$? Where are the extreme points for $a > 0$?

$x(x^2 - a) = 0$: three for $a > 0$ ($0$, $\pm\sqrt a$), one for $a \le 0$. Extreme points: $3x^2 = a$, $x = \pm\sqrt{\tfrac a3}$.

For $f_a(x) = \mathrm{e}^x - ax$ with $a > 0$, find the minimum point and its locus. For which $a$ does the minimum point lie on the $x$-axis?

$\mathrm{e}^x = a$: $T(\ln a, a - a\ln a)$. With $a = \mathrm{e}^x$: locus $y = \mathrm{e}^x (1 - x)$. On the axis: $a(1 - \ln a) = 0$, $a = \mathrm{e}$.

a) $2x - a = 0$ at $x = 3$: $a = 6$. b) Zeros $0$ and $a$, area $\tfrac{a^3}{6} = 36$: $a = 6$. It is the same member of the family, and it is the mirror image of $f_6$ from Example 4 in the $x$-axis.

UNIT 42 LESSONS

4Families of lines and planes, connected exercises

$y = a(x - 2) + 1$: for $x = 2$, $y = 1$, whatever $a$ is. All the lines of this family pass through $(2, 1)$; they form a pencil of lines. With $y = 2x + a$, on the other hand, the gradient stays fixed and the parameter only shifts: a family of parallels.

In space the parameter sits in the position vector or in the direction vector. Typical questions: for which parameter does the line pass through a point, is it perpendicular to a vector or a plane, does it enclose a given angle, does it have a given distance? Each question gives an equation for the parameter. In connected Abitur problems such conditions come together with derivatives, integrals and probabilities.

KEY POINT · FAMILIES OF LINES

Pencil: all the lines have one point in common. Family of parallels: all have the same direction.

Parameter from a condition: point test, $\vec u_a \circ \vec v = 0$ (perpendicular), angle formula, distance formula or touching (discriminant $= 0$).

Families of planes $E_a$: if the equation holds for all $a$ at the same points, sort by $a$: $a \cdot (\ldots) + (\ldots) = 0$ for all $a$ means both brackets are $0$. These points often form a common axis line (pencil of planes).

Check whether there are no, one or several solutions, and interpret all of them.

Gap in the pencil: With $y = a(x - 2) + 1$ you get every line through $(2, 1)$ except the vertical line $x = 2$, because it has no gradient.

Touching: A line touches a parabola if the intersection equation has exactly one solution. The discriminant decides.

INTERACTIVE · FAMILIES OF LINES

Choose pencil, parallels or line and parabola and move the parameter. With the parabola you see the points of intersection in red and the discriminant below.

EXAMPLE 7 · TOUCHING

Which lines of the family $y = ax + 1$ touch the parabola $y = x^2 + 2$, and where?

$x^2 + 2 = ax + 1$, so $x^2 - ax + 1 = 0$. Exactly one solution: discriminant $a^2 - 4 = 0$, $a = \pm 2$.

$y = 2x + 1$ touches at $(1, 3)$, $y = -2x + 1$ at $(-1, 3)$

EXAMPLE 8 · A FAMILY IN SPACE

$g_a\colon \vec x = (1, 2, 3) + t(1, a, 0)$. For which $a$ is $g_a$ perpendicular to $\vec v = (2, 1, 5)$? For which $a$ does $g_a$ make $45^\circ$ with the $x$-axis?

$(1, a, 0) \circ (2, 1, 5) = 2 + a = 0$: $a = -2$. Angle: $\cos 45^\circ = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + a^2}}$, so $a^2 = 1$.

$a = -2$; $a = \pm 1$. All the $g_a$ pass through $(1, 2, 3)$ and lie in the plane $z = 3$.

EXCURSIONMATHEMATICS TO MARVEL AT

A parabola made of straight lines

If you stretch threads between the two arms of a right angle, the first point on one arm to the last on the other, the second to the second-to-last and so on, a curved line appears, although every single thread is straight. The threads only touch the curve: it is the envelope of the family of lines.

You can check this with exercise 19: the lines $y = ax - \tfrac{a^2}{4}$ all touch the standard parabola, each at a different point. The curve of the string art picture is a parabola too. With the arms on the axes and threads from $(k, 0)$ to $(0, c - k)$, the envelope has the equation $\sqrt x + \sqrt y = \sqrt c$, and if you square twice, you get the equation of a parabola that lies at an angle to the axes.

To check: squaring $\sqrt x + \sqrt y = \sqrt c$ twice gives $(x - y)^2 - 2c(x + y) + c^2 = 0$, a parabola with the axis of symmetry $y = x$.
