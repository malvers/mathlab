Numerical integration

The bell curve $\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}$ is one of the most important functions in statistics, but it has no antiderivative you could write down. Measurements from an experiment come without a function term too. Then only calculating with strips helps: you get to know the rectangle and the trapezium rule, fit parabolas instead of lines with Kepler and Simpson, judge the accuracy and retrace the path that integral calculus took from Archimedes to Riemann.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1From history, and the rectangle rule

Long before antiderivatives existed, mathematicians calculated areas with curved boundaries using shapes they knew. In the 3rd century BC, Archimedes filled a parabolic segment with smaller and smaller triangles and found that it is $\tfrac43$ of the largest triangle inside it. Today we use rectangles, and a computer calculates thousands of them in seconds.

You divide $[a, b]$ into $n$ strips of equal width $h = \tfrac{b - a}{n}$ with the sample points $x_0 = a$, $x_1 = a + h$, …, $x_n = b$. Above each strip stands a rectangle. Its height is the function value at the left edge, at the right edge or in the middle of the strip. The more strips, the more accurate. The midpoint rule is usually much more accurate than the other two, because within each strip too much and too little almost cancel out.

KEY POINT · RECTANGLE RULE

With $h = \tfrac{b - a}{n}$ and $x_i = a + i \cdot h$:

left: $L_n = h \cdot \big(f(x_0) + f(x_1) + \ldots + f(x_{n-1})\big)$ · right: $R_n = h \cdot \big(f(x_1) + \ldots + f(x_n)\big)$

Midpoint rule: $M_n = h \cdot \Big(f\big(x_0 + \tfrac h2\big) + f\big(x_1 + \tfrac h2\big) + \ldots + f\big(x_{n-1} + \tfrac h2\big)\Big)$

Timeline: Archimedes, 3rd century BC · Kepler, barrel rule 1615 · Leibniz first writes $\int$ in 1675 · in the 19th century Riemann clarifies what an integral is.

Spreadsheet: column A the sample points, column B the function values, then $h$ times the sum of the right cells.

EXAMPLE 1 · THREE RULES COMPARED

Approximate $\int_0^2 x^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac83 \approx 2.667$ with four strips.

$h = 0.5$: $L_4 = 0.5 \cdot (0 + 0.25 + 1 + 2.25) = 1.75$, $R_4 = 0.5 \cdot (0.25 + 1 + 2.25 + 4) = 3.75$.

$M_4 = 0.5 \cdot (0.0625 + 0.5625 + 1.5625 + 3.0625) = 2.625$: errors $-0.92$, $+1.08$, only $-0.04$.

UNIT 23 LESSONS

2The trapezium rule and accuracy

If you join neighbouring points of the graph with line segments, you get trapezia instead of rectangles. Each has the area $h \cdot \tfrac{f(x_{i}) + f(x_{i+1})}{2}$. When you add them up, every inner function value counts fully, because it belongs to two trapezia. The two end values count only half.

The trapezium sum is exactly the mean of the left and right rectangle sums. It only needs the values at the sample points, which suits series of measurements perfectly. How accurate is it? If you double the number of strips, the error of the trapezium and the midpoint rule falls to about a quarter, that of the left and right rectangles only to about a half. If the graph is convex, the chords lie above it: the trapezia are then too large.

KEY POINT · TRAPEZIUM RULE

$$T_n = h \cdot \Big(\tfrac12 f(x_0) + f(x_1) + \ldots + f(x_{n-1}) + \tfrac12 f(x_n)\Big) = \frac{L_n + R_n}{2}$$

Error when $n$ is doubled: left and right rectangles roughly halved, midpoint and trapezium roughly quartered.

Curvature: convex: $T_n$ too large, $M_n$ too small · concave: the other way round.

Without an antiderivative: $\int_0^1 \mathrm{e}^{-x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x \approx 0.746824$ can only be calculated numerically. The bell curve is part of the normal distribution, which you will meet in chapter 6.

A trick: because the error roughly quarters when you double, $T_{2n} + \tfrac13 (T_{2n} - T_n)$ gives an even much better value.

3Parabolas instead of chords: the barrel rule and Simpson

The trapezium rule joins two points of the graph with a straight line. With curved graphs a piece is always left over. But a parabola fits through three points, and it follows the curvature. Over two strips of width $h$ its area is surprisingly simple: $\tfrac h3 (y_0 + 4y_1 + y_2)$. For $\int_0^1 \mathrm{e}^{-x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x$ this gives $0.74718$ with only three function values, more accurate than four trapeziums.

Why the weights $1$, $4$, $1$? Put the midpoint at the origin, so $p(x) = \alpha x^2 + \beta x + \gamma$ on $[-h, h]$. Then $\int_{-h}^{h} p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac23 \alpha h^3 + 2\gamma h$. On the other hand, $p(-h) + 4p(0) + p(h) = 2\alpha h^2 + 6\gamma$, and times $\tfrac h3$ this gives exactly the same. If you divide $[a, b]$ into an even number of strips and put a parabola over each pair, you get Simpson's rule. When $h$ is halved, its error shrinks to about a sixteenth, the trapezium rule's only to a quarter.

KEY POINT · KEPLER'S BARREL RULE AND SIMPSON'S RULE

Barrel rule (one parabolic arc, midpoint $m$): $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \approx \frac{b - a}{6}\big(f(a) + 4f(m) + f(b)\big)$

Simpson ($n$ even, $h = \tfrac{b - a}{n}$): $S_n = \dfrac h3 \big(f_0 + 4f_1 + 2f_2 + 4f_3 + \ldots + 2f_{n-2} + 4f_{n-1} + f_n\big)$

Exact for all polynomials up to degree 3. Stopping rule: double $n$ until $|S_{2n} - S_n|$ is smaller than the accuracy you want.

Names: Thomas Simpson published the rule in 1743, but it was known before him. The barrel rule is its special case with $n = 2$.

Speed: for six correct decimal places of $\ln 2 = \int_1^2 \tfrac1x\,\mathrm{d}x$, Simpson needs $16$ strips, the trapezium rule over $200$.

Approximate $\ln 2 = \int_1^2 \tfrac1x\,\mathrm{d}x$ with $T_4$ and $S_4$ and compare the errors.

$h = 0.25$, values $1$, $0.8$, $\tfrac23$, $\tfrac47$, $0.5$. $T_4 \approx 0.69702$, $S_4 = \tfrac{0.25}{3}\left(1 + 3.2 + \tfrac43 + \tfrac{16}{7} + 0.5\right) \approx 0.69325$.

EXAMPLE 6 · EXACT FOR CUBIC POLYNOMIALS

Show that the barrel rule gives $\int_0^2 x^3\,\mathrm{d}x$ exactly, but not $\int_0^2 x^4\,\mathrm{d}x$.

$\tfrac26 (0 + 4 \cdot 1 + 8) = 4 = \left[\tfrac14 x^4\right]_0^2$ ✔ For $x^4$: $\tfrac26 (0 + 4 + 16) = \tfrac{20}{3} \approx 6.67$, exactly it is $\tfrac{32}{5} = 6.4$.

Degree $3$ exact, degree $4$ not: the cubic part has point symmetry about the midpoint and cancels out.

Calculate $\int_0^\pi \sin x\,\mathrm{d}x$ with the barrel rule and with $S_4$. The exact value is $2$.

A river is $12$ m wide. At intervals of $2$ m the depths $0$, $1.2$, $2.0$, $2.3$, $1.6$, $0.8$, $0$ m are measured. Calculate the cross-section with the trapezium rule and with Simpson.

Show: $S_{2n} = \tfrac13 (4\,T_{2n} - T_n)$. Test the formula on $\int_1^2 \tfrac1x\,\mathrm{d}x$ with $T_2 \approx 0.70833$ and $T_4 \approx 0.69702$.

In $4T_{2n} - T_n$ the new midpoints get the weight $4 \cdot \tfrac h2 \cdot 1 = 2h$, the old inner points $4 \cdot \tfrac h2 - h = h$, the ends $\tfrac h2$. Dividing by $3$: the Simpson weights $\tfrac{h'}{3}(1, 4, 2, \ldots)$ with $h' = \tfrac h2$. Numbers: $\tfrac13 (2.78810 - 0.70833) \approx 0.69325 = S_4$ ✔

With the trapezium rule the error is divided by about $4$ when $n$ is doubled, with Simpson by $16$. Roughly how many strips does each rule need for $\ln 2$ with an error below $10^{-6}$? Start from the errors for $n = 4$: trapezium $0.0039$, Simpson $0.00011$.

Trapezium: $0.0039 / 4^k < 10^{-6}$ from $k = 6$, so $n = 4 \cdot 2^6 = 256$. Simpson: $0.00011 / 16^k < 10^{-6}$ from $k = 2$, so $n = 16$. Here Simpson is sixteen times more economical.

Calculate $M_2$ and $T_2$ for $\int_0^\pi \sin x\,\mathrm{d}x = 2$. Which rule is closer?

$h = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$: $M_2 = \tfrac{\pi}{2} \cdot (\sin\tfrac{\pi}{4} + \sin\tfrac{3\pi}{4}) \approx 2.221$, $T_2 = \tfrac{\pi}{2} \cdot 1 \approx 1.571$. The midpoint rule is closer.

Connecting with unit 1: show by rearranging that $T_n = \tfrac12 (L_n + R_n)$.

$L_n + R_n = h \cdot \big(f(x_0) + 2f(x_1) + \ldots + 2f(x_{n-1}) + f(x_n)\big)$. Halving gives exactly the trapezium formula.

Describe how you calculate $T_{10}$ for $\int_0^1 \mathrm{e}^{-x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x$ in a spreadsheet: which columns, which formula?

Column A: $0, 0.1, \ldots, 1$. Column B: $=\mathrm{EXP}(-(A2^2))$. Then $T_{10} = 0.1 \cdot \big(\tfrac12 B_2 + B_3 + \ldots + B_{11} + \tfrac12 B_{12}\big)$, that is $0.1 \cdot (\mathrm{SUM}(B2{:}B12) - \tfrac12 (B2 + B12))$.

Connecting with chapter 5.3: Kepler wanted to measure barrels. A barrel has the profile $f(x) = 2 - 0.08x^2$ on $[-2.5, 2.5]$ (in dm). Calculate its volume $\pi \int f(x)^2\,\mathrm{d}x$ with the barrel rule and compare with the exact value $\tfrac{203}{12}\pi$.

$f(-2.5)^2 = f(2.5)^2 = 1.5^2 = 2.25$, $f(0)^2 = 4$. Barrel rule: $\pi \cdot \tfrac56 (2.25 + 16 + 2.25) = \pi \cdot \tfrac{205}{12} \approx 53.67$ dm³. Exactly $\approx 53.15$ dm³: only $1\%$ out, because $f^2$ has degree $4$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Kepler and the wine barrels

Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) is known above all as an astronomer. In 1613 he married for the second time. At the wedding celebrations he noticed how the wine in the barrel was measured: a rod was pushed diagonally through the bung hole, and the volume was inferred from the wetted length, however the barrel was curved.

He could not let it go. He studied the volumes of solids formed by rotation and, much like Archimedes, cut them into infinitely many thin slices. In 1615 his book Nova stereometria doliorum vinariorum, a “new measurement of the volume of wine barrels”, appeared in Linz. The approximation from unit 3 is named after him: Kepler's barrel rule. It takes the cross-section at the bottom, at the top and four times the one in the middle.

Source: MacTutor History of Mathematics, University of St Andrews: biography “Johannes Kepler”.
