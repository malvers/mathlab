Calculating with decimals

How much does the shopping cost, how much money is left over, how long is the route if you run it ten times? You calculate with decimals almost as with natural numbers. You only need to know where the decimal point goes. At the end of the chapter you get to know the mean: the number that comes out if you share everything out fairly.

UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Adding and subtracting

If you calculate in cents, everything is easy: $249 + 115 + 320 + 405 = 1089$ cents, so €10.89. Change: $2000 - 1089 = 911$ cents, so €9.11. Careful with the apples: €3.2 is €3 and 20 cents, not 2 cents!

When calculating on paper, you write the numbers underneath each other so that point is under point. Then tenths are under tenths and hundredths under hundredths too. You fill empty places with zeros.

KEY POINT · POINT UNDER POINT
2.49
+3.20
+4.05
9.74

Estimate first: $2 + 3 + 4 = 9$. The result 9.74 fits.

Typical mistake: $2.49 + 3.2$ is not $2.81$. Anyone who lines the numbers up on the right adds hundredths to tenths.

In your head: $5.7 + 2.5 = 5 + 2 + 0.7 + 0.5 = 7 + 1.2 = 8.2$

EXAMPLE 1 · SUBTRACTING

Calculate $20 - 10.89$.

$20 = 20.00$. Underneath each other: $20.00 - 10.89$. Counting on helps: $10.89 + 0.11 = 11$, $11 + 9 = 20$.

UNIT 23 LESSONS

2Multiplying and dividing

10 sweets weigh $10 \cdot 2.5 = 25$ g, 100 sweets 250 g, 1000 sweets 2,500 g. With every multiplication by 10, every digit moves one place to the left. The other way round: 25 g shared between 10 sweets is 2.5 g per sweet.

KEY POINT · MOVING THE DECIMAL POINT

Times 10, 100, 1000: the decimal point moves 1, 2, 3 places to the right. $3.75 \cdot 100 = 375$

Divided by 10, 100, 1000: the decimal point moves 1, 2, 3 places to the left. $3.75 \div 100 = 0.0375$

Missing places are filled with zeros: $0.4 \cdot 1000 = 400$.

KEY POINT · WITH A ONE-DIGIT NUMBER

Multiplying: calculate without the decimal point and then put it in so that the result has as many decimal places as the decimal. $2.4 \cdot 6$: $24 \cdot 6 = 144$, so $14.4$.

Dividing: calculate as usual and put the decimal point in the result as soon as you pass the decimal point while dividing. $2.4 \div 6 = 0.4$, because $0.4 \cdot 6 = 2.4$.

An estimate shows whether the decimal point is in the right place: $2.4 \cdot 6 \approx 2 \cdot 6 = 12$.

Strictly speaking the decimal point stays where it is and the digits move. You can see this exactly in the widget. For calculating, though, “the point moves” is a good short rule.

UNIT 31 LESSON

3The arithmetic mean

Together the five friends have $6 + 3 + 8 + 2 + 6 = 25$ marbles. Shared out fairly, each one gets $25 \div 5 = 5$ marbles. 5 is the mean of the numbers 6, 3, 8, 2 and 6.

KEY POINT · ARITHMETIC MEAN

You get the arithmetic mean (for short: the mean, the average) of several numbers like this:

$$\text{mean} = \frac{\text{sum of all the values}}{\text{number of values}}$$

The mean does not have to be one of the values itself, and it can be a decimal: the mean of 1, 2 and 4 is $7 \div 3 \approx 2.33$.

In the middle: the mean always lies between the smallest and the biggest value.

INTERACTIVE · THE MEAN AS EVENING OUT
EXAMPLE 5 · TEMPERATURES

On five days at midday 12.5 °C, 14 °C, 9.5 °C, 11 °C and 13 °C were measured. What was the mean temperature?

Sum 60 °C, $60 \div 5 = 12$.

12 °C

EXAMPLE 6 · ONE VALUE IS MISSING

Lisa has 8, 6 and 9 points in three tests. How many points does she need in the fourth so that her mean is 8?

For 4 tests with a mean of 8 she needs $4 \cdot 8 = 32$ points altogether. She has 23.

9 points

The mean of five numbers is 6. Four of the numbers are 4, 9, 5 and 7. What is the fifth? And how does the mean change if you add 2 to every number?

Sum $5 \cdot 6 = 30$; the fifth number is $30 - 25 = 5$. If you add 2 to every number, the mean also goes up by 2: 8.

EXCURSIONSPORT

9.58 seconds

On 16 August 2009, at the World Championships in Berlin’s Olympic Stadium, the Jamaican Usain Bolt ran the 100 metres in 9.58 seconds, a world record that has stood for many years since. A year earlier in Beijing his record had still been 9.69 seconds, so he had become 0.11 seconds faster. Four days after the 100 m race he also ran the 200 metres in a world-record time: 19.19 seconds.

In sport, hundredths of a second often decide. That is why timing is electronic: the starting gun starts the clock, and a camera at the finishing line stops it.

Work it out: how long did Bolt need on average for 10 metres? And how does the 200 metres compare with twice 100 metres?

Source: World Athletics: Usain Bolt 9.58 seconds world record in Berlin, report of 16 August 2009, and the World Athletics list of world records.

Solution: $9.58 \div 10 = 0.958$ s for 10 m. Twice 9.58 s is 19.16 s, only 0.03 s less than 19.19 s.
