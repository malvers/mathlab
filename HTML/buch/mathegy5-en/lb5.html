UNIT 13 LESSONS

1Quantities and units

A day has 24 hours, that is $24 \cdot 60 = 1{,}440$ minutes. If you sleep for 9 hours, you are not awake for $540$ minutes, that is more than a third of the day.

A quantity consists of a number and a unit: 3.5 km, 250 g, 45 min. When you calculate, both quantities must have the same unit. The unit staircase helps with converting: towards the smaller unit the number gets bigger, towards the larger one smaller.

KEY POINT · CONVERSION FACTORS
Lengthkm ⟷ m: 1000 · m ⟷ dm ⟷ cm ⟷ mm: 10 each
Masst ⟷ kg ⟷ g ⟷ mg: 1000 each
Timed ⟷ h: 24 · h ⟷ min ⟷ s: 60 each
Areakm² ⟷ ha ⟷ a ⟷ m² ⟷ dm² ⟷ cm² ⟷ mm²: 100 each
Volumem³ ⟷ dm³ (l) ⟷ cm³ (ml) ⟷ mm³: 1000 each

Careful with time: 1.5 h is not 1 h 50 min, but 1 h 30 min. Time does not go in steps of ten.

Mixed amounts are written in one unit: 3 m 5 cm = 3.05 m = 305 cm.

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Timetables, approximate values and Fermi problems

About 200 hairs grow on one square centimetre of scalp. The hairy part of the scalp is roughly as big as a rectangle of 20 cm by 30 cm, that is 600 cm². That gives about $200 \cdot 600 = 120{,}000$ hairs. Nobody knows the exact number, but “around a hundred thousand” is a good answer.

Problems of this kind are called Fermi problems, after the physicist Enrico Fermi, who liked to ask his students such questions. You split the problem into parts that you can estimate or look up, and calculate with rounded numbers.

KEY POINT · ROUNDING SENSIBLY

A result cannot be more precise than the numbers it was calculated from. If you calculate with estimates, you give the result rounded.

Look at the situation: with buses, boxes and tickets you sometimes have to round up, even though the rounding rule would round down.

Reading a timetable: the columns are the journeys, the rows the stops. A journey time is the difference between two times.

Sensible precision: “The way to school takes 17.384 minutes” sounds precise, but is nonsense. “About 17 minutes” is right.

EXAMPLE 4 · FERMI: PIANOS

How many piano tuners might there be in Dresden?

Dresden has about 560,000 inhabitants, so about 280,000 households. Perhaps one in twenty has a piano: 14,000 pianos. Each is tuned once a year, which takes about 2 hours including the journey: 28,000 hours of work. One person works about 1,600 hours a year.

about 15 to 20 people

EXAMPLE 5 · ROUNDING UP

130 sausages are needed for a party. They are sold in packs of 12. How many packs do you buy?

$130 \div 12 = 10$ remainder 10. Ten packs are not enough.

11 packs

UNIT 33 LESSONS

3Project: mathematics at the school sports day

Real problems do not come as ready-made calculations. You have to find out what you want to know, which information is missing, and make a plan.

KEY POINT · SOLVING A PROBLEM
  1. Understand: what are you looking for? What do you already know?
  2. Find information: what is missing? Measure, ask, look it up or estimate.
  3. Split up: divide the big problem into small sub-problems.
  4. Make a plan and calculate.
  5. Check: does the result make sense? Is the unit right, is the order of magnitude right?

Be honest: write down which numbers you measured and which you estimated. Then others can follow your result.

PROJECT · THE SCHOOL SPORTS DAY

Your class is running the drinks and fruit stall at the sports day. About 400 students and 40 teachers are coming, the event lasts from 8:00 to 13:00.

  1. How much water has to be ready? Estimate how much one person drinks during 5 hours of sport.
  2. How many crates of 12 bottles of 0.75 l is that? What do they cost if a crate costs €4.99?
  3. How many apples and bananas do you need? How heavy is all of that together? Does it fit into a car?
  4. Plan the running course: one lap of the sports ground is 400 m long. How many laps are 3 km?
  5. How can you avoid cups and packaging waste? Calculate how much waste you save.

Present your plan to the class: with calculations, assumptions and a shopping list.

EXERCISES

Work out part 2 of the project with the assumption of 0.75 l per person: how many crates do you need, and what do they cost?

440 people · 0.75 l = 330 l. A crate holds $12 \cdot 0.75 = 9$ l. $330 \div 9 = 36$ remainder 6, so 37 crates. Cost: $37 \cdot 4.99 = 184.63$ euros.

A class wants to raise money for a trip by selling waffles. A waffle costs €0.30 to make and is sold for €1. How many waffles must be sold for €140 of profit to be left? What else do you have to think of?

Profit per waffle €0.70: $140 \div 0.7 = 200$ waffles. To think of: the waffle iron, electricity, batter left over, time for baking.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

A letter weighs 18 g. How much do 250 such letters weigh in kg?

$18 \cdot 250 = 4{,}500$ g = 4.5 kg

Connecting with chapter 4.2: a classroom is 9 m long, 7 m wide and 3 m high. A person breathes about 500 l of air in an hour. For how many person-hours does the air in the room last if it is not aired? Why do you have to air it all the same?

$9 \cdot 7 \cdot 3 = 189$ m³ = 189,000 l. $189{,}000 \div 500 = 378$ person-hours, for 28 people about 13 hours. But the air gets used up and stuffy much earlier, because you take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide.

Fermi problem: how much do all the students of your school weigh together? Would a lorry with a 7.5 t payload be enough?

For example 600 people weighing 45 kg on average: 27,000 kg = 27 t. You would need about four such lorries.

EXCURSIONSPACE TRAVEL

A space probe and a mistake with units

In December 1998 the American space agency NASA launched the probe Mars Climate Orbiter. It was to observe the weather on Mars. After nine months of flight it reached Mars on 23 September 1999, and then it had disappeared.

The investigation report of 10 November 1999 found the reason: a computer program on Earth calculated the effect of small control thrusters in an American unit, the “pound-force”. But the navigation program expected the metric unit newton. One pound-force is about 4.45 newtons. So the effect of the thrusters was misjudged by a factor of 4.45. The probe flew much too low into the Martian atmosphere and burnt up or broke apart.

The mission cost several hundred million dollars. Since then the rule in space travel has been even stricter: every number comes with its unit, and units are always checked.

Source: NASA: Mars Climate Orbiter Mishap Investigation Board, Phase I Report, 10 November 1999.
