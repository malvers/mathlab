UNIT 12 LESSONS

1Parts of a whole and pie charts

In a class of 25 students, 8 come by bike. That is the part $\tfrac{8}{25} = \tfrac{32}{100} = 32\,\%$. In the pie chart the bike gets $32\,\%$ of the circle, that is $0.32 \cdot 360^\circ = 115.2^\circ$. One person stands for $360^\circ : 25 = 14.4^\circ$.

A part of a whole (or proportion) compares a part with the whole. You can give it as a fraction, as a decimal or as a percentage, as you know from chapter 1.1. A pie chart shows parts as sectors of a circle. The whole circle is the whole, that is $100\,\%$ or $360^\circ$.

KEY POINT · PARTS AND PIE CHARTS

Part $= \dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}}$   as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage

Angle in the pie chart $=$ part $\cdot\, 360^\circ$   ·   $1\,\% \;\widehat{=}\; 3.6^\circ$

All the parts together make $1 = 100\,\% = 360^\circ$.

Drawing: choose the centre and the radius, start vertically at the top, mark off the angles one after the other with the set square.

When a pie chart? When the parts together make a whole. It does not suit temperatures over a day.

EXAMPLE 1 · POCKET MONEY

Lina gets €20 pocket money. She spends €8 on the cinema and €5 on sweets, and she saves the rest. Draw a pie chart.

Cinema $\tfrac{8}{20} = 40\,\% \to 144^\circ$ · sweets $\tfrac{5}{20} = 25\,\% \to 90^\circ$ · saving $\tfrac{7}{20} = 35\,\% \to 126^\circ$.

Check: $144^\circ + 90^\circ + 126^\circ = 360^\circ$

EXAMPLE 2 · READING A CHART

In a pie chart about 600 people surveyed, one slice has the angle $72^\circ$. How many people is that?

Part: $\tfrac{72}{360} = \tfrac15 = 20\,\%$. Of 600 people: $\tfrac15 \cdot 600$.

120 people

In an election for class representative Mia gets 12 votes, Jonas 9 and Ela 3. Give the parts as percentages and draw a pie chart.

24 votes. Mia $50\,\% \to 180^\circ$, Jonas $37.5\,\% \to 135^\circ$, Ela $12.5\,\% \to 45^\circ$.

A pie chart shows the favourite fruits of 40 people: apple $108^\circ$, banana $90^\circ$, strawberry $126^\circ$, the rest kiwi. How many people chose each fruit?

One person stands for $9^\circ$. Apple 12, banana 10, strawberry 14, kiwi: $36^\circ$, so 4.

A chart in a newspaper shows: “Favourite subjects: PE 45%, maths 30%, art 20%, music 15%”. What is wrong here? How could it still be right?

Together that is $110\,\%$. That is impossible for a pie chart. It can only be right if each person was allowed to name several subjects. Then a bar chart is more suitable.

UNIT 22 LESSONS

2Parts of areas and volumes

A kite with the diagonals $e$ and $f$ lies in a rectangle with the sides $e$ and $f$. Its area is $\tfrac12 \cdot e \cdot f$, that of the rectangle $e \cdot f$. So the kite covers exactly $\tfrac12 = 50\,\%$ of the rectangle, however big it is.

The part of the area taken up by a piece is its area divided by the area of the whole figure. Both must be measured in the same unit. In the same way the part of the volume taken up by a piece of a solid is its volume divided by the total volume.

For a prism that is partly filled, chapter 4 helps: the volume is base times height. If an aquarium is filled to three quarters of its height, it is also three quarters full.

KEY POINT · PARTS OF AREAS AND VOLUMES

Part of the area $= \dfrac{\text{area of the piece}}{\text{total area}}$   ·   Part of the volume $= \dfrac{\text{volume of the piece}}{\text{total volume}}$

For a prism with the same base: part of the volume = part of the height.

Quick estimate: on squared paper you can count squares and estimate the part that way.

Careful: the part of the area is not the same as the part of the perimeter.

INTERACTIVE · THE KITE IN THE RECTANGLE

Fold out the four triangles. They fill the rest of the rectangle: the kite is always exactly half.

EXAMPLE 3 · A GARDEN

A rectangular garden is 20 m long and 12 m wide. In it there is a triangular flower bed with a base of 8 m and a height of 6 m. What part is flower bed?

Garden: 240 m². Bed: $\tfrac12 \cdot 8 \cdot 6 = 24$ m². Part: $\tfrac{24}{240} = \tfrac{1}{10}$.

$10\,\%$ of the garden is flower bed.

EXAMPLE 4 · AN AQUARIUM

An aquarium (80 cm long, 40 cm wide, 50 cm high) is filled to a height of 30 cm. What part is full? How many litres are missing up to the rim?

Same base, so part $= \tfrac{30}{50} = \tfrac35 = 60\,\%$. 20 cm of height are missing: $80 \cdot 40 \cdot 20 = 64\,000$ cm³.

$60\,\%$ full, 64 ℓ are missing.

EXERCISES

What part of a square is the triangle cut off by a diagonal? And what part is a triangle that has one side of the square as its base and its apex on the opposite side?

Both times $\tfrac12$: same base, same height as the square, triangle formula with $\tfrac12$.

The flag of the Czech Republic is a rectangle 3 : 2 (for example 90 cm by 60 cm). On the left there is a blue triangle whose tip lies at the centre of the flag. What part is blue?

Triangle: base 60 cm (left edge), height 45 cm (up to the centre). $A = \tfrac12 \cdot 60 \cdot 45 = 1350$ cm². Flag: 5400 cm². Part: $\tfrac14 = 25\,\%$.

A cube with a 6 cm edge is built from 27 small cubes. The outer faces are painted red. What fraction of the small cubes has no red face at all?

Only the cube right in the middle: $\tfrac{1}{27} \approx 3.7\,\%$.

A glass is a hexagonal prism. It is filled with juice to $\tfrac23$ of its height. Then someone drinks half of the juice. What part of the glass is still filled now?

$\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac13$ of the glass, about $33\,\%$.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

Connecting with chapter 2.1: a dice was thrown 60 times: 1 → 8, 2 → 12, 3 → 9, 4 → 11, 5 → 10, 6 → 10. Draw a pie chart of the relative frequencies.

One throw stands for $6^\circ$: $48^\circ$, $72^\circ$, $54^\circ$, $66^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $60^\circ$. Together $360^\circ$.

Connecting with chapter 3.3: a trapezium is drawn in a square with 10 cm sides: the whole side at the bottom, 4 cm at the top, as high as the square. What part is the trapezium?

$A = \tfrac12 \cdot (10 + 4) \cdot 10 = 70$ cm². Part $\tfrac{70}{100} = 70\,\%$.

A bar of chocolate has 24 pieces. Anna eats $\tfrac14$, Ben $\tfrac13$ of the rest, Cleo half of what is left then. What part of the bar is left over?

Anna 6, rest 18. Ben 6, rest 12. Cleo 6, rest 6. Left over: $\tfrac{6}{24} = \tfrac14$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

The first pie chart

Today pie charts are everywhere: in newspapers, presentations, school books. The oldest known pie chart is by the Scot William Playfair. He published it in 1801 in his book “The Statistical Breviary” in London.

It showed what part of the Ottoman Empire of the time lay in Asia, in Europe and in Africa. The slices were coloured by hand. Playfair also invented the bar chart and the line graph. For a long time his circles hardly attracted attention. Only later did they become famous.

Think about it: many experts today think that small differences are hard to see in a pie chart. Compare: in the widget above, do you see the difference between 28% and 32% better in the circle or in the table?

Source: William Playfair, “The Statistical Breviary”, London 1801 (printed by T. Bensley for J. Wallis).
