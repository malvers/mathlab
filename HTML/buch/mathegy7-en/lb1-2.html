Circle theorems

Draw a semicircle and put a point on it. Join it to the ends of the diameter: the angle is a right angle. Always! Thales of Miletus knew this more than 2500 years ago. In this chapter you get to know such circle theorems. And you learn what a theorem actually is and how to prove it.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Thales’ theorem

In the set-square experiment a semicircle appears over the line segment between the pins. Every point where a right angle sits lies on this semicircle. And conversely: every point of the semicircle forms a right angle with the pins. Is that a coincidence? No, it is a theorem.

A definition fixes what a word means: “A triangle with a right angle is called right-angled.” There is nothing to prove about it. A theorem, on the other hand, is a statement whose truth has to be proved. It has a hypothesis (“if …”) and a conclusion (“then …”).

KEY POINT · THALES’ THEOREM

Thales’ theorem: If a point $C$ lies on the circle with diameter $\overline{AB}$ (and $C$ is neither $A$ nor $B$), then the angle $\angle ACB$ is a right angle.

Converse: If the triangle $ABC$ has a right angle at $C$, then $C$ lies on the circle with diameter $\overline{AB}$. This circle is called the Thales circle over $\overline{AB}$.

Both together: the angle at $C$ is a right angle if and only if $C$ lies on the Thales circle over $\overline{AB}$.

Converse: You swap the hypothesis and the conclusion. The result can be true or false. It has to be proved separately.

“If and only if” is only said when a theorem and its converse are true.

INTERACTIVE · THALES AND HIS PROOF

Drag $C$ round the circle, below the diameter too. Then tap “Show the idea of the proof”.

PROOF OF THALES’ THEOREM

Hypothesis: $\overline{AB}$ is a diameter of the circle with centre $M$, and $C$ lies on the circle.   Conclusion: $\angle ACB = 90^\circ$.

  1. $\overline{MA} = \overline{MB} = \overline{MC} = r$, because all three are radii.
  2. So the triangles $AMC$ and $MBC$ are isosceles. Their base angles are equal: $\angle MAC = \angle MCA = \alpha$ and $\angle MBC = \angle MCB = \beta$.
  3. The angle at $C$ is made up of two parts: $\gamma = \alpha + \beta$.
  4. Angle sum in the triangle $ABC$: $\alpha + \beta + (\alpha + \beta) = 180^\circ$, so $2 \cdot (\alpha + \beta) = 180^\circ$.
  5. It follows that $\gamma = \alpha + \beta = 90^\circ$. That is what we had to show.

This is a direct proof: from the hypothesis you go to the conclusion in small steps, and every step has a reason.

EXAMPLE 1 · CONSTRUCTING A RIGHT-ANGLED TRIANGLE

Construct a triangle with $c = 6$ cm, $\gamma = 90^\circ$ and $a = 3$ cm.

Draw $\overline{AB}$ 6 cm long and the Thales circle over $\overline{AB}$, i.e. the circle round the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$ with radius 3 cm.

The circle with centre $B$ and radius 3 cm cuts the Thales circle at $C$. By Thales’ theorem the angle at $C$ is a right angle.

You measure $b \approx 5.2$ cm and $\alpha = 30^\circ$.

EXAMPLE 2 · NOT EVERY CONVERSE IS TRUE

Theorem: “If a number is divisible by 4, then it is even.” Form the converse. Is it true?

Converse: “If a number is even, then it is divisible by 4.”

The number 6 is even but not divisible by 4. The converse is false. With Thales’ theorem, on the other hand, the theorem and its converse are both true.

Theorem or definition? a) A quadrilateral with four right angles is called a rectangle. b) In every triangle the angle sum is 180°. c) A chord through the centre is called a diameter. d) In an isosceles triangle the base angles are equal.

a) definition   b) theorem   c) definition   d) theorem

Write as “if – then” and name the hypothesis and the conclusion: “In a rectangle the diagonals are equal in length.”

If a quadrilateral is a rectangle, then its diagonals are equal in length. Hypothesis: the quadrilateral is a rectangle. Conclusion: the diagonals are equal in length.

Use the Thales circle to construct a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse $c = 7$ cm and the side $a = 4$ cm. Measure $b$.

$\overline{AB} = 7$ cm, Thales circle with radius 3.5 cm, circle with centre $B$ and radius 4 cm. You measure $b \approx 5.7$ cm.

Form the converse and check it: a) If a quadrilateral is a square, then all four sides are equal in length. b) If two angles are vertically opposite angles, then they are equal.

a) “If all four sides are equal in length, then it is a square.” False: a rhombus with 60° is not a square. b) “If two angles are equal, then they are vertically opposite angles.” False: two 30° angles in different places are not vertically opposite angles.

In the proof $C$ lies above $\overline{AB}$. Why does it hold in the same way if $C$ lies below? Why does the hypothesis rule out $C = A$?

The proof only uses the equal radii and the angle sum, not whether $C$ lies above or below. For $C = A$ there is no triangle and no angle at $C$.

2Angles in a circle

A footballer shoots at goal. From some places she sees the goal at a large angle, from others at a small one. All the points from which the goal appears equally wide lie on an arc of a circle through the two posts. That is what the inscribed angle theorem says.

A chord $\overline{AB}$ divides the circle into two arcs. An inscribed angle (angle at the circumference) over $\overline{AB}$ has its vertex $P$ on the circle; its arms pass through $A$ and $B$. The central angle (angle at the centre) has its vertex at the centre $M$. A quadrilateral whose corners all lie on a circle is called a cyclic quadrilateral.

KEY POINT · ANGLES IN A CIRCLE

Central angle theorem: The central angle over an arc is twice as big as every inscribed angle over the same arc.

Inscribed angle theorem: All inscribed angles over the same arc are equal.

Opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral: In every cyclic quadrilateral opposite angles add up to $180^\circ$: $\alpha + \gamma = \beta + \delta = 180^\circ$.

Thales’ theorem is a special case: over a semicircle the central angle is $180^\circ$, so every inscribed angle is $90^\circ$.

Which arc? An inscribed angle belongs to the arc that lies between its arms, i.e. the arc opposite $P$.

Distinguishing cases: To prove the central angle theorem you have to distinguish whether $M$ lies between the arms, on one arm or outside. A complete proof deals with all the cases.

INTERACTIVE · INSCRIBED ANGLE AND CENTRAL ANGLE

Drag $P$ along its arc and watch the measurements. Then drag $A$ or $B$. At the top you can switch to the cyclic quadrilateral.

KEY POINT · COUNTEREXAMPLE AND EXAMPLE

You disprove a universal statement (“Every quadrilateral is a cyclic quadrilateral.”) with a single counterexample.

You confirm an existence statement (“There is a cyclic quadrilateral that is not a rectangle.”) with a single example.

A universal statement cannot be confirmed by examples, however many there are. For that you need a proof.

EXAMPLE 3 · FROM THE CENTRAL ANGLE TO THE INSCRIBED ANGLE

The central angle over the chord $\overline{AB}$ is $130^\circ$. How big is an inscribed angle on the large arc, and how big is one on the small arc?

Large arc: $130^\circ \div 2 = 65^\circ$.

A point on the large arc and one on the small arc form a cyclic quadrilateral with $A$ and $B$. On the small arc: $180^\circ - 65^\circ = 115^\circ$.

EXAMPLE 4 · A CYCLIC QUADRILATERAL

In a cyclic quadrilateral $\alpha = 70^\circ$ and $\beta = 105^\circ$. Calculate $\gamma$ and $\delta$.

$\gamma = 180^\circ - 70^\circ = 110^\circ$, $\delta = 180^\circ - 105^\circ = 75^\circ$.

Check: $70^\circ + 105^\circ + 110^\circ + 75^\circ = 360^\circ$. $\gamma = 110^\circ$, $\delta = 75^\circ$

EXERCISES

An inscribed angle over $\overline{AB}$ is $35^\circ$. How big is the central angle over the same arc? How big is a second inscribed angle over this arc?

Central angle $70^\circ$, every inscribed angle over the same arc $35^\circ$.

In a cyclic quadrilateral $\alpha = 80^\circ$ and $\beta = 95^\circ$. Calculate $\gamma$ and $\delta$.

$\gamma = 100^\circ$, $\delta = 85^\circ$

Disprove each with a counterexample: a) Every quadrilateral is a cyclic quadrilateral. b) Every cyclic quadrilateral has a right angle. c) Every rhombus is a cyclic quadrilateral.

a) and c) A rhombus with the angles 60°, 120°, 60°, 120°: opposite angles are equal, $60^\circ + 60^\circ \ne 180^\circ$. b) An isosceles trapezium with the angles 70°, 70°, 110°, 110°: opposite angles give $70^\circ + 110^\circ = 180^\circ$, so it is a cyclic quadrilateral, but it has no right angle.

Confirm with an example: there is a cyclic quadrilateral that is not a rectangle. Draw it in a circle.

For example an isosceles trapezium: draw two parallel chords of different lengths and join their ends. The diameter perpendicular to them is the axis of symmetry.

A football goal is 7.32 m wide. Where do all the players stand who see the goal at exactly a right angle? Draw to the scale 1 : 100.

On the Thales circle over the goal line, i.e. on the semicircle with radius 3.66 m round the middle of the goal, in front of the goal. In the drawing this is a semicircle with a radius of 3.66 cm.

INTERACTIVE · THE CYCLIC QUADRILATERAL

Drag the corners round the circle. With “Show the idea of the proof” the four radii appear.

The circumcentre of a triangle lies on the side $\overline{AB}$. How big is $\gamma$? Give a reason.

Then $\overline{AB}$ is a diameter of the circumcircle, and $C$ lies on it. By Thales’ theorem $\gamma = 90^\circ$.

Connecting with chapter 1.1: Use the converse of Thales’ theorem to explain why the circumcentre of a right-angled triangle lies at the midpoint of the hypotenuse.

By the converse, $C$ lies on the circle with diameter $\overline{AB}$. Its centre, the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$, is the same distance from $A$, $B$ and $C$. That is the circumcentre.

A regular hexagon $ABCDEF$ lies in a circle. How big is the central angle over one side, and how big is an interior angle? Check the theorem on cyclic quadrilaterals with the quadrilateral $ABCD$.

Central angle $360^\circ \div 6 = 60^\circ$, interior angle $120^\circ$. In the quadrilateral $ABCD$ the angles at $B$ and $C$ are $120^\circ$ each, at $A$ and $D$ $60^\circ$ each. $60^\circ + 120^\circ = 180^\circ$.

Prove the theorem on the opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral for the case in which $M$ lies inside the quadrilateral. Hint: draw the four radii and call the base angles $x$, $y$, $z$ and $w$.

The radii form four isosceles triangles with the base angles $x$ (at $\overline{AB}$), $y$ ($\overline{BC}$), $z$ ($\overline{CD}$), $w$ ($\overline{DA}$). Then $\alpha = w + x$, $\beta = x + y$, $\gamma = y + z$, $\delta = z + w$. So $\alpha + \gamma = w + x + y + z = \beta + \delta$. Both sums together are $360^\circ$, so each is $180^\circ$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Thales of Miletus

Thales lived around 600 BC in Miletus, a Greek trading city on the coast of what is now Turkey. He is regarded as one of the first people who wanted to explain the world without gods and myths, only by observing and thinking.

The historian Herodotus reports that Thales predicted an eclipse of the sun. It interrupted a battle between the Lydians and the Medes, and the warriors then made peace. Astronomers today have recalculated it: on 28 May 585 BC there was a total eclipse of the sun there.

In Egypt Thales is said to have found the height of a pyramid without climbing it: he waited until his own shadow was as long as he was tall. At that moment the shadow of the pyramid was also as long as the pyramid was high. And he is said to have been the first to draw a right-angled triangle in a semicircle and to have sacrificed an ox for joy. Nobody knows exactly whether this is true. Others attributed the same discovery to Pythagoras.

Sources: Herodotus, Histories I, 74. Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers I, 24 (after Pamphile) and I, 27 (after Hieronymus).

LOOKING BACKTHE ESSENTIALS AT A GLANCE

THEOREM AND DEFINITION

Definition: fixes a meaning. Theorem: is proved; “if” hypothesis, “then” conclusion.

Converse: swap the two, check again

THALES

$C$ on the circle over the diameter $\overline{AB}$ if and only if $\angle ACB = 90^\circ$

Thales circle: radius $\tfrac12 \overline{AB}$

INSCRIBED ANGLE

Central angle $= 2 \cdot$ inscribed angle · all inscribed angles over one arc are equal

$130^\circ \to 65^\circ$

CYCLIC QUADRILATERAL

$\alpha + \gamma = \beta + \delta = 180^\circ$

$\alpha = 70^\circ \Rightarrow \gamma = 110^\circ$

COUNTEREXAMPLE

A counterexample disproves a universal statement; an example confirms an existence statement.

Rhombus with 60°: not a cyclic quadrilateral
