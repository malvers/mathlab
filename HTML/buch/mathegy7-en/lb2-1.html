Rational numbers

Minus eight degrees in the Ore Mountains, minus twenty euros in your bank account, a submarine fifty metres below sea level: many things in everyday life need numbers less than zero. You get to know the negative numbers, put them in order on the number line and see how all the numbers you know belong to one big family: the rational numbers.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Negative numbers and the number line

On a thermometer, zero is in the middle. Above it, it is warm; below it, cold. In just the same way we extend the number ray beyond zero to the left. To the left of zero are the negative numbers: −1, −2, −3 and all the numbers in between.

For every number to the right of zero there is a number that lies just as far to the left of zero. The two are opposite numbers, for example 3 and −3. How far a number is from zero is given by its absolute value. The absolute value is never negative.

KEY POINT · RATIONAL NUMBERS ON THE NUMBER LINE

Numbers greater than zero are called positive, numbers less than zero negative. Zero is neither positive nor negative.

The numbers …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, … are called integers. All the numbers that can be written as a fraction with or without a minus sign are called rational numbers, for example $-\tfrac34$, $-2.5$, $0$ or $7$.

Ordering: Of two numbers, the one further to the right is the bigger one. So $-5 < -2$.

Opposite number of $a$ is $-a$: reflected at zero. Absolute value $|a|$: the distance from zero, for example $|-4| = 4$ and $|4| = 4$.

Careful: 5 is bigger than 2, but −5 is smaller than −2. At −5 °C it is colder than at −2 °C.

Two meanings: The minus in −3 is a sign. The minus in $7 - 3$ is an operation sign. More on this in chapter 2.2.

UNIT 24 LESSONS

2Number sets

In the sorting machine some cards fit into several groups: 4 is a natural number, but also an integer and a fraction, namely $\tfrac41$. The numbers form ranges that lie inside one another. With sets this can be described exactly.

A set brings things together, its elements. You write it in curly brackets: $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. If you draw sets as areas, you get a set diagram.

KEY POINT · NUMBER SETS

$\mathbb{N}$ natural numbers: 1, 2, 3, … (many books include 0)   ·   $\mathbb{Z}$ integers: …, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …

$\mathbb{Q}^+$ fractions: fractions and decimals without a minus sign   ·   $\mathbb{Q}$ rational numbers: all of them together, with and without a minus sign

$-3 \in \mathbb{Z}$: −3 is an element of $\mathbb{Z}$.   $-3 \notin \mathbb{N}$: −3 is not an element of $\mathbb{N}$.

$A \subset B$: $A$ is a subset of $B$; every element of $A$ is also in $B$. We have $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Q}^+ \subset \mathbb{Q}$.

$A \cap B$ intersection: in $A$ and in $B$ · $A \cup B$ union: in $A$ or in $B$ · $\emptyset$ empty set: with no elements

Memory aid: $\cup$ looks like a “u” for “union”; $\cap$ is the same shape upside down and stands for “and”.

Or means here: in one set, in the other or in both.

INTERACTIVE · WHERE DOES THE NUMBER LIVE?
EXAMPLE 3 · CLASSIFYING

Which number sets do $7$, $-4$, $2.5$ and $-\tfrac13$ belong to?

$7 \in \mathbb{N}$, so also $7 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}^+$ and $\mathbb{Q}$.   $-4 \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$.

$2.5 \in \mathbb{Q}^+$ and $\mathbb{Q}$. $-\tfrac13$ lies only in $\mathbb{Q}$.

EXAMPLE 4 · INTERSECTION AND UNION

$A = \{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\}$ and $B = \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$. Find $A \cap B$ and $A \cup B$.

0, 1 and 2 are in both sets. −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 and 3 are in at least one.

$A \cap B = \{0, 1, 2\}$ · $A \cup B = \{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$

Write as a set: a) all the integers that are greater than −4 and less than 2   b) all the natural numbers that are less than 1 (without 0)

Describe in words: a) $\mathbb{Z} \cap \mathbb{Q}^+$   b) the numbers that are in $\mathbb{Z}$ but not in $\mathbb{N}$. Give three examples of each.

a) The integers without a minus sign, i.e. the natural numbers: 1, 5, 12. b) The negative integers (and, depending on the book, 0): −1, −7, −20.

Outlook: We are looking for a number $x$ with $x \cdot x = 2$. Show that it lies between 1.4 and 1.5. Find a decimal with two decimal places whose square is even closer to 2.

$1.4 \cdot 1.4 = 1.96 < 2$ and $1.5 \cdot 1.5 = 2.25 > 2$. $1.41 \cdot 1.41 = 1.9881$, $1.42 \cdot 1.42 = 2.0164$. There is no rational number whose square is exactly 2. More on this in chapter 2.2.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

A seagull flies 12 m above the water; a diver is 18 m below it. Give both heights as numbers. How far apart are they?

Seagull: 12 m, diver: −18 m. Distance: $12 + 18 = 30$ m.

Lena has a bank balance of −€45, Tim of −€54. Who has more money? Who has to pay in more to get to €0?

$-45 > -54$: Lena has more (less debt). Tim has to pay in more, namely €54.

Connecting with Year 6: Put in order: $-\tfrac23$, $-\tfrac34$, $-0.7$. Hint: write them all as decimals.

$-\tfrac23 \approx -0.667$, $-\tfrac34 = -0.75$. So $-\tfrac34 < -0.7 < -\tfrac23$.

Connecting with chapter 1.2: Is the statement “If $|a| > |b|$, then $a > b$” true? Disprove it with a counterexample if necessary.

False. Counterexample: $a = -5$, $b = 1$. We have $|-5| = 5 > 1 = |1|$, but $-5 < 1$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Red and black rods

In China, more than 2000 years ago, people calculated with small rods that they laid out on a counting board. The book Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art contains rules for calculating with gains and losses. In the year 263 the scholar Liu Hui wrote about it: for positive numbers you take red rods, for negative ones black. Anyone without coloured rods laid the negative ones at a slant.

In India the astronomer Brahmagupta wrote down rules of arithmetic for fortunes and debts in the year 628; that is what he called positive and negative numbers. For example: a debt times a debt gives a fortune. In chapter 2.2 you see why this makes sense. In Europe negative numbers only became a matter of course much later.

Sources: Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art (Jiuzhang suanshu), chapter 8, with the commentary by Liu Hui (263). Brahmagupta, Brahmasphutasiddhanta (628), chapter 18.
