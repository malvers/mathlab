UNIT 1CAS · 4 LESSONS

1Variables: name, value and meaning

For $n$ squares in a row you need $3n + 1$ matchsticks: one match on the far left, then three more for each square. For 10 squares you substitute $n = 10$: $3 \cdot 10 + 1 = 31$. For 100 squares it is $301$ matches. A single term answers all the questions at once.

A variable can play different roles. In the formula $A = a \cdot b$ it is a placeholder for any lengths. In the equation $3n + 1 = 31$, $n$ is an unknown that you are looking for. In a table of values, $n$ is a changing quantity that takes many values one after another.

KEY POINT · VARIABLES AND TERMS

A variable is a symbol, usually a letter, that stands for a number. It has a name ($n$), a value (for example $10$) and a meaning (the number of squares).

A term is a meaningful expression made of numbers, variables, operation signs and brackets, for example $3n + 1$ or $\tfrac{a + b}{2}$. An equation is not a term.

Substituting: you replace the variable everywhere by the same value and calculate.

Two terms are equivalent if they have the same value for every substitution, like $3n + 1$ and $4 + 3(n - 1)$.

Multiplication dot: between a number and a variable it is usually left out: $3n$ means $3 \cdot n$. Between two numbers never: $3 \cdot 4$ is not $34$.

CAS: with n:=10 the variable $n$ gets the value 10. From then on the calculator uses 10 everywhere until you delete the variable again. Don’t forget that, or strange results will puzzle you later.

EXAMPLE 1 · SETTING UP A TERM

A mobile phone contract costs €8 a month and €0.09 for every minute abroad. Set up a term for the monthly cost. What do 40 minutes cost?

Variable: $m$ (name), number of minutes abroad (meaning). Cost in euros: $8 + 0.09m$.

For $m = 40$: $8 + 0.09 \cdot 40 = 8 + 3.60 =$ €11.60

EXAMPLE 2 · EQUIVALENT OR NOT?

Are $2(n + 3)$ and $2n + 3$ equivalent?

Substitute $n = 1$: $2 \cdot 4 = 8$, but $2 + 3 = 5$. A single counterexample is enough: not equivalent.

Conversely, trying out values does not prove equivalence. For that you have to transform, which you will learn in unit 3.

2The structure of terms

In $2 + 3 \cdot 4$ you first calculate $3 \cdot 4$, then the addition. The operation that comes last gives the term its name: $2 + 3 \cdot 4$ is a sum. Its second summand $3 \cdot 4$ is itself a product. So terms are built from smaller terms, like a tree from branches.

In the calculation tree the numbers and variables are at the bottom. Above them are the operation signs in the order in which you calculate. At the very top is the last operation. When you substitute a value, the results travel from the bottom to the top.

KEY POINT · THE STRUCTURE OF A TERM

The operation carried out last determines the type of the term:

  • sum of summand and summand, difference of minuend and subtrahend
  • product of factor and factor, quotient of dividend and divisor
  • power of base and exponent

Order: brackets before powers before multiplication and division before addition and subtraction, otherwise from left to right.

In words: $3(x + 4)$ is “three times the sum of $x$ and $4$”, $3x + 4$ is “the sum of three times $x$ and $4$”. In words, the type of the term comes first.

Careful with powers: $-3^2 = -9$, because the power is calculated first. But $(-3)^2 = 9$.

INTERACTIVE · THE CALCULATION TREE

Choose a term and change $x$. The values travel from the bottom to the top. Tap a node in the tree to see its sub-term.

EXAMPLE 3 · NAMING TERMS

What type of term is it? a) $5 - 2(x - 1)$   b) $(2x - 1)^2$   c) $\tfrac{x + 6}{2}$

a) Difference with the minuend $5$ and the subtrahend $2(x - 1)$, which is itself a product.

b) Power with the base $2x - 1$ and the exponent $2$.   c) Quotient: the fraction bar is carried out last.

EXAMPLE 4 · FROM WORDS TO A TERM

Write as a term: a) the square of the difference of $x$ and $3$   b) the difference of the square of $x$ and $3$   c) half the sum of $a$ and $b$

a) $(x - 3)^2$   b) $x^2 - 3$   c) $\tfrac{a + b}{2}$

The first word gives away the last operation: “the square …” is a power, “the difference …” a difference.

3Transforming terms without aids

The number trick from the explorations as a term: $\big((x + 4) \cdot 2 - 6\big) \div 2 - x$. Transforming shows why the result is always $1$: $(2x + 8 - 6) \div 2 - x = (2x + 2) \div 2 - x = x + 1 - x = 1$. This holds for every number, without any trying out.

Transforming means replacing a term by an equivalent, often simpler term. The most important tools are collecting like terms and the distributive law, forwards as multiplying out and backwards as factorising.

KEY POINT · TRANSFORMING TERMS

Collecting like terms: only like terms (the same variables with the same exponents) are added: $19a - b - 17a + c - a + b = a + c$.

Multiplying out: $a(b + c) = ab + ac$, for example $3(4x - 2y) = 12x - 6y$. A minus in front of the bracket turns all the signs round: $-(x - 5) = -x + 5$.

Factorising: $ab + ac = a(b + c)$, for example $6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)$.

Multiplying sums: every term by every term: $(a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd$.

Check by substituting: substitute, for example, $x = 2$ into the old and the new term. If you get different results, you have made a mistake.

Like terms? $2x$ and $5x$ yes, $x$ and $x^2$ no, $xy$ and $yx$ yes, because $xy = yx$.

PICTURE · MULTIPLYING TWO SUMS

A rectangle with the sides $x + 3$ and $x + 2$ splits into four parts. Together: $(x + 3)(x + 2) = x^2 + 2x + 3x + 6 = x^2 + 5x + 6$.

EXAMPLE 5 · SIMPLIFYING

a) $19a - b - 17a + c - a + b$   b) $3x - 2(x - 4)$   c) $(x + 2)(x + 3) - x^2$

a) $19a - 17a - a = a$, $-b + b = 0$: $a + c$

b) $3x - 2x + 8 =$ $x + 8$   c) $x^2 + 3x + 2x + 6 - x^2 =$ $5x + 6$

EXAMPLE 6 · FACTORISING

Take out as large a factor as possible: a) $12x + 18$   b) $5ab - 10a$   c) $x^2 + 7x$

a) $6$ goes into both: $6(2x + 3)$   b) $5a(b - 2)$   c) $x(x + 7)$

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Letters instead of numbers

For a long time, calculation problems were written in words: “A thing and three things make twelve.” Little by little there were abbreviations for the number you were looking for, but given numbers were always written as digits. So every problem had to be solved separately.

The French lawyer and mathematician François Viète had a far-reaching idea in 1591: he used letters systematically for all quantities, both the unknown and the given ones. The unknowns got vowels (A, E, I, O, U), the known quantities consonants (B, C, D …). That made it possible to solve a whole family of problems at once, just as you describe all rows of matchsticks at once with $3n + 1$.

Our present-day habit of using $x$, $y$ and $z$ for unknowns and $a$, $b$, $c$ for known numbers, on the other hand, goes back to René Descartes (1637).

Sources: F. Viète: In artem analyticem isagoge. Tours 1591. R. Descartes: La Géométrie. Leiden 1637.
