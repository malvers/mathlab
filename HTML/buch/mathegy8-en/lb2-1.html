UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Random experiments, outcomes and events

When you roll a dice, you can get 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6. Those are the possible outcomes. “An even number comes up” is an event: it occurs when 2, 4 or 6 comes up. Nobody can predict which number will really come. That makes rolling a dice a random experiment.

Mathematicians write outcomes and events as sets in curly brackets. The set of all possible outcomes is called the sample space and is given the Greek letter $\Omega$ (omega).

KEY POINT · RANDOM EXPERIMENTS

A random experiment has several possible outcomes, and you cannot predict which one will occur. It can be repeated under the same conditions.

The sample space $\Omega$ contains all outcomes, for a dice $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$.

An event is a subset of $\Omega$, such as “even number”: $E = \{2, 4, 6\}$. It occurs when one of its outcomes comes up.

$\Omega$ itself is called the certain event, the empty set $\{\,\}$ the impossible event.

$4 \in E$ means “4 is an element of $E$”, $\;3 \notin E$ means “3 is not an element of $E$”.

Comma: in sets, numbers are separated by commas. German books use a semicolon instead, because there a comma is the decimal point: $\{1.5, 2\}$ is $\{1{,}5;\,2\}$ in German.

No chance: if you place a coin on the table heads up, the outcome is clear beforehand. That is not a random experiment.

2Combining events

On the spinner with the sectors 1 to 12, $A$ is “even number” and $B$ is “prime number”. “$A$ or $B$” means: an even number or a prime number comes up, or both. “$A$ and $B$” means: both at the same time. Only the 2 manages that, the only even prime number.

KEY POINT · COMBINATIONS

$A \cup B$ (union, “$A$ or $B$”): at least one of the two occurs.

$A \cap B$ (intersection, “$A$ and $B$”): both occur.

$A \setminus B$ (difference, “$A$ without $B$”): $A$ occurs, but $B$ does not.

$\overline{A}$ (complementary event, “not $A$”): all outcomes that are not in $A$.

In everyday life “or” often means “either … or”. In mathematics “or” includes “both”.

Memory aid: $\cup$ is like a cup that everything goes into. $\cap$ is like a roof over what both have in common.

Venn diagram: events as circles in a rectangle for $\Omega$. Where the circles overlap is $A \cap B$. Named after the Englishman John Venn.

INTERACTIVE · EVENTS AS SETS

Choose a template or put $A$ and $B$ together yourself. Then try out the combinations and watch which sectors belong to them.

3Frequency and probability

A drawing pin lands on its back or on its side. How likely is “back”? You cannot calculate that, because the drawing pin is not symmetrical. You have to try it out: throw it very often and count.

The absolute frequency says how often an event has occurred. More meaningful is the share of all trials, the relative frequency. With few trials it jumps wildly back and forth. With many trials it settles down around a fixed value. This value is called the probability of the event.

KEY POINT · FREQUENCY AND PROBABILITY

Absolute frequency $H_n(E)$: how often $E$ has occurred in $n$ trials.

Relative frequency $h_n(E) = \dfrac{H_n(E)}{n}$: the share, as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage.

With many trials the relative frequency stabilises. The value it fluctuates around is called the probability $P(E)$.

It is always true that $0 \le P(E) \le 1$, $\;P(\Omega) = 1$ and $P(\{\,\}) = 0$.

Symmetrical or not? For a coin, a dice and a spinner with equal sectors, all outcomes are equally likely. For a drawing pin, a Lego brick or a bottle cap, only trying it out helps.

$P$ comes from the Latin probabilitas, probability.

INTERACTIVE · THE RELATIVE FREQUENCY SETTLES DOWN

Choose coin or dice and carry out the experiment 1, 10, 100 or 1000 times. Where does the curve settle down?

EXAMPLE 5 · A LEGO BRICK

A Lego brick was thrown 400 times (sample values). It landed 236 times on its base with the studs on top, 132 times on a side and 32 times on its studs. Find the relative frequencies and estimate the probabilities.

$\tfrac{236}{400} = 0.59$, $\;\tfrac{132}{400} = 0.33$, $\;\tfrac{32}{400} = 0.08$. Together $1$.

Estimate: $P(\text{studs on top}) \approx 0.59$. More throws make the estimate more reliable.

EXAMPLE 6 · FAMOUS COIN TOSSES

In the 18th century the naturalist Buffon tossed a coin 4040 times and got heads 2048 times. Around 1900 the statistician Karl Pearson even tossed 24,000 times and got heads 12,012 times. Calculate the relative frequencies.

Buffon: $\tfrac{2048}{4040} \approx 0.507$. Pearson: $\tfrac{12{,}012}{24{,}000} \approx 0.5005$.

More tosses, closer to $0.5$. But exactly $0.5$ is a coincidence even with a great many tosses.

4Laplace experiments

With a fair dice, nothing favours a particular score. Each has the same probability, so $\tfrac16$ each. For “even number”, 3 of the 6 outcomes count: $P = \tfrac36 = \tfrac12$. Here you do not have to roll, here you can calculate.

If all outcomes are equally likely, the experiment is called a Laplace experiment. Then you simply count: favourable outcomes divided by possible outcomes. Careful with two dice: the totals 2 to 12 are not equally likely. What is equally likely are the 36 pairs of first and second dice.

KEY POINT · THE LAPLACE RULE

A random experiment in which all outcomes are equally likely is called a Laplace experiment. Then:

$P(E) = \dfrac{|E|}{|\Omega|} = \dfrac{\text{number of outcomes favourable to } E}{\text{number of all possible outcomes}}$

Complementary event: $P(\overline{E}) = 1 - P(E)$.

Pierre-Simon Laplace (1749–1827) described this rule in 1812 in his great work on probability theory, the “Théorie analytique des probabilités”.

Not a Laplace experiment: switch the spinner from unit 1 to “different sizes”. Then the size of the sectors counts, not their number.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Ten thousand coin tosses in a camp

During the Second World War the mathematician John Kerrich was arrested in Denmark and held in a camp in Jutland. He had a lot of time and used it for an experiment: he tossed a coin 10,000 times and wrote down every result.

After the first 100 tosses he had only 44 heads, the relative frequency was $0.44$. The longer he tossed, the calmer the curve became. At the end there were 5067 heads and 4933 tails, a relative frequency of $0.5067$. Just as you can see in the simulation: the relative frequency approaches the probability $0.5$, but it hardly ever hits it exactly.

After the war Kerrich published his tosses in a book. His numbers still turn up in statistics books today.

Source: J. E. Kerrich: An Experimental Introduction to the Theory of Probability. Copenhagen 1946.
