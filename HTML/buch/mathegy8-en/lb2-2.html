Multi-stage random experiments and counting

Rolling a dice twice, taking three gumballs from the machine, cracking a combination lock: many random experiments take place in several stages. With the tree diagram you keep track, with the path rules you calculate, with the product rule you count without writing everything down, and with random numbers you let the computer roll the dice for you.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Tree diagram and path rules

On the first reach into the bag, $P(\text{red}) = \tfrac35$. If the first ball was red, there are still 2 red and 2 blue ones in the bag: on the second reach, $P(\text{red}) = \tfrac24$. The tree diagram shows all possibilities stage by stage, and each branch carries its probability.

Many random experiments can be acted out as drawing from an urn. If you put the ball back, the probabilities stay the same at every stage, the stages are independent. If you do not put it back, the first draw changes the contents of the urn, the stages are dependent.

KEY POINT · PATH RULES

Product rule: the probability of a path is the product of the probabilities on its branches: $P(\text{red, red}) = \tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac24 = \tfrac{3}{10}$.

Sum rule: the probability of an event is the sum of the probabilities of its paths: $P(\text{same colour}) = \tfrac{3}{10} + \tfrac{1}{10} = \tfrac25$.

At every branching point the branches add up to $1$.

The dice remembers nothing: even after five rolls without a six, a six is not more likely on the sixth roll. Rolls are independent.

Shortcut: for “at least once”, the complementary event “not at all” helps. It often has just a single path.

INTERACTIVE · THE TREE DIAGRAM WORKSHOP

Set the balls in the urn, choose with or without replacement and an event. The matching paths light up, the calculation is shown below.

EXAMPLE 1 · WITHOUT REPLACEMENT

Bag with 3 red and 2 blue gumballs, two are drawn without replacement. Calculate $P(\text{red, red})$, $P(\text{blue, blue})$ and $P(\text{different colours})$.

$P(\text{red, red}) = \tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac24 = \tfrac{3}{10}$, $\;P(\text{blue, blue}) = \tfrac25 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac{1}{10}$

Same colours: $\tfrac{4}{10}$. So $P(\text{different}) = 1 - \tfrac25 = \tfrac35$. Different colours are more likely.

EXAMPLE 2 · AT LEAST ONE SIX

A dice is rolled twice. What is the probability of at least one six?

Complementary event “no six”: only one path, $\tfrac56 \cdot \tfrac56 = \tfrac{25}{36}$.

$P(\text{at least one six}) = 1 - \tfrac{25}{36} = \tfrac{11}{36}$

EXERCISES

A coin is tossed three times. Draw the tree diagram. Calculate $P(\text{three heads})$ and $P(\text{exactly two heads})$.

Each of the 8 paths has $\tfrac18$. Three heads: $\tfrac18$. Exactly two: $HHT$, $HTH$, $THH$, so $\tfrac38$.

An urn contains 4 red balls and 1 white ball. Two balls are drawn with replacement. Calculate $P(\text{red, red})$, $P(\text{both white})$ and $P(\text{exactly one white})$.

$\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac45 = \tfrac{16}{25}$, $\;\tfrac15 \cdot \tfrac15 = \tfrac{1}{25}$, $\;2 \cdot \tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac15 = \tfrac{8}{25}$

The same urn, but without replacement. Calculate the three probabilities again. Why is $P(\text{both white})$ now $0$?

$\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac34 = \tfrac35$; both white is impossible, there is only one white ball; exactly one white: $\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac14 + \tfrac15 \cdot \tfrac44 = \tfrac25$.

In a game you spin a spinner twice; on each spin you win with probability $\tfrac14$. What is the probability of at least one win?

$1 - \tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac34 = 1 - \tfrac{9}{16} = \tfrac{7}{16}$

A basketball player scores 70% of her free throws. She shoots three times. Calculate $P(\text{three hits})$ and $P(\text{at least one hit})$. Which assumption is hidden in your calculation?

$0.7^3 = 0.343$ and $1 - 0.3^3 = 0.973$. Assumption: the throws are independent, nerves or tiredness play no part.

2Counting and simulating

With the combination lock there are 10 possibilities for the first wheel and another 10 for each further one. Together that makes $10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 = 10{,}000$ codes. At 2 seconds per attempt, trying them all takes up to $20{,}000$ seconds, more than five and a half hours. You do not have to write the codes down, you multiply.

When drawing from an urn it matters whether you put the balls back and whether the order counts. Without replacement the choice gets smaller with every draw. If the order does not count, outcomes that differ only in their order merge into one.

KEY POINT · COUNTING WITH THE PRODUCT RULE

If a process has $k$ stages with $n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_k$ possibilities, there are $n_1 \cdot n_2 \cdot \ldots \cdot n_k$ possibilities altogether.

With replacement, order counts: $k$ times from $n$ balls gives $n \cdot n \cdot \ldots \cdot n = n^k$.

Without replacement, order counts: $n \cdot (n - 1) \cdot (n - 2) \cdot \ldots$ with $k$ factors.

If the order does not count, you divide by the number of possible orders: 2 out of 5 without order gives $\tfrac{5 \cdot 4}{2} = 10$.

METHOD · SIMULATING WITH RANDOM NUMBERS

A random experiment can be acted out with random numbers: dice → random number from 1 to 6, coin → 0 or 1. In the CAS, for example, randInt(1,6), in a spreadsheet =RANDBETWEEN(1,6). Many runs give a relative frequency that estimates the probability you are looking for.

Orders: 3 things can be arranged in $3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 6$ ways, 4 things in $4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 24$ ways. For short you write $4!$, read “4 factorial”.

Looking ahead: the tool below also shows names and formulae that you will only need later. For now, the list of all possibilities is what counts.

EXAMPLE 4 · SIMULATING

On average, how often do you have to roll until the first six comes up? Simulate with the random digits above.

Read the digits in order, skip 0, 7, 8 and 9 and count the rolls up to the first 6. Then start again.

Many runs give, on average, about 6 rolls. Single runs can take much longer, though.

EXERCISES

An ice-cream parlour has 8 flavours. How many possibilities are there for a cone with 2 scoops, a) if the order counts and flavours may be repeated, b) if they are to be two different flavours and the order does not matter?

a) $8 \cdot 8 = 64$   b) $\tfrac{8 \cdot 7}{2} = 28$

A password consists of 4 lower-case letters (26 letters). How many passwords are there? How many if the 10 digits are allowed as well?

$26^4 = 456{,}976$ and $36^4 = 1{,}679{,}616$. Even one more character makes a big difference: $26^5 = 11{,}881{,}376$.

In a race with 8 runners, gold, silver and bronze are awarded. How many different podiums are there?

$8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 = 336$

CAS In the German lottery “6 aus 49” (6 from 49), 6 numbers are drawn without replacement. How many possibilities are there if the order counts? When you fill in a ticket it does not count. Which number do you have to divide by?

$49 \cdot 48 \cdot 47 \cdot 46 \cdot 45 \cdot 44 = 10{,}068{,}347{,}520$. The 6 numbers drawn can be ordered in $6! = 720$ ways: $\tfrac{10{,}068{,}347{,}520}{720} = 13{,}983{,}816$ tickets.

Simulate with random digits: how likely is it that in a group of 5 people at least two have their birthday in the same month? Read pairs of digits and use only 01 to 12. Compare with the calculation $1 - \tfrac{12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8}{12^5}$.

The fraction is the probability that all five have their birthdays in different months.

Calculation: $1 - \tfrac{95{,}040}{248{,}832} \approx 0.62$. With 20 to 30 runs the simulation is usually already close. So more than every second group of five shares a birth month.

An urn contains 2 red and 3 green balls. Three balls are drawn with replacement. Calculate $P(\text{three times green})$ and $P(\text{at least once red})$.

$\left(\tfrac35\right)^3 = \tfrac{27}{125}$ and $1 - \tfrac{27}{125} = \tfrac{98}{125}$

Lena’s outfits from the explorations: 3 T-shirts, 2 pairs of trousers, 2 pairs of shoes. How many outfits are there? How many if the yellow T-shirt does not go with the red trousers?

$3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 12$. The combination yellow with red exists with both pairs of shoes, so 2 drop out: 10 outfits.

CAS The Chevalier de Méré’s bet (excursion): calculate the probability of at least one six in 4 rolls and that of at least one double six in 24 rolls of two dice.

$1 - \left(\tfrac56\right)^4 \approx 0.518$ and $1 - \left(\tfrac{35}{36}\right)^{24} \approx 0.491$. The first bet wins in the long run, the second narrowly loses.

Lena and Tom play “rock, paper, scissors”, and both choose at random. Draw a tree diagram with two stages. What is the probability that Lena wins, and that it is a draw?

9 equally likely paths with $\tfrac19$ each. Lena wins on 3 paths: $\tfrac13$. A draw on 3 paths: $\tfrac13$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

The Chevalier de Méré’s bet

In 17th-century Paris, the Chevalier de Méré liked to bet on dice. His favourite bet: in four rolls of one dice, at least one six comes up. With it he won money in the long run. Then he tried it with two dice. A double six is six times rarer than a six, so he thought: six times as many rolls, 24 instead of 4, give the same chance. But now he lost.

He asked the mathematician Blaise Pascal, who wrote about it to Pierre de Fermat in the summer of 1654. In their letters the two worked out what you calculated in exercise 13: the first bet wins with about $0.518$, the second only with about $0.491$. De Méré’s rule of thumb was wrong. The exchange of letters is regarded as the birth of probability theory.

Source: Correspondence between B. Pascal and P. de Fermat, July to October 1654, among others Pascal to Fermat of 29 July 1654.
