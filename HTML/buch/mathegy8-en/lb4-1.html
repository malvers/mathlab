Central dilation

A projector throws a small picture onto the wall in large, a torch turns a hand into a huge shadow, a photo becomes a poster. Behind all of them is the same mapping: the central dilation. You will learn to construct it with a centre and a scale factor, and discover its most important properties: angles stay the same, all lengths are stretched by the same factor.

1Constructing the central dilation

With the rubber band enlarger, the knot is always exactly halfway between $Z$ and the pencil. So the pencil is always twice as far from $Z$ as the knot, and in the same direction. The figure is stretched from $Z$ by the factor $2$.

This is how you construct every image point: draw a ray from $Z$ through the point $P$, measure the distance $\overline{ZP}$, multiply it by $k$ and mark the result off from $Z$. If $k$ is negative, you mark the distance off in the opposite direction, on the other side of $Z$. The centre itself always stays where it is.

KEY POINT · CENTRAL DILATION

You are given a centre $Z$ and a scale factor $k \neq 0$.

The image point $P'$ lies on the line through $Z$ and $P$, and $\overline{ZP'} = |k| \cdot \overline{ZP}$. For $k > 0$, $P'$ lies on the same side of $Z$ as $P$, for $k < 0$ on the other side.

$|k| > 1$: enlargement · $|k| < 1$: reduction · $k = -1$: point reflection in $Z$.

Construction: rays from $Z$ through all the vertices, mark off the distances times $|k|$, join the image points.

Geometry software: in GeoGebra the command Dilate(Polygon, k, Z) does the whole construction. Then drag the points and watch what stays the same.

A bar: $\overline{ZP}$ is the line segment from $Z$ to $P$. We write its length in the same way.

INTERACTIVE · STRETCHING FROM Z

Change $k$, negative values too. Drag the centre $Z$ and the vertices of the triangle. The table shows lengths, angles and areas.

EXAMPLE 1 · IN THE COORDINATE SYSTEM

Stretch the triangle $A(2, 1)$, $B(4, 1)$, $C(3, 3)$ from the origin $Z(0, 0)$ with $k = 2$ and with $k = 0.5$.

If $Z$ is at the origin, you simply multiply both coordinates by $k$.

$k = 2$: $A'(4, 2)$, $B'(8, 2)$, $C'(6, 6)$. $\;k = 0.5$: $A'(1, 0.5)$, $B'(2, 0.5)$, $C'(1.5, 1.5)$

EXAMPLE 2 · LOOKING FOR THE CENTRE

A triangle $ABC$ and its image $A'B'C'$ are drawn. How do you find the centre and the scale factor?

Draw the lines through $A$ and $A'$ and through $B$ and $B'$. Their point of intersection is $Z$.

Then $|k| = \tfrac{\overline{ZA'}}{\overline{ZA}}$. If $A$ and $A'$ lie on different sides of $Z$, $k$ is negative.

2Properties of the central dilation

Look at the table of the dilation tool: for $k = 2$ every image segment is twice as long, but every angle has stayed the same size. The triangle becomes a larger triangle with exactly the same shape. The area, on the other hand, grows to four times the size.

KEY POINT · PROPERTIES

The central dilation maps lines to lines, keeps parallels parallel and preserves angles: lines are mapped to lines, every segment is parallel to its image, angles stay the same size.

Ratio of lengths: every image segment is $|k|$ times as long: $\overline{A'B'} = |k| \cdot \overline{AB}$.

Corresponding segments have the same ratio: $\dfrac{\overline{A'B'}}{\overline{AB}} = \dfrac{\overline{ZA'}}{\overline{ZA}} = |k|$.

Area: $A' = k^2 \cdot A$.

Intercept theorems: the proportions on two rays that start from $Z$ and are cut by two parallels are also called the intercept theorems.

Why $k^2$? A square with twice the side length is made up of four squares of the old size.

PICTURE · TWO RAYS, TWO PARALLELS

$A'B'$ is the image of $AB$ under a dilation from $Z$. That is why the two segments are parallel, and all the ratios agree. (Sketch, not to scale.)

EXAMPLE 3 · A PROPORTION

In the picture, $\overline{ZA} = 3$ cm, $\overline{AA'} = 4.5$ cm and $\overline{AB} = 2$ cm, $AB$ is parallel to $A'B'$. Calculate $k$ and $\overline{A'B'}$.

Careful: $\overline{ZA'} = 3 + 4.5 = 7.5$ cm. So $k = \tfrac{7.5}{3} = 2.5$.

$\overline{A'B'} = 2.5 \cdot 2\,\text{cm} = 5$ cm

EXAMPLE 4 · THE AREA

A triangle with an area of $6\,\text{cm}^2$ is stretched with $k = 3$, another one of the same size with $k = 0.5$. How big are the images?

$k = 3$: $9 \cdot 6 = 54\,\text{cm}^2$. $\;k = 0.5$: $0.25 \cdot 6 = 1.5\,\text{cm}^2$

A third of the length would be only a ninth of the area: areas react much more strongly than lengths.

EXERCISES

A rectangle of 4 cm by 3 cm is stretched with $k = 1.5$. Calculate the sides, the perimeter and the area of the original and the image. By what factor do the perimeter and the area change?

Image: 6 cm and 4.5 cm. The perimeter of 14 cm becomes 21 cm, factor $1.5$. The area of $12\,\text{cm}^2$ becomes $27\,\text{cm}^2$, factor $1.5^2 = 2.25$.

An angle of $37^\circ$ is centrally dilated with $k = 4$. How big is its image?

$37^\circ$, the central dilation preserves angles.

In a figure like the one in the picture, $\overline{ZA} = 4$ cm, $\overline{AA'} = 6$ cm and $\overline{AB} = 3$ cm. Calculate $\overline{A'B'}$.

$\overline{ZA'} = 10$ cm, $k = 2.5$, so $\overline{A'B'} = 7.5$ cm.

A photo is 10 cm × 15 cm. The long side is enlarged to 45 cm. How long does the short side become? How many times as large is the area of the poster?

$k = 3$: 30 cm. The area grows from $150\,\text{cm}^2$ to $1350\,\text{cm}^2$, to 9 times the size.

A projector stands 3 m in front of the wall and throws a picture 1.6 m wide. How wide does the picture become if it stands 4.5 m in front of the wall? What assumption do you make?

$k = \tfrac{4.5}{3} = 1.5$, so 2.4 m. Assumption: the light comes from one point in the lens, and the wall is perpendicular to the ray of light in the middle.

A model car at the scale 1 : 18 is 25 cm long. How long is the real car? The model’s windscreen has an area of $40\,\text{cm}^2$. How big is the real one?

$18 \cdot 25 = 450$ cm $= 4.5$ m. Area: $40 \cdot 18^2 = 12{,}960\,\text{cm}^2 \approx 1.3\,\text{m}^2$.

Connecting with learning area 3: the line $y = 2x + 1$ is stretched from the origin with $k = 3$. Give the equation of the image line. What do you notice?

A point $(x, y)$ of the line has the image $(3x, 3y)$.

$y = 2x + 1$ becomes $3y = 2 \cdot 3x + 3$, so for the image points $y = 2x + 3$. The gradient stays the same: image and original are parallel. The y-intercept is multiplied by $k$.

EXCURSIONTECHNOLOGY AND HISTORY

The pantograph

In 1603 a painter in Dillingen on the Danube told the young Jesuit Christoph Scheiner about a device with which you can enlarge and reduce drawings. He did not want to show it to him. Scheiner found the solution himself and built a pantograph out of four wooden rods, called a Storchschnabel (stork’s bill) in German.

The four rods form a movable parallelogram. One point is fixed on the table, it is the centre $Z$. With one pencil you trace the original, a second pencil draws the image at the same time. All three points always lie on a straight line, and the ratio of their distances stays fixed: a central dilation you can touch.

Only in 1631 did Scheiner describe his invention in a book of its own. Until the 20th century, draughtsmen, cartographers and engravers used it to copy plans and pictures in other sizes.

Source: C. Scheiner: Pantographice, seu ars delineandi. Rome 1631.
