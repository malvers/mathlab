Similarity

“You look similar to your sister” means something different from “the two triangles are similar”. In mathematics, similar means: the same shape, perhaps a different size. You will learn the main similarity theorem, solve problems with auxiliary figures, measure heights with shadows like Thales and work out why Gulliver gets so much to eat among the Lilliputians.

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Similar figures and the main similarity theorem

Two triangles are called similar if you can place one exactly on the other by translating, rotating or reflecting it and then stretching it. The shape stays the same, only the size may change. Because the central dilation preserves angles, similar figures have angles of the same size.

For triangles, even less is enough to recognise similarity: if two angles agree, then because of the angle sum of $180^\circ$ the third does too. For quadrilaterals that is not enough: a square and a long rectangle have nothing but right angles, but they are not similar.

KEY POINT · SIMILARITY

Two figures are similar if they can be mapped onto each other by translating, rotating, reflecting and a central dilation. Symbol: $\triangle ABC \sim \triangle A'B'C'$.

Similar figures have corresponding angles of the same size, and their corresponding sides are in the same ratio $k$.

Main similarity theorem: two triangles are similar if they agree in two angles.

Similar with $k = 1$ means congruent.

Mathematical and everyday language: in everyday life “similar” is vague, with similar colours there is no right or wrong. In mathematics it is laid down precisely when two figures are similar.

Corresponding sides lie opposite angles of the same size. Sort the sides by size, then the smallest, the middle and the largest ones belong together.

INTERACTIVE · BUILD A SIMILAR TRIANGLE
EXAMPLE 1 · SIMILAR OR NOT?

Triangle 1 has the angles $\alpha = 40^\circ$ and $\beta = 75^\circ$. Triangle 2 has $\beta' = 75^\circ$ and $\gamma' = 65^\circ$. Are the triangles similar?

In triangle 1, $\gamma = 180^\circ - 40^\circ - 75^\circ = 65^\circ$. Both triangles have a $75^\circ$ and a $65^\circ$ angle.

Similar by the main similarity theorem.

EXAMPLE 2 · CALCULATING SIDES

$\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF$ with $a = 4$ cm, $b = 5$ cm, $c = 6$ cm. The side $d$, which corresponds to $a$, is 6 cm long. Calculate $e$ and $f$.

$k = \tfrac{6}{4} = 1.5$. So $e = 1.5 \cdot 5 = 7.5$ cm and $f = 1.5 \cdot 6 = 9$ cm.

EXERCISES

Are the triangles similar? Two angles of each are given. a) $30^\circ, 60^\circ$ and $60^\circ, 90^\circ$   b) $45^\circ, 45^\circ$ and $45^\circ, 90^\circ$   c) $50^\circ, 70^\circ$ and $50^\circ, 50^\circ$

a) yes, both have $30^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $90^\circ$   b) yes, both $45^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $90^\circ$   c) no: $50^\circ$, $70^\circ$, $60^\circ$ against $50^\circ$, $50^\circ$, $80^\circ$

A triangle has the sides 3 cm, 4 cm and 5 cm. A similar triangle has 7.5 cm as its shortest side. Calculate its other sides.

$k = 2.5$: 10 cm and 12.5 cm.

Are all squares similar to each other? All rectangles? All equilateral triangles? All circles? Give reasons.

Squares: yes. Rectangles: no, the ratio of the sides can be different. Equilateral triangles: yes, all angles are $60^\circ$. Circles: yes, a dilation from the centre turns any circle into any other.

In a triangle $ABC$, $D$ lies on $\overline{AC}$ and $E$ on $\overline{BC}$, and $DE$ is parallel to $AB$. Justify that $\triangle DEC \sim \triangle ABC$. Calculate $\overline{DE}$ for $\overline{AB} = 9$ cm, $\overline{CD} = 4$ cm and $\overline{CA} = 6$ cm.

The angle at $C$ belongs to both triangles, and $\angle CDE = \angle CAB$ as corresponding angles at parallels. Two equal angles: similar. $k = \tfrac46 = \tfrac23$, so $\overline{DE} = 6$ cm.

A sheet of A4 paper measures 21.0 cm × 29.7 cm. If you halve it, you get A5 with 14.85 cm × 21.0 cm. Are A4 and A5 similar? Compare the ratios of the sides.

$\tfrac{29.7}{21.0} \approx 1.414$ and $\tfrac{21.0}{14.85} \approx 1.414$: yes. That is exactly why you can reduce an A4 sheet to A5 without distortion.

2Auxiliary figures and measuring heights

A stick 2 m tall casts a shadow 1.5 m long, a tree at the same time one 12 m long. The sun’s rays come in parallel, so the two shadow triangles are similar. The tree’s shadow is $\tfrac{12}{1.5} = 8$ times as long, so the tree is also 8 times as tall as the stick: 16 m.

Similarity also helps with constructions. Often the shape of a figure is easy to draw, but not the right size. Then you first draw an auxiliary figure with the right shape and afterwards stretch it to the right size.

KEY POINT · THE AUXILIARY FIGURE STRATEGY
  1. Draw an auxiliary figure with the right shape, for example from the given angles, in any size.
  2. Find the scale factor that gives it the required size.
  3. Stretch the auxiliary figure from a cleverly chosen centre.

Working forwards: what do I know, what follows from it? Working backwards: what do I need to reach the goal?

Forester’s triangle: an isosceles right-angled triangle held in front of the eye. If you sight the top of the tree exactly along the long side, you are standing as far from the tree as the tree rises above eye level.

Measure at the same time: the length of the shadows changes during the day. Only at the same time are the triangles similar.

INTERACTIVE · MEASURING HEIGHTS

Shadow method: set the height of the sun and calculate the height of the tree from the two shadows. Forester’s triangle: walk back until you can see the top.

EXAMPLE 3 · THE SHADOW OF A TOWER

A person 1.80 m tall casts a shadow 2.40 m long, a tower at the same time one 36 m long. How tall is the tower?

The tower’s shadow is $\tfrac{36}{2.4} = 15$ times as long. So the tower is $15 \cdot 1.8 = 27$ m tall.

EXAMPLE 4 · CONSTRUCTING WITH AN AUXILIARY FIGURE

Construct a triangle with $\alpha = 50^\circ$, $\beta = 70^\circ$ and a perimeter of 24 cm.

Auxiliary triangle: $c' = 4$ cm with the angles $50^\circ$ and $70^\circ$ at its ends. Measured, its perimeter is about 11.9 cm.

Scale factor $k = \tfrac{24}{11.9} \approx 2.02$. Stretch the auxiliary triangle from $A$: $c \approx 8.1$ cm, the angles stay the same.

EXERCISES

A flagpole casts a shadow 9 m long, a stick 1 m long at the same time one 0.75 m long. How tall is the pole?

$\tfrac{9}{0.75} = 12$, so 12 m.

Lena’s eye level is 1.50 m. With the forester’s triangle she sees the top of a tree exactly along the long side when she is standing 14 m away. How tall is the tree?

$14 + 1.5 = 15.5$ m

The thumb jump: hold your thumb at arm’s length 60 cm in front of your eyes and close your left and right eye in turn. The thumb jumps. Your eyes are 6 cm apart. If the thumb jumps by 30 m in front of a house, how far away is the house?

Draw the eyes, the thumb and the two points on the house. You will find two similar triangles with their tip at the thumb.

The ratio of arm length to distance between the eyes is $\tfrac{60}{6} = 10$. So the house is about $10 \cdot 30 = 300$ m away.

Use an auxiliary figure to construct a triangle with $\alpha = 40^\circ$, $\beta = 60^\circ$ and the height $h_c = 5$ cm. Describe your steps.

Draw an auxiliary triangle with any side $c'$ and the two angles. Measure its height $h_c'$ from $C'$ to $c'$. Then stretch from $C'$ with $k = \tfrac{5\,\text{cm}}{h_c'}$.

A school building casts a shadow 15 m long. Ben, who is 1.60 m tall, stands in the shadow so that the tip of his shadow coincides exactly with the tip of the building’s shadow. He is then 12 m from the building. How tall is the building?

Ben’s shadow is $15 - 12 = 3$ m long. The building is $\tfrac{15}{3} = 5$ times as tall as Ben: 8 m.

3Lengths, areas and volumes of similar objects

Gulliver is 12 times as tall as a Lilliputian. Is he therefore 12 times as heavy? No: he is 12 times as long, 12 times as wide and 12 times as thick. His volume is $12 \cdot 12 \cdot 12 = 1728$ times as large. For his shirt you need $12 \cdot 12 = 144$ times as much cloth.

KEY POINT · SIMILAR OBJECTS

If two figures or solids are similar with the factor $k$, then for corresponding

  • lengths (edges, perimeter, height): times $k$
  • areas (area, surface area): times $k^2$
  • volumes and, for the same material, the mass too: times $k^3$

Scale $1 : m$: reality is $m$ times as long as the picture, so $k = m$.

Why elephants have thick legs: if a mouse grew to the size of an elephant, its weight would rise with $k^3$, but the strength of its bones only with their cross-sectional area, that is with $k^2$.

Source on Gulliver: J. Swift: Gulliver’s Travels. London 1726, part I, chapter 3.

INTERACTIVE · k, k² AND k³

Choose a scale factor. The large cube consists of $k \cdot k \cdot k$ unit cubes. At $k = 12$ it becomes Gulliver.

EXAMPLE 5 · A MODEL HOUSE

A model of a cube house at the scale 1 : 50 has a floor area of $40\,\text{cm}^2$ and a volume of $600\,\text{cm}^3$. How big are the floor area and the volume of the real house?

$k = 50$: floor area $40 \cdot 2500 = 100{,}000\,\text{cm}^2 =$ $10\,\text{m}^2$.

Volume $600 \cdot 125{,}000 = 75{,}000{,}000\,\text{cm}^3 =$ $75\,\text{m}^3$.

EXAMPLE 6 · WHICH PIZZA?

A pizza with a diameter of 26 cm costs €7, one of 32 cm costs €9.50. Which is better value for its area?

The pizzas are similar with $k = \tfrac{32}{26} \approx 1.23$. The area grows with $k^2 \approx 1.51$, the price only by the factor $\tfrac{9.5}{7} \approx 1.36$.

The large pizza is better value.

EXERCISES

A cube with an edge length of 3 cm is enlarged with $k = 2$. Calculate the edge, the surface area and the volume before and after.

The edge of 3 cm becomes 6 cm. The surface area of $54\,\text{cm}^2$ becomes $216\,\text{cm}^2$ (times 4). The volume of $27\,\text{cm}^3$ becomes $216\,\text{cm}^3$ (times 8).

A town map has the scale 1 : 20,000. Two junctions are 6.5 cm apart on the map. How far is that in reality? A park measures $4\,\text{cm}^2$ on the map. How big is it really?

$6.5 \cdot 20{,}000 = 130{,}000$ cm $= 1.3$ km. Area: $4 \cdot 20{,}000^2\,\text{cm}^2 = 1.6$ billion $\text{cm}^2 = 160{,}000\,\text{m}^2$, that is 16 hectares.

In Jonathan Swift’s book, Gulliver gets food for 1724 Lilliputians every day, because he is 12 times as tall as they are. What number does your calculation give? Does it make sense to work out the food from the volume?

$12^3 = 1728$, four more than in Swift. The volume is not an entirely sensible measure: a large body loses relatively less heat through its skin and therefore needs less than $k^3$ times as much food.

A chocolate ball with a diameter of 3 cm weighs 12 g. How heavy is a ball made of the same chocolate with a diameter of 6 cm? How much foil do you need to wrap it, by comparison?

$k = 2$: mass $8 \cdot 12 = 96$ g. Foil: $k^2 = 4$, so four times as much.

In your mind, a mouse is enlarged to 50 times its length. By what factor do its weight and the cross-section of its bones grow? Why could this giant mouse not walk?

Weight times $50^3 = 125{,}000$, bone cross-section times $50^2 = 2500$. Every square centimetre of bone would have to carry 50 times as much as before. The legs would break.

Similar or not? a) two isosceles triangles, each with $40^\circ$ at the apex   b) any two right-angled triangles   c) rectangles of 2 × 3 and 4 × 6   d) rectangles of 2 × 3 and 3 × 4

a) yes, the base angles are $70^\circ$ each   b) not necessarily, only one angle is sure to agree   c) yes, $k = 2$   d) no

A tree casts a shadow 20 m long, a bush 1.20 m tall at the same time one 1.50 m long. How tall is the tree? How long is the shadow of a house 4 m tall at the same time?

Tree: $\tfrac{20}{1.5} \cdot 1.2 = 16$ m. House: $4 \cdot \tfrac{1.5}{1.2} = 5$ m of shadow.

The model of a church at the scale 1 : 200 is 30 cm tall. Its outer walls have an area of $0.25\,\text{m}^2$. How tall is the church, and how big is the area of its outer walls?

Height $200 \cdot 30$ cm $= 60$ m. Area $0.25 \cdot 200^2 = 10{,}000\,\text{m}^2$.

Connecting: a cube with an edge length of 1 cm is stretched with $k$. For which $k$ do the surface area (in $\text{cm}^2$) and the volume (in $\text{cm}^3$) of the image have the same numerical value?

Surface area $6k^2$, volume $k^3$. From $6k^2 = k^3$ it follows that $k = 6$: edge 6 cm, surface area $216\,\text{cm}^2$, volume $216\,\text{cm}^3$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Thales measures the pyramid

About 2600 years ago, Thales of Miletus is said to have measured the height of a pyramid in Egypt without climbing up it. There are two accounts of it, both written down many centuries later.

The older one comes from the philosopher Hieronymus and has come down to us through the historian Diogenes Laertius. It is quite simple: Thales waited for the moment when his own shadow was exactly as long as he was tall. At that moment every shadow is as long as its object is tall, the shadow of the pyramid too.

Plutarch tells it more geometrically: Thales set up a stick at the end of the pyramid’s shadow. The sun’s rays form two similar triangles, and the height of the pyramid is to the length of the stick as shadow is to shadow. That is exactly the shadow method from unit 2. Whether it really happened like that, nobody knows. But the idea still holds today.

Sources: Diogenes Laertius: Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers, book I, 27. Plutarch: The Dinner of the Seven Wise Men, chapter 2.
