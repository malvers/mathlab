Heuristic strategies

Some problems look impossible at first sight. Then a plan helps: what do I know? What do I want? What is missing in between? You will learn two strategies that you need again and again: working forwards and working backwards. On top of that come solution plans with sub-goals, aids such as a sketch and a table, and presenting your solution.

UNIT 12 LESSONS

1Working forwards and backwards

“I think of a number, multiply it by 3, add 5, halve the result and subtract 4. The result is 9.” From the start you would have to guess. From the end it is easy: $9 + 4 = 13$, $\;13 \cdot 2 = 26$, $\;26 - 5 = 21$, $\;21 \div 3 = 7$. The number was 7.

When you work forwards, you start from what is given and think about what follows from it. That works well when there are only a few possibilities, as with the jugs. When you work backwards, you start from the goal and ask what you need for it. That works well when the end is known, as with the number puzzle or the matchstick game.

KEY POINT · TWO STRATEGIES

Working forwards: start from what is given. What can I conclude from it? Step by step towards the goal.

Working backwards: start from what you are looking for. What do I need to get it? Where do I get that from? Until I arrive at what is given.

When you calculate backwards, every operation is replaced by its inverse, plus by minus and times by divided by, and the order is reversed.

Mazes: you often find the way more quickly from the goal, because fewer passages branch off from there.

Combining strategies: you often work from both sides and meet in the middle, at a sub-goal.

INTERACTIVE · THINK OF A NUMBER

Try out a number in the first box, or calculate backwards step by step. “New puzzle” always gives you new problems.

EXAMPLE 1 · BACKWARDS

Lena spends half her pocket money on a book, then €4 on an ice cream. Of what is left, she gives half to her brother. Now she has €6 left. How much did she have at the start?

Backwards: before sharing she had $6 \cdot 2 = 12$ euros, before the ice cream $12 + 4 = 16$ euros, before the book $16 \cdot 2 = 32$ euros.

€32. Check forwards: $32 \div 2 = 16$, $\;16 - 4 = 12$, $\;12 \div 2 = 6$.

EXAMPLE 2 · FORWARDS

With a 5-litre and a 3-litre jug, exactly 4 litres are to be measured out.

States (large jug, small jug): $(5, 0)$ fill, $(2, 3)$ pour across, $(2, 0)$ pour away, $(0, 2)$ pour across, $(5, 2)$ fill, $(4, 3)$ pour across until the small one is full.

After six steps there are 4 litres in the large jug.

EXERCISES

I think of a number, subtract 7, multiply by 4, add 10 and get 50. Which number did I think of?

Backwards: $50 - 10 = 40$, $\;40 \div 4 = 10$, $\;10 + 7 = 17$.

A snail sits at the bottom of a well 10 m deep. By day it climbs up 3 m, at night it slides back 2 m. On which day does it reach the top?

Careful: on the last day it does not slide back any more. Work from the end: where must it be on the morning of the last day?

On the morning of the last day it must be at least 7 m up. It is there after 7 days and nights. It reaches the top on the 8th day.

The matchstick game with 21 matches: each player takes 1, 2 or 3, whoever takes the last one wins. How many do you take if you go first? Justify it backwards.

Whoever faces 4 matches loses: whatever they take, the other player takes the rest. The same goes for 8, 12, 16 and 20. So take 1 match, then 20 are left. After that you make each of the other player’s moves up to 4.

With a 7-litre and a 4-litre bucket, exactly 5 litres are to be measured out. Find a way and write it down as a table.

(7-litre bucket, 4-litre bucket): $(0, 4)$, $(4, 0)$, $(4, 4)$, $(7, 1)$, $(0, 1)$, $(1, 0)$, $(1, 4)$, $(5, 0)$. There are other ways too.

UNIT 2PRESENTING · 2 LESSONS

2Solution plans and presenting

How many guests shake hands 45 times? A table helps: 2 guests give 1 handshake, 3 guests 3, 4 guests 6, 5 guests 10. Every new guest shakes hands with everyone who is already there. So it goes on: 15, 21, 28, 36, 45. There were 10 guests.

KEY POINT · A SOLUTION PLAN IN FOUR STEPS
  1. Understanding: what is given, what are you looking for? A sketch, your own words, an example.
  2. Planning: set sub-goals and choose a strategy: forwards, backwards, a table, an equation, a simple special case.
  3. Carrying out: calculate and check every step.
  4. Looking back: check, answer in a sentence. Is there another way?

A simpler case: if a problem is too big, first solve it for small numbers and look for a pattern, as with the handshakes.

In general: with $n$ guests each one shakes hands with $n - 1$ others, and every handshake is counted twice: $\tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2}$.

METHOD · PRESENTING SOLUTIONS
  1. Put the question so that everyone understands it.
  2. Show your sketch or table.
  3. Explain the idea in one sentence before you calculate.
  4. Only work through the important steps.
  5. Finish with an answer in a sentence and a check. Five minutes at most.
EXAMPLE 3 · HANDSHAKES WITH A FORMULA

Check with the formula $\tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ that with 10 guests 45 handshakes take place.

$\tfrac{10 \cdot 9}{2} = 45$. There were 10 guests. The table and the formula give the same.

EXAMPLE 4 · WITH SUB-GOALS

A swimming pool is 25 m long and 12 m wide and is to be filled with water to a depth of 1.8 m. A pump manages $30\,\text{m}^3$ per hour. How long does filling take?

Sub-goal 1, the volume: $25 \cdot 12 \cdot 1.8 = 540\,\text{m}^3$. Sub-goal 2, the time: $540 \div 30 = 18$.

Filling takes 18 hours.

EXERCISES

Hens and rabbits live on a farm, with 20 heads and 56 legs altogether. Solve it once with a table (trying systematically) and once with a system of equations. Which way do you prefer?

$h + k = 20$ and $2h + 4k = 56$: $k = 8$, $h = 12$. The table needs more rows but is easy to picture, the system of equations is quicker.

How many diagonals does a decagon have? Start with a triangle, a quadrilateral and a pentagon and draw up a table.

From each vertex, diagonals go to all the vertices except itself and its two neighbours.

Triangle 0, quadrilateral 2, pentagon 5, hexagon 9. In general $\tfrac{n(n - 3)}{2}$, for the decagon $\tfrac{10 \cdot 7}{2} = 35$.

Your train runs so that you arrive at your destination at 10:30. The journey takes 1 hour 50 minutes, it takes you 25 minutes to get to the station, and you want to be there 10 minutes before departure. When must you leave at the latest?

Backwards: departure $10{:}30 - 1{:}50 = 8{:}40$, at the station at $8{:}30$, set off at 8:05.

Present an exercise from this chapter in five minutes at most. Assess each other with the five points from the method box.

A good presentation puts the question clearly, shows a sketch or a table, names the idea before the calculation and ends with an answer in a sentence and a check.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

A price is first reduced by 20% and then by another €10. Now the item costs €30. What was the original price?

Backwards: before the second reduction it cost €40. That is 80% of the old price: $40 \div 0.8 = 50$ euros.

In a tournament every team plays every other team exactly once. There are 28 matches. How many teams take part?

As with the handshakes: $\tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2} = 28$ for $n = 8$.

Invent your own number puzzle “Think of a number …” with five steps that can be solved backwards. Swap it with your neighbour and solve each other’s.

Make sure that all the divisions work out. Check your puzzle yourself before you pass it on: once forwards, once backwards.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

George Pólya and how to solve it

The mathematician George Pólya (1887–1985) was born in Budapest, taught in Zurich and later at Stanford University in California. He was interested not only in what is in a proof, but above all in how you come up with the idea for it.

In 1945 his book How to Solve It appeared, in German “Schule des Denkens”. It was translated into more than 15 languages and sold more than a million copies. At the beginning are the four steps that you got to know in unit 2: understanding the problem, devising a plan, carrying out the plan, looking back. In addition there are many questions you can ask yourself along the way: do I know a related problem? Have I used all the data? Can I work backwards from what I am looking for?

Pólya believed that you can learn problem solving like swimming: by imitation and a lot of practice.

Source: G. Pólya: How to Solve It. Princeton University Press 1945. German: Schule des Denkens. Bern 1949.
