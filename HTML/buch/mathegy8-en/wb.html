1Random numbers and structurally equivalent experiments

A dice is a random number generator for the numbers 1 to 6. A spinner with 10 sectors gives the digits 0 to 9. The computer generates such numbers in seconds. With them you can act out any random experiment that has the same structure: a coin behaves like “the random digit is even”.

Even $\pi$ can be estimated in this way. A quarter circle with radius 1 has the area $\tfrac{\pi}{4} \approx 0.785$, the square around it the area 1. If a great many points fall into the square at random, about $\tfrac{\pi}{4}$ of them land in the quarter circle. Multiplied by 4, this gives an estimate for $\pi$.

KEY POINT · SIMULATING

For a random experiment you build a structurally equivalent experiment: the same number of outcomes with the same probabilities, but easier to carry out.

Random numbers come from dice, spinners, urns, tables and computers, in the CAS for example randInt(1,6), in a spreadsheet =RANDBETWEEN(1,6).

Many runs give a relative frequency that estimates the probability you are looking for. This is called the Monte Carlo method.

More runs make the estimate more reliable, but never completely exact.

Pseudo-randomness: a computer cannot roll dice. It calculates numbers from a formula that look so jumbled that they seem random.

Area of a circle: a circle with radius $r$ has the area $\pi r^2$. More on this in Year 9.

Use the random digits 0 to 9 to build structurally equivalent experiments: a) a coin   b) a dice   c) a basketball shot that scores 70% of the time

a) even digit: heads, odd: tails   b) use the digits 1 to 6, skip 0, 7, 8, 9

EXAMPLE 2 · ESTIMATING π

Of 1000 random points in the square, 791 lie in the quarter circle. Which approximation for $\pi$ do you get?

Share $\tfrac{791}{1000} = 0.791 \approx \tfrac{\pi}{4}$, so $\pi \approx 4 \cdot 0.791 = 3.164$.

Even the second decimal place is not right yet. For more accuracy you need a great many points.

2Simulations in practice

Three doors, behind one there is a car, behind each of the other two a goat. You choose a door. The host, who knows where the car is, opens another door with a goat and asks: “Do you want to switch?” Most people say: “It doesn’t matter, now it is fifty-fifty.” A simulation shows something quite different.

If you switch, you win exactly when your first choice was wrong. That happens in two out of three cases. If you stay, you only win if your first choice was right: in one case out of three. So switching doubles your chance of winning.

KEY POINT · WHY WE SIMULATE

If an experiment is too expensive, too dangerous, too slow or too hard to calculate, it is acted out on the computer.

A model lays down what matters and which probabilities hold. The result is only as good as the model.

Simulations give estimates. More runs make them more precise.

Simulations today: weather forecasts, the spread of diseases, flight simulators for pilot training, disaster control and traffic planning.

At the stadium: how long will the queues at the entrance get if every tenth person is checked thoroughly? Things like this are tried out beforehand in a simulation.

EXAMPLE 3 · MONTY HALL WITH A DICE

Simulate with a dice: 1 or 2 means “car behind door 1”, 3 or 4 “behind door 2”, 5 or 6 “behind door 3”. You always choose door 1. When do you win if you switch?

If the car is behind door 2 or 3 (the dice shows 3 to 6), the host opens the other goat door, and switching wins.

$P(\text{win by switching}) = \tfrac46 = \tfrac23$

EXAMPLE 4 · STICKER ALBUM

An album has 10 stickers. The simulation with 1000 albums gives about 29 packets on average. What does that mean for someone who buys 10 packets?

With 10 packets the album is almost never full. On average you need almost three times as many packets as stickers, and about one time in twenty even more than 50.

Swapping pays off. Towards the end only a few stickers are missing, and almost every packet brings a duplicate.

EXERCISES

Play the Monty Hall problem in the book 30 times with and 30 times without switching. Compare your relative frequencies with $\tfrac23$ and $\tfrac13$.

With switching you usually get something around $0.67$, without switching around $0.33$. With only 30 games it still fluctuates noticeably.

Name three areas in which computer simulations are used today, and for one of them describe what is simulated.

For example the weather (how air, heat and moisture move), medicine (how a disease spreads from person to person), a flight simulator (how an aircraft reacts to control inputs), traffic, climate, crash tests.

An album has 6 stickers. Simulate 10 full albums with a dice or in the book. Which mean do you get? Compare with the theoretical value, $6 \cdot \left(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac15 + \tfrac16\right) = 14.7$.

With 10 albums the mean is usually between 11 and 19. The more albums, the closer to 14.7.

At the stadium entrance each person is checked thoroughly with probability 10% (2 minutes), otherwise briefly (20 seconds). How long does a steward need per person on average? How could you use random digits to simulate how long the queue gets?

$0.1 \cdot 120 + 0.9 \cdot 20 = 12 + 18 = 30$ seconds. Simulation: digit 0 means thorough, 1 to 9 brief. In addition you decide at random how many people arrive per minute and keep track of the queue.

A variant with 4 doors and one car: after your choice the host opens a goat door. You may switch to one of the two other closed doors, chosen at random. Is it worth it? Simulate or calculate.

Without switching: $\tfrac14$. With switching: the first choice is wrong with $\tfrac34$, then the car is behind one of the two other doors, and you hit it with $\tfrac12$: $\tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac38$. Switching is worth it.

REVIEW · DEEPEN · CONNECT

A test has 10 yes-no questions, and someone just guesses. How do you use random digits to simulate how often at least 7 answers are right?

Even digit: right, odd: wrong. Every 10 digits are one test. Play through many tests and count how often there are at least 7 even digits.

When rolling a dice with random digits, 0, 7, 8 and 9 are skipped. On average, how many digits do you need for 100 rolls? Why?

Only 6 of 10 digits can be used, so you need about $\tfrac{100}{0.6} \approx 167$ digits.

Explain the $\tfrac23$ to someone who does not believe the Monty Hall problem, with 100 doors: you choose one, the host opens 98 goat doors. Do you switch?

Your door has the probability $\tfrac{1}{100}$. The one door that the host has left out of 99 has $\tfrac{99}{100}$. Switch!

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Monte Carlo: a casino gives its name

In the 1940s the mathematician Stanisław Ulam and his colleagues in Los Alamos in the USA were working on problems that nobody could calculate. Ulam had an idea while playing patience: instead of laboriously calculating the chance of winning a game of patience, you could simply play it very often and count, ideally on one of the new computing machines.

Because the method plays with chance, it was given the name of the famous casino of Monaco. In 1949 Nicholas Metropolis and Ulam published the article “The Monte Carlo Method”. Today Monte Carlo simulations are found in weather forecasts, in medicine and in the pictures of animated films.

The Monty Hall problem also became famous through simulations. When the columnist Marilyn vos Savant advised switching in an American magazine in 1990, she received thousands of indignant letters, many of them from people with doctorates. Only recalculating and acting it out convinced most of them.

Source: N. Metropolis, S. Ulam: The Monte Carlo Method. Journal of the American Statistical Association 44 (1949), pp. 335–341.
