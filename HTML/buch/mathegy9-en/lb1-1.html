Powers and roots

UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Raising to powers and taking roots

After $n$ folds the pile of paper is $0.1 \cdot 2^n$ mm thick. Because $2^{10} = 1024$ is roughly $1000$, it becomes about a thousand times thicker every ten folds: 10 cm after 10, 100 m after 20, 100 km after 30 folds. After 42 folds it is $0.1 \cdot 2^{42}$ mm, about 440,000 km. That is further than the moon.

When raising to a power you calculate the value of the power from the base and the exponent: $4^3 = 64$. When taking a root you go the other way: which number gives $64$ when cubed? That is the cube root $\sqrt[3]{64} = 4$.

Some roots do not work out exactly. $\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\ldots$ neither terminates nor recurs and therefore cannot be written as a fraction. Such numbers are called irrational. Together with the rational numbers they form the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$.

KEY POINT · POWERS AND ROOTS

$a^n = a \cdot a \cdot \ldots \cdot a$ with $n$ factors. In addition $a^1 = a$, $a^0 = 1$ and $a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$ for $a \neq 0$.

For $a \geq 0$, $\sqrt[n]{a}$ is the non-negative number whose $n$-th power is $a$: $\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^n = a$. Instead of $\sqrt[2]{a}$ you write $\sqrt{a}$.

The equation $x^n = a$ with even $n$ has the two solutions $\pm\sqrt[n]{a}$ for $a > 0$, only $x = 0$ for $a = 0$ and none for $a < 0$. With odd $n$ it has exactly one solution: $\sqrt[n]{a}$ for $a \geq 0$, $-\sqrt[n]{-a}$ for $a < 0$.

$\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$. $\mathbb{R}^+$ denotes the positive real numbers.

Watch the brackets: $-2^4 = -16$, but $(-2)^4 = 16$. An even power of a negative number is positive, an odd one negative.

Roots of negative numbers: $x^3 = -8$ has the solution $x = -2$. But we only use the root sign for $a \geq 0$ and write $x = -\sqrt[3]{8}$.

On the calculator: the key $\sqrt[x]{\phantom{a}}$ or $a$ to the power $1/n$. In the CAS the command is usually called root or nthroot.

2Laws of indices for rational exponents

The double cube needs an edge $a$ with $a^3 = 2$, so $a = \sqrt[3]{2} \approx 1.26$ dm. The edge grows by only a little more than a quarter. If you write $a = 2^{\frac13}$, this fits the laws of indices: $\left(2^{\frac13}\right)^3 = 2^{\frac13 \cdot 3} = 2^1 = 2$.

You know the laws of indices for natural exponents. Powers are extended in such a way that these laws still hold (principle of permanence). Each step to the right in the table divides by $2$. This gives $2^0 = 1$ and the negative exponents all by themselves.

KEY POINT · LAWS OF INDICES

For $a, b > 0$ and rational exponents $r, s$:

$a^r \cdot a^s = a^{r+s}$   ·   $a^r : a^s = a^{r-s}$   ·   $\left(a^r\right)^s = a^{r \cdot s}$

$a^r \cdot b^r = (a \cdot b)^r$   ·   $a^r : b^r = (a : b)^r$

Rational exponents: $a^{\frac{1}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a}$ and $a^{\frac{m}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m$ for $a > 0$, $n \geq 2$.

No law for sums: $2^3 + 2^4 = 24$, but $2^7 = 128$. Equal bases are only combined when multiplying and dividing.

Laws of roots are laws of indices: $\sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} = a^{\frac12} \cdot b^{\frac12} = (ab)^{\frac12} = \sqrt{ab}$, for example $\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{8} = \sqrt{16} = 4$.

Why $a > 0$? With a negative base the laws go wrong. Exercise 10 shows where.

Lisa calculates: $(-8)^{\frac13} = (-8)^{\frac26} = \sqrt[6]{(-8)^2} = \sqrt[6]{64} = 2$. But $2^3 = 8$ and not $-8$. Where is the mistake?

The laws of indices and the notation $a^{\frac{m}{n}}$ only hold for $a > 0$. When $\tfrac13$ is extended to $\tfrac26$, the negative base is silently squared and the sign is lost. The solution of $x^3 = -8$ is $x = -2$. That is why powers with fractional exponents are only defined for positive bases.

3Equations with roots

From $h = 5\,t^2$ you get the time of fall $t = \sqrt{\tfrac{h}{5}}$. If the stone hits the bottom after 3 seconds, then $\sqrt{\tfrac{h}{5}} = 3$. Squaring gives $\tfrac{h}{5} = 9$, so $h = 45$. The check $\sqrt{\tfrac{45}{5}} = \sqrt{9} = 3$ works: the well is about 45 m deep.

In an equation with roots the variable is under a root. You free it by squaring both sides. But squaring is not an equivalence transformation: from $a = b$ it follows that $a^2 = b^2$, but from $a^2 = b^2$ it only follows that $a = b$ or $a = -b$. That is why the squared equation can have solutions that the original one does not satisfy.

KEY POINT · SOLVING EQUATIONS WITH ROOTS
  1. Get the root on its own on one side.
  2. Square both sides; for a cube root, cube them.
  3. Solve the resulting equation.
  4. Check in the original equation. Values that do not satisfy it are called false solutions and do not belong to the solution set.

Quick check: a square root is never negative. $\sqrt{x} = -2$ has no solution; you can see that even before squaring.

Domain: there must be nothing negative under the root. With $\sqrt{x - 3}$ we need $x \geq 3$.

Careful with the CAS: for $(-8)^{\frac13}$ some calculators give an error message or a complex number, others $-2$. Find out what your calculator does, and check results by substituting.

INTERACTIVE · WHERE THE FALSE SOLUTION COMES FROM

The equation $\sqrt{x} = -2$ has no solution: the graph of $y = \sqrt{x}$ never goes below the $x$-axis. After squaring you get $x = 4$. This number solves the equation $-\sqrt{x} = -2$. Squaring turns $\sqrt{x}$ and $-\sqrt{x}$ into the same equation.

EXAMPLE 5 · WITH A CHECK

Solve $\sqrt{2x + 3} = 5$.

Squaring: $2x + 3 = 25$, so $x = 11$.

Check: $\sqrt{2 \cdot 11 + 3} = \sqrt{25} = 5$ ✓   $L = \{11\}$

EXAMPLE 6 · A FALSE SOLUTION

Solve $\sqrt{x - 1} + 4 = 1$.

Isolate the root: $\sqrt{x - 1} = -3$. Squaring: $x - 1 = 9$, so $x = 10$.

Check: $\sqrt{9} + 4 = 7 \neq 1$. $10$ is a false solution: $L = \{\,\}$

A stone takes 2.4 s to hit the bottom. How deep is the well ($h = 5t^2$)? How long does a stone take to fall into a well twice as deep?

$h = 5 \cdot 2.4^2 = 28.8$ m. Twice as deep: $t = \sqrt{\tfrac{57.6}{5}} = \sqrt{11.52} \approx 3.39$ s. Not twice as long, but $\sqrt{2}$ times as long.

CAS The period of a simple pendulum is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}}$ with $g = 9.81\,\tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$. How long must the pendulum be for $T = 2$ s?

$\sqrt{\tfrac{l}{g}} = \tfrac{1}{\pi}$, squaring: $l = \tfrac{g}{\pi^2} \approx 0.994$ m. So a “seconds pendulum” is almost exactly 1 m long.

Looking ahead: squaring turns $\sqrt{x + 2} = x$ into $x^2 - x - 2 = 0$. Check that $x = 2$ and $x = -1$ solve this equation. Which number also solves the equation with the root? (You learn how to solve such quadratic equations without guessing in chapter 1.4.)

$4 - 2 - 2 = 0$ and $1 + 1 - 2 = 0$ ✓. Check in the equation with the root: $\sqrt{4} = 2$ ✓, but $\sqrt{1} = 1 \neq -1$. Only $x = 2$ is a solution.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Roots like the Babylonians

How do you take a root without a calculator? You are looking for the side of a square with area $a$. You guess a side $x$. A rectangle with area $a$ and side $x$ has the other side $\tfrac{a}{x}$. If $x$ is too small, $\tfrac{a}{x}$ is too large, and vice versa. The mean $\tfrac12\left(x + \tfrac{a}{x}\right)$ is a better side for the square. If you repeat this, the number of correct digits doubles with every step.

For $\sqrt{2}$ and the start $x = 1$: $1.5$, then $1.41\overline{6}$, then $1.414215\ldots$ After only three steps five decimal places are correct.

A Babylonian clay tablet from around 1800 to 1600 BC shows a square with its diagonals and the value $1;24,51,10$ in the base-60 system, that is $1 + \tfrac{24}{60} + \tfrac{51}{60^2} + \tfrac{10}{60^3} = 1.41421296\ldots$ That is $\sqrt{2}$ to six digits. Heron of Alexandria describes the method in the 1st century and uses it to calculate $\sqrt{720} \approx 26\tfrac56$.

Sources: tablet YBC 7289, Yale Babylonian Collection. D. Fowler, E. Robson: Square Root Approximations in Old Babylonian Mathematics. YBC 7289 in Context. Historia Mathematica 25 (1998), pp. 366–378. Heron of Alexandria, Metrica I, 8.
