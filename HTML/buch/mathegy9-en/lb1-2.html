Power functions

The area of a square grows with $a^2$, the volume of a cube with $a^3$, the brightness of a lamp falls with $\tfrac{1}{r^2}$. Behind all of these are power functions. You get to know their graphs, read off symmetry, domain and range and find zeros, poles and asymptotes.

1Power functions with natural exponents

A square with side $a$ has the area $A(a) = a^2$, a cube the volume $V(a) = a^3$. If you double $a$, the area becomes four times and the volume eight times as large. Both are power functions, just with a different exponent.

A function $f(x) = x^n$ is called a power function. Whether $n$ is even or odd decides the shape of the graph: for even $n$, $(-x)^n = x^n$; for odd $n$, $(-x)^n = -x^n$.

KEY POINT · $f(x) = x^n$ WITH $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $n \geq 1$

Domain $D = \mathbb{R}$. All graphs pass through $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$.

$n$ even: graph symmetric about the $y$-axis, $f(-x) = f(x)$, range $W = \{\, y \mid y \geq 0 \,\}$, through $(-1, 1)$.

$n$ odd: graph symmetric about the origin, $f(-x) = -f(x)$, range $W = \mathbb{R}$, through $(-1, -1)$.

The larger $n$, the flatter the graph for $|x| < 1$ and the steeper for $|x| > 1$.

Names: the graph of $y = x^2$ is called the standard parabola, those of $y = x^n$ with $n \geq 2$ parabolas of order $n$.

Checking symmetry: substitute $-x$. If you get $f(x)$, the graph is symmetric about the $y$-axis; if you get $-f(x)$, it is symmetric about the origin.

INTERACTIVE · POWER FUNCTIONS

Choose an exponent. With “Show relatives” the graphs with the same kind of exponent appear as well. All these graphs have the white points in common.

EXAMPLE 1 · VALUES AND SYMMETRY

For $f(x) = x^4$, calculate the values $f(-2)$ and $f(0.5)$ and justify the symmetry.

$f(-2) = 16$, $f(0.5) = 0.0625 = \tfrac{1}{16}$.

$f(-x) = (-x)^4 = x^4 = f(x)$: symmetric about the $y$-axis.

EXAMPLE 2 · BACKWARDS

At which point does $f(x) = x^3$ take the value $-64$? And at which points does $g(x) = x^4$ take the value $81$?

$x^3 = -64$: $x = -4$. $x^4 = 81$: $x = \pm 3$.

With an even exponent every positive value belongs to two points, symmetric about the $y$-axis.

EXERCISES

Do the points lie on the graph of $f(x) = x^5$? $A(2, 32)$, $B(-1, 1)$, $C\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac{1}{32}\right)$, $D(-2, -32)$

$A$ yes, $B$ no ($(-1)^5 = -1$), $C$ yes, $D$ yes.

For $y = x^6$ and $y = x^7$, give the range, the symmetry and the points in common with $y = x^2$.

$x^6$: $y \geq 0$, symmetric about the $y$-axis, common points $(-1, 1)$, $(0, 0)$, $(1, 1)$. $x^7$: $\mathbb{R}$, symmetric about the origin, common points with $x^2$: $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$.

For which $x$ is $x^4 < x^2$? Justify with the graphs.

For $0 < |x| < 1$, that is $-1 < x < 1$ and $x \neq 0$. There $x^2 < 1$ and $x^4 = x^2 \cdot x^2 < x^2$.

The edge of a cube is tripled. How do the surface area and the volume change? And if the edge grows by only 10 %?

Surface area $\cdot 9$, volume $\cdot 27$. With $+10\,\%$: $1.1^2 = 1.21$, so $+21\,\%$ surface area; $1.1^3 = 1.331$, so $+33.1\,\%$ volume.

Show in general: for odd $n$, $(-x)^n = -x^n$. Deduce from this that the graph is symmetric about the origin.

$(-x)^n = (-1)^n x^n$, and $(-1)^n = -1$ for odd $n$. If $(x, y)$ lies on the graph, then so does $(-x, -y)$: that is the point reflection in the origin.

2Negative and rational exponents

The travel time $t = \tfrac{120}{v} = 120 \cdot v^{-1}$ halves if you go twice as fast. Its graph is a hyperbola: it comes closer and closer to the $t$-axis for small $v$ and to the $v$-axis for large $v$, without ever reaching them.

With the laws of indices from chapter 1.1, negative and fractional exponents can also be read as functions. With fractional exponents the function is only defined for $x \geq 0$, because the root is only defined for non-negative numbers.

If you add power functions with natural exponents, possibly with factors in front, you get a polynomial, for example $2x^3 - x + 4$. The largest exponent is called the degree. Polynomials are also called polynomial functions.

KEY POINT · NEGATIVE AND RATIONAL EXPONENTS

$f(x) = x^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{x^n}$ ($n \in \mathbb{N}$): $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$, the graph is a hyperbola through $(1, 1)$.

$n$ even: symmetric about the $y$-axis, $W = \{\, y \mid y > 0 \,\}$ · $n$ odd: symmetric about the origin, $W = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$.

$f(x) = x^{\frac{p}{q}} = \sqrt[q]{x^p}$: defined for $x \geq 0$ (for a negative exponent, for $x > 0$), no symmetry, through $(1, 1)$.

For $x \geq 0$, $y = x^{\frac12} = \sqrt{x}$ is the inverse of $y = x^2$: its graph is the right-hand branch of the parabola reflected in $y = x$.

Which is on top? For $0 < x < 1$ the power with the smaller exponent is larger: $0.25^{\frac12} = 0.5$, but $0.25^2 = 0.0625$. For $x > 1$ it is the other way round.

In the CAS you type $x^{(1/3)}$ with brackets. Without brackets the calculator works out $\tfrac{x^1}{3}$.

EXAMPLE 3 · A HYPERBOLA

For $f(x) = x^{-2}$, calculate the values $f(0.5)$, $f(-2)$ and $f(10)$. What do you notice?

$f(0.5) = \tfrac{1}{0.25} = 4$, $f(-2) = \tfrac14$, $f(10) = \tfrac{1}{100}$.

Near $0$ the values become very large, for large $|x|$ very small, and they are always positive.

EXAMPLE 4 · FRACTIONAL EXPONENTS

Calculate $f(4)$ for $f(x) = x^{\frac32}$ and $g(9)$ for $g(x) = x^{-\frac12}$.

$4^{\frac32} = \left(\sqrt{4}\right)^3 = 8$   ·   $9^{-\frac12} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{9}} = \dfrac{1}{3}$

$f(4) = 8$, $g(9) = \tfrac13$

3Zeros, poles and asymptotes

If you shift the hyperbola $y = \tfrac{1}{x^2}$ down by 4, you get $f(x) = \tfrac{1}{x^2} - 4$. At $x = 0$ the graph grows beyond all bounds; for large $|x|$ it hugs the line $y = -4$. And it cuts the $x$-axis where $\tfrac{1}{x^2} = 4$, that is at $x = \pm\tfrac12$.

KEY POINT · CHARACTERISTIC POINTS AND LINES

Zero: a point $x_0$ with $f(x_0) = 0$. There the graph cuts or touches the $x$-axis at the point $(x_0, 0)$. The intersection with the $y$-axis $(0, f(0))$ only exists if $0$ belongs to the domain.

Pole: a point $x_p$ that does not belong to the domain and near which the absolute values of the function grow beyond all bounds. The line $x = x_p$ is a vertical asymptote. If the graph changes sign there, it is called a pole with a change of sign.

Horizontal asymptote: a line $y = c$ that the graph approaches arbitrarily closely for $x \to +\infty$ or $x \to -\infty$.

Local extremum: a maximum (minimum) whose function value is larger (smaller) than all values near it.

Read it like this: $x \to +\infty$ means “$x$ tends to plus infinity”, that is: $x$ gets larger and larger.

With the CAS: zeros with zeros or solve, extrema on the graph with “Minimum” and “Maximum”. Always check on the graph as well whether the result fits.

FIGURE · $f(x) = \tfrac{1}{x^2} - 4$

Zeros at $\pm\tfrac12$, pole without a change of sign at $0$ (the vertical asymptote is the $y$-axis), horizontal asymptote $y = -4$.

EXAMPLE 5 · WITHOUT A POLE

Investigate $f(x) = x^3 - 8$ for zeros, intersection with the $y$-axis, poles and asymptotes.

$x^3 = 8 \Rightarrow x = 2$. $f(0) = -8$. $D = \mathbb{R}$, so no pole and no asymptote.

Zero $2$, intersection $(0, -8)$

EXAMPLE 6 · WITH A POLE

Investigate $f(x) = \tfrac{1}{x} - 2$.

$\tfrac1x = 2 \Rightarrow x = \tfrac12$. $0 \notin D$, no intersection with the $y$-axis.

Zero $\tfrac12$, pole at $0$ with a change of sign, asymptotes $x = 0$ and $y = -2$

EXCURSIONBIOLOGY AND HISTORY

Why there are no giants

A giant, ten times as tall as a human and built the same in every proportion: how strong would its bones have to be? Its weight grows with the volume, that is with $10^3 = 1000$. But the load a bone can carry depends on its cross-sectional area, and that only grows with $10^2 = 100$. Every square centimetre of bone would have to carry ten times as much. The giant would break its legs at the first step.

Galileo Galilei described this square-cube law in 1638: large animals need disproportionately thick bones. That is why an elephant’s leg looks quite different from a gazelle’s leg. And that is why ants can carry many times their own weight.

Sources: Galileo Galilei: Discorsi e dimostrazioni matematiche intorno a due nuove scienze, Leiden 1638, second day. J. B. S. Haldane: On Being the Right Size. Harper’s Magazine, March 1926.
