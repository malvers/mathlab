Parameters and quadratic functions

A graph can be stretched, compressed, shifted and reflected, and the equation of the function shows exactly how. You learn what parameters do to a graph and apply this to the most important function of this year: the quadratic function in standard form and vertex form.

UNIT 1WITH CAS · 4 LESSONS

1The effect of parameters

For $y = (x + 3)^2$ the graph has its vertex where the bracket becomes zero, that is at $x = -3$. What used to happen at $x = 0$ now already happens at $x = -3$: the graph has moved 3 to the left. So a plus in the brackets shifts to the left, a minus to the right.

Starting from a basic function $f$, each parameter acts in its own way. You can carry out the steps one after the other: first in the $x$-direction, then stretch or reflect, and finally shift in the $y$-direction.

KEY POINT · PARAMETERS

$y = a \cdot f(x)$: stretch in the $y$-direction by the factor $a$. $|a| > 1$ stretches, $0 < |a| < 1$ compresses, $a < 0$ also reflects in the $x$-axis.

$y = f(x) + e$: shift by $e$ in the $y$-direction (upwards for $e > 0$).

$y = f(x - d)$: shift by $d$ in the $x$-direction (to the right for $d > 0$). So $f(x + 3)$ shifts 3 to the left.

$y = f(b \cdot x)$: compression in the $x$-direction by the factor $\tfrac{1}{b}$. $y = f(-x)$ reflects in the $y$-axis.

What moves along: when shifting by $d$ to the right, zeros, poles, vertical asymptotes and the axis of symmetry move along too. Horizontal asymptotes shift with $e$.

What stays: stretching by $a$ leaves zeros and poles where they are.

EXAMPLE 2 · A SHIFTED HYPERBOLA

Investigate $h(x) = \dfrac{1}{x + 2} - 1$.

2Standard form and vertex form

Does the template fit $y = x^2 - 4x + 3$? By the binomial formula, $x^2 - 4x + 4 = (x - 2)^2$. So $x^2 - 4x + 3 = (x - 2)^2 - 1$: the tip of the template goes to $(2, -1)$. This trick is called completing the square.

Every quadratic function can be written in two forms. The standard form shows the intersection with the $y$-axis, the vertex form shows the vertex. Its graph is a parabola, symmetric about the vertical line through the vertex.

KEY POINT · QUADRATIC FUNCTIONS

Vertex form: $f(x) = a(x - d)^2 + e$ with the vertex $S(d, e)$.

Standard form: $f(x) = x^2 + px + q$ · general form: $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a \neq 0$.

Completing the square: $x^2 + px + q = \left(x + \tfrac{p}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\tfrac{p}{2}\right)^2 + q$, so $S\!\left(-\tfrac{p}{2},\; q - \tfrac{p^2}{4}\right)$.

With $ax^2 + bx + c$, first take $a$ out of the first two terms.

Recipe: halve the number in front of the $x$, square it, add it and subtract it again straight away: $x^2 + 6x = (x + 3)^2 - 9$.

Drawing: mark the vertex, then from the vertex go 1 to the right and $a$ up, 2 to the right and $4a$ up, and the same to the left.

INTERACTIVE · THE VERTEX FORM

Drag the vertex or use the sliders. Below the graph the same function is shown in general form.

EXAMPLE 3 · STANDARD FORM

Find the vertex of $f(x) = x^2 - 6x + 5$.

$x^2 - 6x + 9 - 9 + 5 = (x - 3)^2 - 4$.

$S(3, -4)$, opening upwards, the shape of the standard parabola.

EXAMPLE 4 · WITH A FACTOR

Find the vertex of $f(x) = 2x^2 + 8x + 3$.

$2(x^2 + 4x) + 3 = 2\left[(x + 2)^2 - 4\right] + 3 = 2(x + 2)^2 - 5$.

$S(-2, -5)$, opening upwards, narrower than the standard parabola.

A ball flies along the path $h(x) = -0.05x^2 + x + 1.5$ ($x$ horizontal distance, $h$ height, both in m). How high does it fly at most?

$-0.05(x^2 - 20x) + 1.5 = -0.05(x - 10)^2 + 6.5$: 6.5 m after 10 m of flight.

Show by completing the square: the vertex of $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ is at $S\!\left(-\tfrac{b}{2a},\; c - \tfrac{b^2}{4a}\right)$.

$a\left(x^2 + \tfrac{b}{a}x\right) + c = a\left[\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \tfrac{b^2}{4a^2}\right] + c = a\left(x + \tfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + c - \tfrac{b^2}{4a}$.

EXCURSIONHISTORY AND ARCHITECTURE

Parabola or catenary?

Hang a chain from two nails. It looks like a parabola opening upwards, and in 1638 Galileo Galilei described it as approximately parabolic. Joachim Jungius showed that it is not a parabola; his result appeared in 1669, after his death. The correct curve, the catenary, was found independently in 1691 by Johann Bernoulli, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz and Christiaan Huygens.

If you turn a catenary upside down, you get an arch that carries only itself and does not bulge out sideways anywhere. The Gateway Arch in St. Louis is built like this: 192 m high and 192 m wide at the ground. A parabola through the same three points would be $y = 192 - \tfrac{x^2}{48}$. Near the top the two curves are very similar; towards the feet the catenary becomes steeper.

Sources: Galileo Galilei, Discorsi, Leiden 1638. Joachim Jungius, Geometria empirica, Hamburg 1669. Johann Bernoulli, G. W. Leibniz, C. Huygens in the Acta Eruditorum, June 1691. Dimensions of the Gateway Arch: U.S. National Park Service.
