UNIT 14 LESSONS

1Mode, median and arithmetic mean

Nine journeys to school in minutes: 8, 12, 15, 15, 15, 18, 20, 25, 30. The most frequent value is 15, the middle one is 15, the average is $\tfrac{158}{9} \approx 17.6$. If a tenth person with 75 minutes is added, the average jumps to 23.3. The middle value hardly changes: 16.5.

Which average makes sense depends on the kind of data. On a nominal scale (favourite subject, eye colour) you can only count. On an ordinal scale (placing, “very good” to “poor”) you can put things in order. On a metric scale (time, length, money) you can also calculate.

KEY POINT · THREE AVERAGES

Mode: the most frequent value. For every scale, nominal too.

Median (central value): the value in the middle of the ordered list; for an even number of values, the mean of the two middle values. From the ordinal scale upwards.

Arithmetic mean: $\bar{x} = \dfrac{x_1 + x_2 + \ldots + x_n}{n}$. Only for metric data.

Outliers shift the arithmetic mean a lot, the median hardly at all.

Frequency table: if the value $x_i$ occurs exactly $h_i$ times, then $\bar{x} = \dfrac{h_1 x_1 + h_2 x_2 + \ldots}{n}$.

School marks are strictly speaking ordinal: the gap from 1 to 2 is not the same as from 4 to 5. The average mark is still common, but should be read with care.

2Range, variance and standard deviation

Both cities have a mean of 11.25 °C. In the city by the sea the monthly values only vary between 6 °C and 17 °C, inland between −1 °C and 23 °C. A mean on its own does not describe data. You also need to know how far they spread around it.

The simplest measure is the range, the distance between the largest and the smallest value. But it depends on only two values. It is more precise to look at all the distances from the mean. Because positive and negative distances would cancel out, you square them and take the mean of the squares.

KEY POINT · MEASURES OF SPREAD

Range: $R = x_{\max} - x_{\min}$

Variance: $s^2 = \dfrac{(x_1 - \bar{x})^2 + (x_2 - \bar{x})^2 + \ldots + (x_n - \bar{x})^2}{n}$, the mean squared deviation from the mean

Standard deviation: $s = \sqrt{s^2}$. It has the same unit as the data.

Spreadsheet: =AVERAGE(A1:A12), =VAR.P(A1:A12) and =STDEV.P(A1:A12) divide by $n$ as here. =STDEV.S divides by $n - 1$ and gives slightly larger values.

Units: temperature differences are given in kelvin (K). 1 K is the same difference as 1 °C.

A list has $\bar{x} = 20$ and $s = 4$. What changes if you a) add 5 to every value, b) double every value?

a) $\bar{x} = 25$, $s = 4$ stays: all the distances stay the same.   b) $\bar{x} = 40$, $s = 8$: all the distances double, and the variance quadruples.

Why do you square the deviations instead of simply adding them? Show with 4, 7, 7, 8, 9 that the sum of the deviations $x_i - \bar{x}$ is zero, and explain why this is always true.

$-3 + 0 + 0 + 1 + 2 = 0$. In general: $\sum (x_i - \bar{x}) = \sum x_i - n\bar{x} = n\bar{x} - n\bar{x} = 0$. The squares are never negative, so they do not cancel out.

3Histograms and statistical manipulation

With 40 body heights almost every value occurs only once. A bar chart with 40 bars shows nothing. If you group the values into classes, for instance from 150 to under 155 cm, from 155 to under 160 cm and so on, the shape of the distribution becomes visible: many in the middle, few at the edges.

A histogram draws a bar above each class. The bars touch, because the classes follow each other without gaps. The choice of classes changes the picture. And some charts change the picture on purpose.

KEY POINT · HISTOGRAMS AND TYPICAL TRICKS

Choose classes without gaps and without overlaps, usually of equal width: $[150, 155)$ means 150 to under 155. The relative frequency is the proportion of all the values.

Truncated axis: if the axis does not start at zero, small differences look huge.

Picture instead of bar: if a picture grows in height and width, its area grows with the square.

Wrong reference value: from 4 % to 6 % is 2 percentage points, but an increase of 50 %. Absolute numbers without a reference (“twice as many cases”) say little.

Trends and causes: two points are not yet a trend, and things that rise together do not cause each other.

Rule of thumb: with $n$ values, about $\sqrt{n}$ classes are a good start, so 6 to 7 for 40 values.

Storks and babies: in European countries the number of pairs of storks and the number of births are related. Both grow with the area of the country. Source: R. Matthews, Storks Deliver Babies (p = 0.008), Teaching Statistics 22 (2000), pp. 36–38.

EXAMPLE 6 · PER CENT OR PERCENTAGE POINTS?

In a poll a party rises from 4 % to 6 %. One newspaper writes “+2 %”, the other “+50 %”. Which is right?

The share rises by 2 percentage points. Relative to the old 4 %, that is growth of $\tfrac{2}{4} =$ 50 %.

“+2 %” is wrong; “+50 %” is right, but misleading without the starting value.

EXCURSIONHISTORY

Florence Nightingale’s roses

In the Crimean War of 1854 to 1856 the British nurse Florence Nightingale ran the nursing in a military hospital near Constantinople. She counted carefully: far more soldiers died of preventable diseases such as cholera and typhus than of their wounds.

To convince politicians who did not read tables, she invented a special kind of chart: each month is a sector, and its area shows the number of deaths, coloured separately by cause. You could see at once how huge the share of disease was, and how it shrank after hygiene improved. Her diagrams helped to reform the army’s health service. In 1858 she became the first woman member of the Statistical Society of London, today’s Royal Statistical Society.

A statistic can deceive, but it can also save lives.

Source: F. Nightingale: Notes on Matters Affecting the Health, Efficiency, and Hospital Administration of the British Army. London 1858.
